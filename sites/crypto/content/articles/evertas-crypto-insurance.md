## How crypto insurance fits into digital-asset risk management

At a high level, insurance is a possible way to transfer specified financial risks under agreed terms. It is not the same as preventing an incident, restoring access to assets, or guaranteeing reimbursement. Whether a particular event qualifies as a covered loss depends on the applicable policy language and the facts involved. No universal conclusion about coverage follows from describing a risk as “crypto-related.”

Insurance is also distinct from controls designed to manage operational and technical risks. Key management, for example, concerns protecting cryptographic keying material throughout its lifecycle, including generation, storage, distribution, use, recovery, revocation, and destruction. These are control activities an organization can assess separately from whether it has insurance. The existence of a policy does not, by itself, show that key-management controls are adequate or that a loss connected to keys would be covered.

Technical systems have their own boundaries. Ethereum smart contracts, for example, are constrained by gas for deployment and execution and cannot retrieve offchain facts directly without an oracle mechanism. This describes technical conditions, not insurance coverage. It would be unsupported to conclude from those conditions that a policy covers a contract failure, an oracle issue, or a loss associated with either one.

This separation helps prevent a common misunderstanding: a risk-management measure is not proof that every risk has been transferred. Organizations may need to consider operational controls, technical design, and policy terms as distinct parts of their overall approach. A policy’s scope depends on the words and conditions that apply to it; technical controls address different aspects of how an operation is run. Without the applicable policy documents, readers should not assume that an operational, technical, or financial loss falls within coverage.

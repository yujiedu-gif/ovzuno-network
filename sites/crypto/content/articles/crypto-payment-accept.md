Before accepting cryptocurrency payments, decide what customers may pay with, who controls the payment assets, how the business will handle exceptions and records, and which jurisdictions govern the transaction. Treat legal, tax, security, and service questions as unresolved until they receive current, qualified review. This is a readiness framework, not a determination that a particular business or payment model is permitted.

## Define what accepting crypto means for the business

“Accepting crypto” can describe different arrangements. A business might receive a cryptoasset and retain it, or use an intermediary or process that converts proceeds into another currency. Those choices affect who handles the assets and which questions the business must resolve. The distinction alone does not establish the applicable legal, tax, or accounting treatment.

Write down the proposed scope before assessing tools or services. Identify intended customer groups, sales channels, transaction types, and locations relevant to the business and its customers. State whether the business is considering direct receipt, an intermediary, or both. If the asset, conversion approach, or payment route is undecided, mark it as undecided rather than treating an assumption as an approved operating choice.

Map the roles in the proposed flow: who presents the payment request, who controls the destination address or keys, who monitors incoming activity, and who can access any account or service used to process funds. A digital signature can authorize a Bitcoin transaction through signed ownership records, but a valid signature does not by itself establish which human, organization, device, or service controls the key. Technical evidence of key use and business accountability are separate questions. [source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>)

Keep the business purpose specific. Is the proposal meant to serve a defined customer group, accommodate a particular sales channel, or test an operational process? Record what would count as a successful review and which open decisions require approval. This is not a prediction about customer demand or business results; it is a way to prevent a vague goal from becoming a substitute for an assessed payment flow.

Distinguish the asset from the service arrangement. The asset and network determine the kind of transaction under consideration, while a wallet, payment service, or intermediary may add separate control, processing, and recordkeeping arrangements. Do not assume that a familiar asset symbol establishes which network, address format, or service route is intended. Write the exact proposed combination into the scope document, and flag any uncertainty for verification with the relevant recipient or service.

## Choose the payment and settlement model to evaluate

Compare models by their control points, not by broad labels such as “crypto payments.” Determine whether the business would control the receiving keys, rely on a third party for custody or processing, or use a route that converts or settles proceeds through an intermediary. For each option, identify who can authorize movement of funds, what records each party supplies, and which party is responsible for access recovery. Do not infer a provider’s custody, security, or recovery arrangements from a general product description; request current documentation for the specific service and account configuration under consideration.

At the policy level, decide how the business would treat pending, incomplete, failed, underpaid, mistaken, or disputed payments. Specify who reviews exceptions and what evidence is required before staff mark an order as paid or take another action. A transaction being visible or submitted is not, by itself, a complete business policy for fulfillment. The appropriate acceptance threshold and exception process depend on the proposed network, asset, transaction, and business risk; verify relevant service terms and network behavior rather than assuming one universal confirmation rule.

Refunds also need a policy before launch. Decide who may approve them, how the original customer request will be matched to a refund, what records will be retained, and how customer support will explain cases where the business cannot establish the right recipient from available information. Do not assume a refund follows the same path as a card reversal or that a particular network or service guarantees reversibility. The evidence for this guide does not establish a universal refund mechanism or transaction-reversal rule.

Define reconciliation at the business level: which records will be compared, who reviews discrepancies, and how exceptions are documented. Specify how the business will distinguish its own accounting and order records from records supplied by an intermediary or service. Keep these as policy and operating questions here; checkout code, callbacks, and implementation-specific reconciliation belong to a later technical plan.

A decision log helps prevent provisional ideas from becoming operational commitments. For each candidate flow, record the assumptions about asset, network, custody, conversion, and settlement; identify who must verify each assumption; and note what remains undecided. If two options differ in who controls keys or who handles conversion, assess those differences explicitly rather than treating them as interchangeable ways to accept payment.

## Map the operational controls before launch

Assign named roles for routine monitoring, exception review, recordkeeping, customer support, and escalation. If one person or team performs several roles, document that explicitly and define who can provide review or approval when an unusual case arises. A responsibility map helps distinguish the business’s tasks from functions performed by a payment service, wallet, or other intermediary.

Before selection, request evidence about the proposed arrangement’s security controls, custody model, account-access protections, recovery procedures, incident response, and division of responsibilities. These are questions to verify, not qualities to presume. Cryptographic systems use distinct tools—including digital signatures, hash functions, key management, message authentication, and encryption—and the presence of one cryptographic mechanism does not establish the security of the entire payment operation. [source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/Projects/cryptographic-standards-and-guidelines>)

Create an approval and escalation path for mistaken payments, suspected fraud, account-access problems, and refund requests. Define which facts staff must record and who can authorize a response. A reported payment should not be treated as proof of a customer’s identity or intent without an appropriate business process: cryptographic authorization concerns control of a key, not the identity of the person or service using it. [source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>)

Include fraud scenarios in the review. For example, U.S. FinCEN describes scams in which victims are directed under false pretenses to insert cash at a convertible virtual currency kiosk and send virtual currency to a destination controlled by a scammer. This is a reason to consider who initiated a payment and who controls its destination; it does not mean a legitimate kiosk transaction is itself evidence of fraud. [source](<https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2025-08/FinCEN-Notice-CVCKIOSK.pdf>)

Document how the business will preserve and review records. Identify which staff roles can access the records, who reviews exceptions, and how a discrepancy is escalated. Ask each proposed service what records it provides and what the business must maintain separately. Do not treat a service record as a complete account of the business’s responsibilities without confirming the scope of that record and the arrangement.

Plan for access problems before they arise. The business should identify who is authorized to recover access, what evidence or approvals the recovery process requires, and how a suspected compromise is escalated. Request documentation describing the proposed service’s recovery and incident procedures; if the arrangement leaves a material responsibility unclear, list that uncertainty as a launch blocker rather than relying on an informal understanding.

## Check legal, tax, and compliance obligations by jurisdiction

List the jurisdictions relevant to the business, its customers, and each proposed part of the payment flow. The governing review may depend on where the business operates, whom it serves, what role it performs, and which service or intermediary is involved. This article cannot determine whether accepting a particular asset is legal, whether a license is required, or which consumer-protection, sanctions, or other compliance duties apply. No jurisdiction-specific conclusion follows from general Bitcoin guidance.

Ask qualified professionals to review current requirements for each relevant jurisdiction and the specific business model. Provide them with the proposed flow, customer scope, asset choices, custody and conversion arrangements, and service terms. Ask them to identify which activities trigger obligations, who is responsible for each control, and what records or customer disclosures are required. Do not treat a general product statement or a requirement in one jurisdiction as permission or a rule for another.

Obtain separate tax review. Ask a qualified tax professional how receipts, any conversion, recordkeeping, and reporting should be handled for the business’s relevant locations and circumstances. The evidence available for this guide does not support jurisdiction-specific tax conclusions or a universal accounting treatment.

For any proposed service, verify current availability and permitted use for the business’s locations, customer scope, assets, and transaction model. Confirm fees, settlement terms, custody arrangements, security controls, access recovery, and operational limitations from current official documentation and applicable service terms. A feature described generally should not be taken as proof that it is available to this business or permitted in every location it serves.

Keep the review tied to the actual flow. A change from direct receipt to intermediary processing, or a change in the business’s customer or jurisdiction scope, may raise different questions for reviewers. Record what changed and return the updated flow to the appropriate qualified professional rather than carrying forward an earlier conclusion as if it covered a new arrangement.

## Build a go/no-go checklist for acceptance

Before approving a live payment flow, require documented answers to the following:

- **Purpose and scope:** What business need is being assessed, which customers and transactions are in scope, and which decisions remain open?
- **Payment route:** Will the business receive and hold crypto, use conversion, or rely on an intermediary? Who controls keys, payment processing, and access recovery?
- **Operating roles:** Who monitors payments, reviews exceptions, supports customers, maintains records, and escalates incidents?
- **Customer and fraud handling:** What approval path applies to refunds, mistaken payments, disputes, and suspected fraud? What evidence supports decisions?
- **Service evidence:** Are current terms and documentation available for fees, settlement, availability, custody, security, recovery, and limitations in each relevant location?
- **Qualified review:** Have legal, tax, and compliance questions been assessed for the actual business model and jurisdictions, rather than inferred from a general guide?
- **Open risks:** Which concerns remain unresolved, who owns them, and what evidence or review is required before approval?

For each checklist item, retain the supporting documentation or note the reviewer responsible for obtaining it. Assign an owner and due point for unresolved questions, and record whether the decision is approved, deferred, or blocked. This separates facts verified for the proposed arrangement from assumptions that still need investigation.

Proceed to an implementation plan only after accountable owners accept the defined scope and unresolved questions have a documented disposition. If key custody, service availability, jurisdiction-specific obligations, or exception handling cannot be established, record that as a readiness gap—not as an assumption that the issue will be settled after launch. This framework ends at business readiness; detailed website integration and provider selection require separate evaluation.

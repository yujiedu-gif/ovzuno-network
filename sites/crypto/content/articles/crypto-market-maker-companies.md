Crypto market maker companies may be one part of a broader exchange environment, but the evidence available here does not establish a universal definition of their services or engagement models. Organizations evaluating a provider should verify its actual role, contract, incentives, and controls rather than infer them from a label or assume a particular liquidity outcome.

## What crypto market maker companies do—and what they do not guarantee

Start by separating trading activity from the other functions around it. A crypto exchange environment can be mapped conceptually as participants and access interfaces connected to trading systems, alongside custody, settlement, data, and external-service relationships. This is a functional map, not a universal technical design; the arrangement varies by provider. ([source](<https://www.iosco.org/news/pdf/IOSCONEWS712.pdf>))

Exchange-facing access can connect participants with a trading platform through direct interaction or through cryptoasset service providers. That access relationship does not, by itself, show who provides trading services, holds assets, or handles settlement. ([source](<https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/office-hours-gary-gensler-what-are-crypto-trading-platforms>))

Related functions may sit within one firm or be distributed among different firms and services. Trading and custody, for example, can be combined or separated; the presence of one function does not establish that the same organization performs another. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/fsi-summary-financial-stability-implications-multifunction-cryptoasset-intermediaries-executive-summary>)) Some cryptoasset service providers may combine trading, custody, lending, market-making, and other services, but this possibility should not be treated as a description of every provider. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/fsi-paper-27-cryptoasset-service-providers-financial-intermediaries-risks-and-policy-approaches>))

For procurement, ask the prospective firm to define its role in writing: what it will do, where it will interact, and which responsibilities remain with the client, venue, or another provider. Do not treat the phrase “market maker” as proof of a particular service, trading method, or outcome. The allowed evidence does not establish guaranteed spreads, depth, volume, or price results.

Liquidity is multidimensional. It reflects the time and expense of buying or selling, including spread, transaction cost, price impact, and immediacy; those dimensions can change with transaction size and market conditions. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/speeches/20160502-market-and-funding-liquidity-overview>)) A single headline figure or broad promise is therefore not enough to assess what a proposed engagement delivers. Require the provider to define each measure and explain its limits.

## Service models and engagement structures

Do not assume that labels such as principal, agency, or hybrid describe a provider’s actual arrangement. The evidence available for this article does not define those models or establish which one a particular company uses. Ask the provider to explain its operating model in plain language and identify the activities it performs itself versus those performed by other entities.

Apply the same scrutiny to contract scope. Request a written description of covered assets, venues, trading periods, reporting, and any service-level commitments. These are questions for the provider’s documentation, not facts that can be inferred from its company category. Ask who controls accounts and assets, who can submit or modify orders, and who is responsible for resolving operational issues. Check the answers against contract terms and the actual arrangement.

Incentives need a separate review. Ask how the provider is compensated, whether it holds or receives assets or funds, and whether the engagement creates other commercial relationships. The evidence here supports only the general point that some service providers combine multiple activities; it does not establish particular compensation structures, inventory arrangements, or advances. Treat each as a contract-specific question. Request written disclosure rather than assuming that a provider has—or lacks—a conflict.

## Evidence to request before evaluating a provider

Build the review around verifiable statements, not promotional language. Request, where applicable:

- A written scope naming the entities, functions, assets, and venues involved, as well as activities expressly outside the engagement.
- A fee schedule and contract terms explaining compensation, responsibilities, and relevant asset or funding arrangements.
- Sample reports with definitions for each metric, the measurement period, and the calculation method.
- Written explanations of how quoted spreads, depth, uptime, or venue coverage are measured, including conditions and limitations.
- Supporting records for material claims, plus references or other suitable evidence that can be checked independently.

The available evidence does not establish provider-specific performance, fees, venue coverage, or service commitments. Do not fill those gaps with industry-wide assumptions. Ask what records substantiate a claim, who produced them, and whether they cover the entities, venues, and period relevant to the proposed engagement.

When comparing proposals, make sure the scopes and metrics are comparable. A provider’s service description may use a different scope or measurement from another provider’s. Because liquidity has several dimensions, request separate definitions for relevant measures instead of treating one number as a complete account of trading conditions. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/speeches/20160502-market-and-funding-liquidity-overview>)) This keeps the evaluation focused on documented scope and evidence rather than a promise of a particular outcome.

## Conflicts, controls, and operational risks

Ask what other services the provider or related entities perform. Some providers may combine trading, custody, lending, market-making, and other services, but the existence and details of any combination require provider-specific verification. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/fsi-paper-27-cryptoasset-service-providers-financial-intermediaries-risks-and-policy-approaches>)) The purpose is to understand functional boundaries and possible dependencies, not to presume misconduct or conclude that a combination is necessarily unsuitable.

Request written disclosures and procedures for conflicts, order handling, information access, and escalation. The evidence available here does not establish particular controls, security standards, custody protections, recovery procedures, or incident practices. Treat claims on these topics as unverified until you receive current documentation that identifies the responsible entity and the scope of the documentation.

Map responsibilities across the engagement. Ask which party handles trading-system access, asset custody, settlement, reporting, and external-service relationships, where applicable. A conceptual exchange map includes these as distinct relationships that can vary by arrangement; it does not prove how any individual provider is organized. ([source](<https://www.iosco.org/news/pdf/IOSCONEWS712.pdf>)) Confirm the map against the agreement and supporting operational documentation, and record responsibilities that remain with the client or another provider.

## Jurisdiction, contract, and ongoing review

The general evidence used here cannot determine legal treatment, tax treatment, provider availability, fees, or compliance obligations. Confirm questions that depend on the relevant entities, assets, venues, and jurisdictions using current, jurisdiction-specific primary evidence and qualified professionals where appropriate. Avoid relying on general marketing statements as proof of legal status or compliance.

Before signing, check that the contract identifies the parties, scope, responsibilities, compensation, reporting, and processes for addressing changes or ending the engagement. The evidence here does not establish standard contract terms, notice periods, or service levels, so verify each detail in the actual documents.

After onboarding, set review points to check whether the provider’s activities still match the agreed scope and whether its reports continue to support material claims. Reassess when entities, venues, services, or contractual arrangements change. Keep a record of what was verified, which documents support it, and what remains unresolved. This does not guarantee a market outcome; it provides a grounded way to assess the provider’s stated role and continuing responsibilities.

“Nasdaq crypto” is an ambiguous phrase, not enough by itself to identify a specific Nasdaq product or service. It may refer broadly to digital-asset-related activity or infrastructure associated with the company, but that wording does not establish that Nasdaq operates a crypto exchange, holds customer crypto, or offers any particular service. To distinguish infrastructure from exchange operation, identify the function and the responsible organization in current, service-specific documentation.

## What “Nasdaq crypto” can mean

The phrase could be used in discussion of digital-asset initiatives, market technology, or a named service. Those meanings are not interchangeable. A company’s association with market infrastructure does not, on its own, prove that it offers a service directly to users or performs a particular role in a transaction.

Start by asking what the specific statement is claiming. Does it name a technology service, an operating venue, a custody arrangement, a brokerage function, or an asset issuer? These are distinct roles. A general company description or headline may not specify which one applies, and the phrase “Nasdaq crypto” alone cannot resolve the ambiguity.

This overview does not confirm any current Nasdaq digital-asset offering. Before treating a named initiative or service as active, consult current official materials that identify its scope, operator, intended users, and status. If those materials do not establish a role, do not infer it from the company name or from a general reference to digital assets.

## Nasdaq crypto and the difference between infrastructure and an exchange

Market infrastructure is a broad category for systems and services used by markets and their participants. Providing technology that supports market activity is conceptually different from operating the trading venue itself. It is also different from taking custody of customer assets, acting as a broker, or issuing an asset. Each function needs evidence tied to the specific arrangement.

A centralized crypto exchange offers a useful point of comparison, but it is not a template for every market service. In a common centralized-exchange model, a venue records customer orders and executed trades in an internal order book and account system. Customers typically transfer assets into the exchange’s custody before using its internal trading system. These are features of the model described by the evidence; they do not show that Nasdaq performs either function. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>)

The distinction is practical: a technology provider might support another organization’s operations without itself matching buyers and sellers or holding customer assets. Conversely, a business that does operate a venue may have additional functions. Do not combine these possibilities into a conclusion about Nasdaq unless current documentation names the service and assigns the role.

Custody also requires careful wording. A crypto wallet generally stores private keys or passcodes used to access and authorize crypto assets rather than storing the assets themselves. A third-party custodian may manage access to private keys for customers, but that is a separate relationship from merely supplying market technology. The precise custody arrangement and customer rights require service-specific evidence. [source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>) [source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>)

## Where digital-asset services fit in market operations

When assessing a service description, map each activity to the organization responsible for it. Useful categories include technology provision, trading-venue operation, custody, brokerage, and asset issuance. A firm may combine functions, but evidence for one function should not be treated as proof of another. Nor should an infrastructure description be taken as evidence that a company lists, trades, or holds a particular crypto asset.

For example, documentation that identifies a technology provider should explain what the technology supports and which entity operates the relevant market or service. Separate documentation would be needed to establish whether a provider receives or controls customer assets, handles orders, or issues an asset. If the documentation does not identify the responsible party or explain the activity, treat that role as unverified.

Digital-asset arrangements can also involve multiple records and providers. A stablecoin represented on different blockchains may depend on different issuers, contracts, custody arrangements, or bridge mechanisms. A bridge coordinates activity between otherwise separate blockchain environments, but its presence alone does not identify the issuer, reserve holder, custodian, or trading venue. [source](<https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2023/NIST.IR.8408.pdf>)

The same boundary applies when several services appear under one business name. A group may have separate entities, systems, or contractual arrangements; a broad brand reference does not establish which one is responsible for a specific function. The relevant question is not just whether a name appears in a digital-asset context, but what the named service does and which party is accountable for it.

## What to verify before relying on a service description

Use current official documentation and disclosures for the specific service, not a general company profile or an older description. Check the following points separately:

- **Service and operator:** What does the service do, and which legal or operating entity provides it? Does the evidence establish technology provision, venue operation, custody, brokerage, or issuance?
- **Availability and scope:** Is the service currently offered, and to whom? Confirm any stated asset, market, or use-case boundaries instead of assuming they apply broadly.
- **Fees and terms:** Look for terms and fees that apply to the named service and user type. A general infrastructure description does not establish pricing or contractual conditions.
- **Custody and recovery:** Identify who controls relevant assets or keys, and review the service-specific terms and technical materials for custody and recovery details. A statement about one security control does not, by itself, establish the entire custody arrangement or a recovery outcome.
- **Jurisdiction and compliance:** Check which jurisdiction, entity, activity, and rule a regulatory or compliance statement addresses. The Financial Stability Board’s recommendations for global stablecoin arrangements call for transparent information about governance, stabilization, reserves and custody, legal claims, redemption rights, operations, risk management, and financial condition. Those recommendations concern the specified type of arrangement; they do not prove that a particular provider complies or establish requirements for every digital-asset service. [source](<https://www.fsb.org/uploads/P170723-3.pdf>)

These checks should remain separate. Evidence that a service exists does not automatically establish its fees, availability to a particular user, custody model, security controls, or compliance status. If current primary materials do not answer a question, leave it unresolved rather than filling the gap with a broader claim.

A crypto air drop is a distribution of tokens to selected wallet addresses or eligible participants. Some distributions require no action from recipients; others use a claim process. The campaign’s own rules determine who qualifies and what recipients must do, so the term “airdrop” alone does not verify a token, link, or offer.

## What a crypto air drop means

An airdrop describes a way tokens are distributed. It does not establish what a token is worth, whether it can be sold, or whether it will be useful. A token appearing in an account is not, by itself, evidence of market value or a particular ownership arrangement.

Projects may send tokens to selected addresses, or they may invite eligible participants to claim them. In the first case, the project initiates the distribution; in the second, the recipient follows a specified process. These are broad descriptions, not universal procedures. A campaign may also set conditions that change who receives tokens or how distribution is handled.

It helps to distinguish the token record from the wallet interface. A wallet generally stores private keys or passcodes used to access and authorize crypto assets rather than storing the assets themselves. An interface displaying a token does not, on its own, explain the project’s rules, the token’s value, or what rights are associated with it. ([source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>))

Token standards are another separate matter. For example, ERC-20 defines a common interface that includes balances, transfers, approvals, and related events. That interface does not establish backing, issuance policy, market value, compliance, or implementation safety. Those questions require evidence about the specific token and its arrangements. ([source](<https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-20>))

## How token distribution and eligibility work

A project may set eligibility using criteria such as prior activity, registration, or participation. The campaign’s rules determine which criteria apply; there is no single eligibility checklist that can be assumed for every distribution. A project may also use recorded wallet or activity data when deciding which addresses qualify, but the details must be documented by that project.

Some campaigns refer to a snapshot: a record of specified data at a particular point used in determining eligibility or allocation. Whether a campaign uses a snapshot, what information it records, and how that information affects an allocation are project-specific matters. Do not infer a snapshot date or eligibility result from a social post, an unsolicited message, or another campaign’s process.

Read the project’s published rules for the eligibility criteria and allocation method. Look for a clear explanation of which wallets qualify, how allocations are determined, whether exclusions apply, and whether there is a deadline. Confirm that the published terms match the campaign information you are reviewing. If the documentation does not answer an important question, treat that as unresolved rather than assuming another project’s rules apply.

A wallet address also does not automatically identify a person. A signature can support attribution to control of a key for a particular transaction, but it does not by itself identify the human, organization, device, or service controlling that key. This matters when a campaign describes eligibility by wallet activity: an address’s recorded activity is not automatically proof of who performed it.

## What recipients may need to do to claim

Some distributions may arrive without the recipient initiating a claim. Others may direct eligible wallets to a claim page or contract interaction. The exact steps and requested actions depend on the project. A familiar-looking page, a token displayed in a wallet, or a message claiming that an allocation is waiting does not establish that the claim route is authentic.

Start with project documentation and reach it through channels whose identity you have checked independently. Verify that any claim destination and contract address match information the project publishes through those channels. General information cannot authenticate a particular link or contract address; project-specific verification is still needed.

Before signing, review the action presented by the wallet. A transaction’s authorization can commit the sender to fields such as a destination, value, optional input data, nonce, and fee limits. Wallet interfaces may summarize or decode those fields differently, so do not rely only on a page’s label for the action. If you cannot determine what you are being asked to authorize, pause rather than signing on the assumption that every claim request is routine.

Pay particular attention to approvals. Under the ERC-20 interface, an approval can let another address spend up to an authorized amount. That is distinct from merely seeing a token or reading campaign instructions. Check the spender and amount shown, and compare the request with the project’s documented process. An interface standard does not establish that a particular approval is appropriate or that a token implementation is safe. ([source](<https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-20>))

## Risks to check before interacting

Treat unexpected tokens and unsolicited claim invitations as unverified until you can confirm their origin. A wallet connection alone does not prove that a page or project is authentic, and it does not answer what a later signature or transaction will authorize. Never provide private keys or recovery material as a condition of claiming. Those credentials relate to access and authorization, not to proving eligibility.

Keep the stages distinct: viewing a page, connecting a wallet, signing a message, and authorizing a transaction are not interchangeable actions. Read the details presented for the action you are asked to take. Be cautious if a page’s description does not match the destination, amount, or permissions shown in the wallet, or if the instructions cannot be confirmed in project documentation. No general explanation can promise that an erroneous transfer or compromised credential can be recovered.

Also distinguish a wallet controlled by the recipient from an account managed by a third-party custodian. A third-party custodian manages access to private keys on a customer’s behalf. The rights and bankruptcy exposure connected with third-party token arrangements depend on their structure and governing documents; they may involve an indirect entitlement, contractual claim, or synthetic exposure rather than direct ownership of the referenced asset. Do not assume that a token’s appearance in an account establishes a particular custody or ownership arrangement. ([source](<https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/corp-fin-statement-tokenized-securities-012826-statement-tokenized-securities>))

## Project terms, compliance, and tax depend on context

Eligibility, availability, restrictions, and claim steps may vary by project and location. This explainer cannot establish that a specific distribution is available to a particular person or permitted in a particular place. Check the campaign’s current documentation and seek authoritative, location-specific information where a legal or compliance question matters.

Tax treatment also cannot be determined from the label “airdrop” alone. This article does not state tax consequences or offer legal conclusions. A specific assessment requires current guidance for the relevant jurisdiction or qualified professional advice. Keep the distinction clear: general token-distribution mechanics explain how a campaign might work, but they do not substitute for project terms or jurisdiction-specific analysis.

Before interacting, the practical questions are whether the project’s eligibility rules and claim steps are documented, whether the link and contract address match independently verified project channels, and whether the requested wallet action is clear. If an answer is missing, avoid treating assumptions or another campaign’s procedures as confirmation.

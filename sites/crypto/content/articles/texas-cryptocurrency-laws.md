Texas and federal rules may apply differently depending on what a cryptocurrency-related business does, how it handles assets or customer transactions, and who its customers are. This guide identifies the questions to verify; the available evidence does not establish current Texas licensing rules, federal registration duties, or tax treatment. Use current official sources and qualified Texas legal and tax advisers for decisions about a specific activity.

## What activities can bring crypto businesses under Texas rules?

Begin with the service, not the label. A company described as a “crypto platform,” “wallet,” or “exchange” may perform several distinct functions—or only one. Map whether it arranges or conducts exchanges, transfers assets for customers, holds or manages access keys, operates an application, issues a token, or accepts crypto in payment. Identify which entity performs each function and whether it handles customer assets or acts on a customer’s behalf.

A cryptocurrency, in a technical definition, is a digital asset, credit, or unit that can be cryptographically transferred between users of a blockchain network. A digital signature can authorize a transfer under the protocol, but a valid signature establishes control of the signing key under that protocol—not the signer’s legal identity or whether the transaction was prudent. Neither fact by itself decides how a regulator or tax authority treats an activity. [source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/Cryptocurrency>)

A direct peer-to-peer exchange is also different from a service that intermediates for customers. Cryptocurrency systems can support digital peer-to-peer exchange using distributed record-keeping rather than a conventional central transaction processor, although users may still rely on centralized wallets, exchanges, gateways, or other intermediaries. For a business review, trace whether the organization merely supplies a tool or instead participates in the transfer, exchange, custody, or other service. The technical model does not by itself determine the legal result. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2018/cryptocurrencies-looking-beyond-hype>)

Do not treat “exchange” as a complete description of a business model. A centralized exchange operator may combine trading with proprietary activity, lending, token issuance, and custody, but not every exchange performs every function. Combining roles can also create operational dependencies and conflicts. A useful activity map identifies each function, the entity responsible for it, and the customer relationship involved; the presence of one function does not establish that the others are present. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>)

Asset terminology may need similar care. A digital token can be a ledger record about an asset and its owner, with programmable rules governing use or transfer. That technical record does not by itself determine legal title, enforceability, or the quality of the represented asset. If a service involves a token tied to another asset, document what the token represents and which arrangement governs the customer’s rights rather than assuming the ledger entry answers those questions. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/tokenisation-context-money-and-other-assets-concepts-and-implications-central-banks>)

These distinctions help frame legal questions; they do not settle them. Asset type, customer relationship, business model, transaction structure, and locations may all be relevant facts to present to counsel. No broad category such as “wallet” or “payment” establishes an exemption or a licensing outcome without applying current law to the actual arrangement.

## Tax reporting depends on the transaction and taxpayer

Tax reporting is a separate inquiry from regulatory licensing. Whether an activity requires authorization does not, on its own, answer how a transaction should be classified or reported. Conversely, a tax reporting question does not determine whether a business needs a license or registration.

The accepted evidence for this overview does not establish current federal or Texas tax rules, reporting forms, classifications, valuation methods, filing duties, or the treatment of a particular transaction. It does not support a universal conclusion about whether a given transfer is taxable. ## A practical verification checklist for Texas operators

Use this process to turn a broad concern into a bounded set of questions and evidence:

1. **Map the product and activity.** Describe each service, asset involved, customer type, transaction path, and relevant location. Identify who executes or arranges transfers, who controls assets or keys, and which entity performs each function. Separate exchange, transfer, custody, platform, issuance, and payment activities instead of relying on one business label.
2. **Identify the questions without assuming the answer.** Ask whether the facts could fit a Texas or federal definition, whether licensing or registration may be relevant, and what exemptions or geographic conditions require review. Treat these as questions for counsel, not findings that a requirement applies or does not apply.
3. **Check current official sources.** Locate the applicable statutes, regulations, agency materials, and tax authority guidance. Record jurisdiction, source type, publication or effective date, and any uncertainty. Confirm that the material addresses the activity and entity under review; do not rely on an undated summary for a current operational decision.
4. **Separate legal and tax review.** Have qualified legal counsel assess regulatory and licensing questions. Have a qualified tax professional assess classification, valuation, reporting, and filing questions. Give each adviser the same accurate activity map and relevant records, but do not treat one adviser’s answer as resolving the other subject.
5. **Escalate unresolved interpretations.** If the source does not clearly address the transaction structure, customer relationship, asset, or geographic connection, document the uncertainty and obtain professional review before relying on an interpretation. The evidence available for this guide does not resolve such questions.
6. **Recheck before launch or material changes.** Revisit the analysis if the service, asset flow, customer relationship, custody arrangement, entity role, or relevant legal materials change. Verify current sources again before making a decision based on an earlier review.

This checklist is a method for identifying and verifying issues, not a compliance determination. The central task is to establish what each party does, then compare that activity with current Texas and federal sources and separately assess tax reporting. Where the applicable rule, exception, or tax treatment is not clear from authoritative current materials, obtain advice from qualified professionals rather than inferring an answer from the word “crypto.”

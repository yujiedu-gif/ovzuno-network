In a crypto proof-of-stake system, **stake** is cryptocurrency committed under the protocol’s rules so it can influence participation in consensus. Those rules can use stake to help determine who may validate or how much weight a participant has; stake alone does not guarantee selection, control, or a return.

## What “stake” means in crypto

Stake is not merely a token someone owns. In proof of stake, it refers to cryptocurrency committed to the network under its rules, with the stake influencing consensus participation or weight. The exact commitment and its consequences depend on the protocol, so the general definition does not establish whether assets are self-bonded, delegated, or held through another arrangement. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/reports/trillion-dollar-security.pdf>))

A useful distinction is between **ownership** and **protocol stake**. Owning a token does not, by itself, mean that it has been committed in a way the network recognizes. Conversely, a protocol’s use of stake does not tell you who controls the keys or what relationship a user has with any service. Those details require examining the particular network and arrangement.

## How stake relates to validators

A validator is a participant assigned protocol-defined tasks in a proof-of-stake system. Stake can influence whether a participant takes part in consensus or the weight assigned to that participation. It is one input to a protocol’s design—not a general promise that a particular holder will be selected or that the holder controls the network. Eligibility, selection, and responsibilities are defined by each protocol, and a general definition cannot specify their exact mechanics. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/reports/trillion-dollar-security.pdf>))

Ethereum offers a bounded example: its validator participation uses ETH deposits as slashable economic collateral. Ethereum validators check and attest to blocks and may propose a block when selected. These details describe Ethereum, not every proof-of-stake network. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/consensus-mechanisms/pos/>))

The connection to network security is economic and protocol-specific: committed stake is part of the mechanism the protocol uses to organize consensus participation. It is not, by itself, proof that a network is secure. Operational risks can include validator or client concentration, key compromise, inactivity, slashing, censorship, and recovery under extreme conditions. The relevance and handling of these risks depend on the network. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/reports/trillion-dollar-security.pdf>))

## Stake is not the same as a guaranteed return

The word *stake* describes a technical role in consensus; it does not promise a payment. A protocol may define incentives for participation, but the existence, conditions, and consequences of those incentives are separate from the definition of stake.

Ethereum’s rules, for example, include rewards for correct participation, missed rewards or penalties for inactivity, and slashing for specified conflicting messages. This is an Ethereum-specific description, not a universal schedule or promise of earnings. The technical meaning of stake should not be confused with passive income or a guaranteed outcome. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/consensus-mechanisms/pos/rewards-and-penalties/>))

## What can happen to committed stake

In some designs, committed assets are subject to protocol conditions, including penalties. Ethereum’s use of slashable collateral illustrates that stake can be exposed to protocol-defined consequences. That example does not establish the penalty rules, withdrawal timing, or recovery process for another network—or for a service built on top of one. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/consensus-mechanisms/pos/>))

It is also important to separate protocol rules from the arrangement used to access them. A protocol defines how it recognizes stake and what consequences its rules attach to participation. A wallet, intermediary, or other service may involve a separate custody or operating arrangement. The word *stake* alone does not explain those arrangements or resolve their risks. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/reports/trillion-dollar-security.pdf>))

## What the word “stake” does not tell you

By itself, *stake* does not identify whether assets are committed directly, delegated, or managed through an intermediary. Nor does the term specify rewards, fees, access conditions, custody, recovery, tax treatment, or legal status. Those details cannot be inferred from the general definition.

For exact mechanics, consult current documentation for the relevant protocol and distinguish its rules from the terms and responsibilities of any wallet or service involved. In short, stake is crypto committed under proof-of-stake rules to participate in consensus; how it affects validators and what conditions apply are protocol- and arrangement-specific.

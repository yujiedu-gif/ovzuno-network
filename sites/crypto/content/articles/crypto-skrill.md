Skrill is the name of a payment service; cryptocurrency is an asset category, and a blockchain is one type of system used to record transactions. Those terms describe different parts of a possible payment arrangement. A service can be relevant to a crypto-related task without every step occurring on a blockchain, but no particular Skrill feature or route can be inferred from the search phrase alone.

The question may concern different tasks: accessing a crypto-related feature through an account, transferring an asset, or paying a recipient. ## Crypto Skrill versus an on-chain crypto payment

An on-chain payment is submitted to a blockchain network for processing under that network’s rules. It differs conceptually from an account-mediated service transaction, where a provider’s terms and records may determine how the customer’s account activity is represented. This distinction does not establish Skrill’s specific custody, settlement, or recordkeeping arrangements; those depend on the product and route actually offered.

A transaction-signing interface provides one useful general distinction: some interfaces can return a signed transaction for an application to broadcast separately. In that arrangement, signing and network submission are separate steps. A signed transaction alone therefore does not demonstrate that it has been broadcast or included on a blockchain. This is an example of a technical workflow, not evidence that Skrill uses it. ([source](<https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/server-wallets/v2/evm-features/eip-712-signing>))

For a particular payment, establish which record you should expect to see. It could be a service account update, a provider’s transaction reference, or an on-chain transaction, depending on the route. Do not assume that a crypto-related account feature entails a direct transfer to an external wallet, or that the word “blockchain” in a description tells you who controls access to the asset. The actual product documentation must answer those questions.

The service and network also have distinct roles. A provider’s terms can describe its account features and responsibilities; the blockchain protocol governs the transactions submitted to that network. If a service arranges a transaction, its process may interact with a network, but that does not make every service-account event an on-chain payment. Conversely, an on-chain transfer does not by itself explain what a service provider may do with a customer account. Keep the transaction route and provider arrangement separate when comparing options.

A phygital NFT is a blockchain token associated with a physical item or with a claim about one. The token can record an association, but the label does not establish that the item is authentic, that the token holder possesses it, or that legal ownership has transferred. Those questions depend on evidence and terms beyond the token record.

## What “phygital NFT” means

“Phygital” combines physical and digital. In this context, it describes a connection between an NFT and a physical object. The token is a digital record; the object is a separate thing. A token may be associated with a stated claim, but the existence of a record does not make every associated claim true.

There is no universal meaning for the phrase established by the evidence available here. A project might describe a token as a certificate, a provenance record, or a way to access a service. Each description needs to be assessed on its own terms: what does the record refer to, who made the claim, and what process supports it? The word “phygital” does not answer those questions or define a standard set of rights.

It is useful to separate three ideas. First is the token record: what the blockchain records about the token. Second is the item: the physical object and evidence about its identity or condition. Third is the claim connecting the two: for example, that this particular token corresponds to that particular item. A record can state that a connection exists without independently proving the physical facts behind it.

## How an NFT can be linked to a physical item

A project could use an identifier, a tag, a certificate, packaging, or an external record to associate an item and a token. These are illustrative possibilities, not requirements of a universal phygital-NFT standard. Each approach creates a different question: how was the item identified, who recorded the connection, and how can someone check that the link still refers to the same item?

One technical proposal, ERC-4519, defines an interface for linking NFTs to physical assets or devices through asset addresses and owner or user authentication states. That describes a way an interface can represent a link. It does not, by itself, establish that a particular physical device is genuine, that a token was correctly bound to it, or that the item’s off-chain identity has been independently verified. Security depends on factors including the physical device, key protection, the manufacturer or issuer, firmware, and the process that connects the physical item to the blockchain record.

The connection may also rely on metadata. Metadata is information associated with a token; it may be recorded on-chain or accessed through a reference. A token URI can refer to metadata for a specific token, but that feature alone does not guarantee that the referenced content is available, permanent, accurate, or licensed.

This creates an important distinction. The blockchain may record or point to a claim, while details about the item and the process behind the claim may sit elsewhere. A reader should not treat the presence of a token, a URI, or an entry in a record as a substitute for evidence that the item and token were correctly connected.

## What the token may represent—and what it may not

A token may be associated with a digital certificate, information about an item, access, or a stated claim. What it represents depends on the relevant system and terms. The token record and the item are not automatically the same asset, and the record alone does not establish that the person controlling the token has physical possession.

Nor does a token transfer necessarily establish a transfer of the physical item or of legal title. In some third-party token arrangements, a token may represent an indirect custodial entitlement, a contractual claim, or synthetic exposure rather than direct ownership of the referenced asset. The rights can therefore differ from direct ownership. [source](<https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/corp-fin-statement-tokenized-securities-012826-statement-tokenized-securities>)

That example is a caution about possible structures, not a conclusion about any particular phygital NFT. The permitted meaning of a token depends on its specific arrangement and governing documents; the evidence available here does not establish what a particular holder’s legal rights are. A reader should keep separate what the token record says, what a project promises, and what the applicable terms actually provide. This article does not determine legal title or rights for an individual item.

## How to assess a claimed physical-digital link

Assess the association as a claim that needs supporting evidence, rather than assuming the token verifies it automatically. A practical review can start with these questions:

- **What does the token record identify?** Read the metadata and any cited records. Determine whether they describe an item, a certificate, an access benefit, or a claim about the item. If the token points to information elsewhere, the pointer is not itself proof of the information’s accuracy or continued availability.
- **How was the link made?** Look for a documented process explaining how the item was identified and connected to the token. If the project uses a tag, device, or certificate, ask what evidence shows that the identifier belongs to this item and how that association was established.
- **Who is making the claim?** Identify the issuer or other party responsible for the information and what role that party has in creating or maintaining the connection. A blockchain entry can record information according to system rules; that fact alone does not verify the off-chain facts that were entered.
- **What is supposed to happen after a change?** Look for documented procedures covering transfer of the item or token, replacement, loss, damage, or redemption. The existence of a token does not answer how the project handles these events. If a process is not documented, its outcome remains unclear rather than guaranteed.
- **What supports authenticity and custody?** Seek evidence about the item and its handling beyond the NFT itself. The token may help identify a claim, but the record alone does not independently authenticate a physical object or establish who has custody of it.

These questions help distinguish the existence of a record from the strength of the evidence behind it. They are not a certification method, and they do not establish that a particular item or token is authentic.

## Risks, limitations, and unresolved questions

A physical item and its associated digital record can become separated, damaged, inaccessible, or disputed. A tag or identifier may be lost or detached; records may depend on information or procedures outside the blockchain. The specific risks depend on how a project created and maintains the connection, and no general label resolves them.

Security statements also require careful scope. An interface that supports associating a token with a physical asset does not establish that the device, keys, issuer, or binding process is secure. Likewise, a token record does not by itself describe a recovery process. To assess those matters, readers need evidence about the particular implementation and arrangements; the label alone supplies none.

If an item changes hands while the digital record follows a different process, people may disagree about what the token represents or who can claim a benefit. A project’s terms and records may explain its intended procedure, but the token’s existence alone does not demonstrate that every party followed it or that the physical item remains in the stated condition.

Do not infer regulatory treatment, tax consequences, fees, availability, or compliance from the phrase “phygital NFT.” Those are separate questions that require current, relevant evidence, and the evidence used here does not resolve them. The supported conclusion is limited: a token can be associated with a physical item, but the connection’s meaning and reliability depend on the process, records, and terms supporting it—not on the label itself.

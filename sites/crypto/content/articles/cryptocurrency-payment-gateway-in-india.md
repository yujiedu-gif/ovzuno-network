An India-based business evaluating a proposed cryptocurrency payment gateway should first document exactly how a payment would move, who controls funds or keys at each step, and how the business would receive and account for settlement. Then it should obtain current, qualified Indian legal and tax review, confirm banking and provider terms directly, and record unresolved security, compliance, refund, and recovery questions. This checklist is for due diligence—not a conclusion that a particular workflow is permitted, suitable, or available in India.

## What a cryptocurrency payment gateway does in a business workflow

A gateway may sit between a customer’s payment instruction and the merchant’s process for recognizing or receiving payment. The label alone does not establish what the service actually does. A proposed service might coordinate a payment, monitor a network transaction, arrange settlement, convert an asset, or hold assets; the business must establish which functions are included in its particular arrangement and which party performs them.

Map the parties and responsibilities rather than relying on product terminology. Identify the customer, merchant, gateway, blockchain network, and any exchange, custodian, bank, or other financial intermediary. For each party, record what it does, what information or assets it handles, and what contract or policy governs that role. Token arrangements can support intermediation across an asset’s lifecycle, but the operational and governance design matters; the existence of a token or platform does not settle who is responsible for each step. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/tokenisation-context-money-and-other-assets-concepts-and-implications-central-banks>))

A useful first document is a one-page workflow diagram accompanied by a responsibility table. Avoid treating the gateway as a single, all-purpose actor unless its current documentation and contract actually assign it every relevant task.

## Map the proposed payment and settlement flow

Describe the flow in sequence, from the customer’s payment instruction to the merchant’s decision that an order is paid. Include the asset and network, the destination for the payment, how the transaction is monitored, the confirmation condition used by the business, and the form and destination of settlement. If conversion is proposed, identify who performs it and what the merchant receives. Confirm these details with the provider and relevant financial institution rather than relying on a general product description.

Record custody at every stage. Ask whether the customer sends directly to a merchant-controlled address, whether a provider receives or controls assets, and who can authorize transfers. If a third party holds assets or controls keys, document the contractual arrangement and the process for access, withdrawal, and recovery. Do not infer the answer from the words “gateway,” “wallet,” or “settlement.”

Keep network status separate from business status. A transaction being observed or reported by a service is not automatically the same as the merchant’s accounting decision, fulfillment decision, or refund decision. Write down the business’s own conditions for marking an order paid and releasing goods or services, and identify who is responsible for applying them. The payment workflow should also state how partial, duplicate, delayed, or otherwise disputed payments are escalated; if the provider’s terms do not explain these cases, treat them as open questions rather than assuming a standard remedy.

## Verify India-specific legal and regulatory requirements

Ask qualified Indian counsel to review the actual activity, not merely the proposed gateway’s category or marketing description. Provide the workflow diagram, contracts, parties, asset flows, custody details, conversion arrangements, and intended customer-facing process. Ask counsel to identify which current statutes, regulations, official guidance, or other requirements may apply to the business and its counterparties, and what facts could change that assessment.

Do not treat a provider’s ability to onboard a customer, offer a service, or process a transaction as proof that the merchant’s proposed use is legally permitted. The evidence available for this article does not establish the current Indian legal status or requirements for any particular gateway, asset, merchant, or business model. Those points require current authoritative Indian sources and review by a qualified adviser.

Ask counsel to clarify responsibility for customer disclosures, complaints, records, reporting, and any applicable compliance duties. Assign an owner to each responsibility and identify the contract or policy that supports the assignment. If a provider and merchant each assume the other will handle a task, document the gap for resolution before operational decisions are made.

## Check tax, banking, and compliance dependencies

Obtain transaction-specific tax advice based on the proposed business model and actual flows. Give the adviser the asset and settlement details, whether conversion is involved, the contractual parties, and the intended accounting treatment. Ask which current tax rules and reporting duties may apply to the particular transactions. This checklist does not determine tax treatment or substitute for advice about a specific business.

Confirm banking and settlement access directly with every relevant institution. Ask whether the proposed route is available for the business and workflow, what conditions or documentation apply, and who can change or suspend the arrangement. Do not assume that a provider’s service description proves that a bank or other institution will support the merchant’s intended use. Record the date and source of each confirmation because availability and terms can change.

Review compliance responsibilities with qualified advisers against the specific parties and transaction flow. Ask who is responsible for any applicable customer identity checks, transaction monitoring, sanctions controls, record retention, reporting, and escalation. The answer must be tied to the business’s circumstances and current requirements; this article cannot establish that any one control is legally required or sufficient for a particular Indian business.

## Assess security, recovery, fees, and availability

Request current provider documentation and contract terms covering custody, key management, access controls, incident response, and the division of security responsibilities. Ask who can initiate or approve transfers, how access is restored after a credential or key problem, and what happens if the provider cannot operate. Record which statements are contract commitments, which are operational descriptions, and which remain unverified. Do not present provider documentation as independent security testing.

Ask how mistaken transfers, network interruptions, delayed confirmations, refunds, and recovery requests are handled. Separate a provider’s customer-support process from what the network or settlement arrangement can actually do. Do not assume that an on-chain transfer can be reversed or recovered; obtain a workflow-specific explanation and document the limits. If the business cannot establish who makes the decision or bears responsibility in an exception, escalate that gap.

Verify service particulars directly and in writing: whether the service is currently available for the proposed India-based business, which assets and networks are supported for the intended route, what fees and conversion terms apply, and whether any offered yield or other feature has separate terms. Confirm limitations, exclusions, and conditions in the current contract and product documentation. This article does not establish the availability, price, performance, or terms of any provider.

For settlement planning, ask the parties responsible for payment and settlement how they will meet obligations under ordinary and stressed conditions. A financial-market infrastructure standard identifies sufficient liquid resources in relevant currencies as a liquidity-risk principle for designated infrastructures; it does not establish that every crypto product is such an infrastructure or prove that a particular provider has sufficient resources. Use the question as a prompt for workflow-specific diligence, not as a certification. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/committees/cpmi/pfmi/overview>))

## Document the decision and unresolved checks

Maintain a decision record with the workflow version, source or contract for each answer, date verified, responsible owner, and any adviser who reviewed it. Keep separate sections for legal, tax, banking, compliance, security, settlement, and provider availability so that one party’s confirmation is not mistaken for evidence on another topic.

Before reaching an operational decision, resolve or formally escalate questions about authorization, custody, banking access, settlement, recovery, and reporting. A record of unresolved points is more useful than an unsupported assumption that a provider will handle them. Establish who must revisit each issue and what change would trigger a review.

Recheck the record when laws or official guidance, provider terms, banking access, or the proposed workflow change. The review should reflect the actual arrangement, including any change in asset, network, custody, conversion, or settlement route. A documented checklist can make those dependencies visible, but it is not a legal or tax opinion, a security assurance, or a guarantee that a service will remain available.

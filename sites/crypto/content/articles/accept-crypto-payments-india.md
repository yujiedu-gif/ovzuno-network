An India-based business should not decide whether to accept crypto-related payments until it has mapped the exact flow, obtained current India-specific legal and tax advice, assigned responsibilities, and assessed operational readiness. The evidence available for this article does not establish India’s current legal or tax treatment, whether a particular business may accept such payments, or which services are available. Use this checklist to identify questions for qualified advisers, not as a legal or tax conclusion.

## Map what “accepting crypto” means for the business

Begin with a diagram of the proposed arrangement. Identify who pays, which entity receives the asset or payment benefit, and who controls the receiving address or account. Record the asset and network, each intermediary, any conversion or custody step, the intended settlement destination, and the point at which the business would treat the payment as complete in its records.

These choices describe different parts of a flow. Direct receipt and receipt through an intermediary should not be treated as equivalent: they may involve different counterparties, account arrangements, custody, and operational steps. A centralized crypto exchange may combine functions such as trading, lending, token issuance, proprietary activity, and custody. Not every exchange performs every function, and combining roles can create operational dependencies or conflicts. This general description does not establish the services, terms, or legal status of any particular intermediary in India. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>)

For each proposed route, write down what happens from payer to business and onward to any conversion or settlement destination. State which organization performs each step and which records it will provide. If more than one route is being considered, map them separately rather than describing them all as “accepting crypto.” The precise flow gives advisers a defined arrangement to review and helps the business locate questions about responsibilities, records, and exceptions.

Do not assume that an asset label alone describes the full arrangement. Record the exact asset and network under consideration and the role of any service that receives, holds, converts, or transfers it. The available evidence does not establish a universal India-specific asset list, network list, or service availability. Those details need to be checked for the proposed arrangement and at the time the business is considering it.

## Accept crypto payments india: verify the current legal and tax position

Ask qualified advisers with relevant India expertise to review the actual flow and business activities. Give them the diagram, proposed contracts and intermediary terms, asset and network details, and intended treatment of receipts. Request that they identify the primary legal and tax materials they reviewed, the dates and scope of their review, assumptions, and questions that remain unresolved.

The evidence available here does not establish an India-specific rule, eligibility condition, tax treatment, reporting requirement, or general permission or prohibition. It cannot support a conclusion that a business may accept, retain, convert, or otherwise use crypto-related payments in India. Do not fill this gap by importing a rule from another jurisdiction. For example, U.S. sanctions guidance addresses U.S. persons and virtual-currency businesses subject to U.S. jurisdiction; it does not state Indian requirements. [source](<https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/913571/download?inline=>)

Ask advisers to consider the specific asset, entities and counterparties, receipt and conversion steps, accounting treatment, and any relevant tax or reporting questions. Separate confirmed conclusions from assumptions and items needing further review. Where current primary materials have not been verified, record the verification as an open condition rather than treating an unconfirmed answer as settled.

This process should produce a dated advice record tied to the proposed flow, not a generic statement about crypto. If the business changes the asset, network, intermediary, receiving entity, or conversion and custody steps, ask advisers whether the earlier review still applies. The evidence here does not specify how Indian law or tax treatment applies to any of those variants; the business needs qualified, current review of the actual proposal.

## Assign compliance and counterparty responsibilities

Create a responsibility matrix before implementation. For each control that advisers determine is applicable, name the party performing it, the party accountable for the outcome, the records retained, and the route for questions or exceptions. Potential subjects to raise with advisers include customer and transaction checks, monitoring, reporting, sanctions screening, and recordkeeping. Listing these topics does not establish that each applies to an India-based business or to the proposed flow.

Review each intermediary’s role and written terms. Identify which entity receives the asset, who controls relevant accounts or keys, and what the intermediary does with the asset. A provider’s involvement does not, by itself, establish that the business has no remaining responsibilities. The evidence available here does not establish the duties of an Indian merchant or intermediary, nor does it establish that a particular service is available or appropriate in India.

A U.S.-specific example illustrates why jurisdiction and role must be checked rather than assumed: certain U.S. persons and virtual-currency businesses subject to U.S. jurisdiction are responsible for avoiding prohibited sanctions transactions and applying a risk-based compliance program appropriate to their activities. That evidence is not a basis for assigning an Indian business the same duties. [source](<https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/913571/download?inline=>)

Set an escalation route for suspected fraud, disputed or unusual payments, service interruptions, and uncertainty about assigned controls. Name the person who reviews changes to the arrangement and checks whether earlier advice remains applicable. Keep operational ownership distinct from legal applicability: obtain professional advice before describing a control as a legal requirement.

## Test operational, security, and recovery readiness

Assess how the business would verify the intended destination, asset, and network before relying on the payment flow. Determine who can access the receiving account or keys, how access changes are authorized, and who reviews those arrangements. If the business considers self-custody, recognize that the self-custody user controls and is responsible for the private keys and recovery material needed to access the crypto assets. This does not establish that any particular custody approach is secure or that lost access can be restored. [source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>)

Write exception procedures before relying on the arrangement. Consider delayed, misdirected, underpaid, or unsupported-network payments. Specify who investigates, who communicates with the payer, what evidence is retained, and who decides whether to pause further activity. The available evidence does not establish that a misdirected transfer can be reversed or recovered, so do not promise recovery. Similarly, do not describe an arrangement as secure without evidence relevant to that specific design and its limitations.

Assess continuity and recordkeeping as part of readiness. Identify who can access the relevant business records and receiving arrangements during staff absence or an incident, how authority is maintained or changed, and how the business will compare payment records with its own accounting records. Assign an owner to maintain each procedure and document any limits. This assessment does not establish a particular technical control, recovery method, or accounting treatment as sufficient.

Do not treat operational readiness as a substitute for legal or tax review. A well-documented process cannot answer whether the proposed business activity is permitted or what tax treatment applies. Conversely, advice about legal or tax questions does not establish that the business can manage access, exceptions, continuity, and recordkeeping for the chosen flow. Keep the assessments distinct and identify which remains incomplete.

## Make a documented go, pause, or stop decision

Prepare a decision record stating the proposed scope and flow, parties involved, responsible owners, advice received, dates of source review, unresolved questions, and conditions for approval. A pause is appropriate when a material legal, tax, counterparty, custody, or recovery question remains unresolved. The available evidence does not justify recommending adoption or categorical rejection.

Set review triggers for changes to the asset, network, receiving entity, intermediary, conversion or custody steps, and relevant advice. Before proceeding, obtain qualified confirmation of applicable India-specific requirements and decide whether the business can operate the controls it has assigned. If a material question remains open, record who must resolve it and what evidence is needed before reconsidering the decision.

The result should be a traceable decision, not a broad conclusion about crypto payments in India. This checklist helps operators describe the arrangement, request current jurisdiction-specific review, assign responsibilities, and surface operational gaps. It does not determine Indian law, tax treatment, eligibility, or the availability of a service.

## How a crypto payment processor fits into a transaction

At a high level, a payer initiates a transfer, the business assesses whether it meets the business’s payment requirements, and the transaction is handled under the relevant network’s rules. A processor may be involved in presenting payment information or reporting status, but those possible roles do not establish what any particular provider does. Its actual functions must be verified in its current documentation.

For an on-chain payment, a key operational distinction is that broadcast does not itself mean the merchant’s settlement conditions have been met. A merchant should not fulfill an order solely because a transaction was broadcast; the merchant’s configured settlement conditions apply. In practice, a business needs to know what status information it receives and what conditions its own process requires before fulfillment. A notification or transaction reference should not automatically be treated as proof that an order is ready.

Processing, custody, and conversion are separate questions. A business should not assume that a processor holds funds, controls the payer’s wallet, or converts crypto to fiat unless the provider’s documentation confirms the arrangement. Likewise, the broad phrase “crypto payments” does not specify confirmation handling, supported assets or networks, or refund procedures. Those details may differ by provider and configuration.

The transaction’s network also matters when checking instructions. Payment requirements are not interchangeable across all assets and networks. Before authorization, the exact asset and network should be compared with the recipient’s instructions; compatibility and acceptance also depend on the destination, recipient requirements, and service. This is a practical verification step, not evidence that a particular provider supports a given combination.

## Claims to verify before relying on a provider

Use a claim-by-claim check rather than treating a provider description as a complete account of the service:

- **Identity and documentation:** Confirm the legal entity through authoritative provider materials. Locate official product documentation and terms, and check that they apply to the service and account arrangement being considered.
- **Service scope:** Verify directly which payment functions are offered. Check supported assets, networks, countries, integrations, and availability in current materials; do not infer these from a name or third-party summary.
- **Payment status and fulfillment:** Determine how the service reports payment progress and which conditions the business uses before treating an order as settled. A broadcast transaction alone does not meet the merchant’s configured settlement conditions.
- **Fees and commercial terms:** Review the current published fee schedule and terms for the relevant product and transaction. Do not rely on a general description or an older summary for commercial details.
- **Custody and conversion:** Establish whether custody or conversion to fiat is involved, which entity performs each function, and what the provider’s terms say about the arrangement. Do not assume these services are included in payment processing.
- **Refunds, security, and recovery:** Check current provider documentation for workflows, controls, responsibilities, and limitations. Do not treat a general description as proof of security, a promise of recovery, or a guarantee that a payment can be reversed.
- **Business responsibilities:** Identify what the business itself must do for payment-status checks, customer communication, exceptions, refunds, and record handling. Do not assume a processor takes on every responsibility connected to a sale.

This checklist is a way to structure research, not a ranking of providers. It does not establish that any named company meets the checklist or that a particular service is suitable for a business. Each claim should be tied to a specific source and to the product and circumstances it actually covers.

Review current provider materials for the exact product and arrangement, and note what those materials do and do not establish. Do not turn a provider’s broad security language into a guarantee.

These categories should not be treated as interchangeable. A plan describes an intended direction; it does not establish deployment. ## How blockchain infrastructure could fit into capital markets

A distributed ledger is a record maintained according to rules shared across network participants. At a general level, a transaction becomes part of a shared ledger after cryptographic authorization, validation under network rules, grouping into a block, and a consensus decision. This describes a broad transaction-recording process, not a Nasdaq system or a specific securities workflow. [source](<https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2018/nist.ir.8202.pdf>) [source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ir/8202/final>)

Blockchain participation models also vary. Some allow open participation in block publication, while others authorize operators to maintain the ledger; systems may separately restrict who can read data or submit transactions. This distinction is useful when evaluating a proposed market infrastructure, but it does not identify the permissions, governance, or participant roles of any Nasdaq initiative. Those details would need to be stated in Nasdaq’s own technical or product documentation. [source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ir/8202/final>)

In a capital-markets context, a shared record could, in principle, be relevant to coordinating transaction information or recording a digital representation of an asset. But a ledger record is only one potential component of a larger arrangement. It does not, by itself, explain who holds assets or keys, how participants are identified, which organization handles compliance, how settlement is completed, or how a new system connects with existing market infrastructure. The available evidence does not establish Nasdaq’s approach to any of those matters.

The distinction between a ledger and a user-facing service is important. A technical network may record transactions, while a separate application or service may provide access and workflow functions. A general description of one infrastructure component cannot establish the full responsibilities, controls, or service terms of an organization associated with it. No Nasdaq-specific architecture or operational roles are documented in the allowed evidence.

Likewise, a decentralized exchange is a particular model, not a generic synonym for a securities exchange. In one described model, users can retain control of assets while trades execute or settle through smart contracts on a blockchain. That model is a general comparison point only. It is not evidence that Nasdaq has implemented decentralized exchange functionality, adopted self-custody, or changed how any market operates. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>)

## What to verify in Nasdaq’s initiative disclosures

Begin with the original source: a Nasdaq announcement, filing, product document, or other attributable company disclosure. Record the publication date, the exact status language, and whether the statement refers to a proposal, pilot, or operating service. Then check for a later primary source that clarifies or updates that status. Without this step, a summary can make an initiative sound more mature or broader than the source supports.

Next, extract the disclosed scope rather than inferring it from a technology label. Look for the named workflow, participating organizations, assets, market functions, technical integrations, and limits. If the source does not identify these elements, mark them as unknown. An announcement that mentions blockchain does not establish that a system processes live activity, supports particular assets, connects to other networks, or includes every part of a market workflow.

Operational claims need their own evidence. A reader assessing whether a system is in production should look for explicit, dated information about actual use and the extent of that use. A pilot announcement alone does not establish production operation, broad participant access, or interoperability. Where the documentation is silent, the accurate conclusion is that the detail is unverified—not that the most common industry design applies.

Also separate documented technical functions from organizational responsibilities. A ledger’s ability to record information does not establish who controls access, custody, records, keys, or incident response. The allowed evidence does not verify Nasdaq-specific security controls, recovery arrangements, fees, availability, or compliance responsibilities. Those matters require current, initiative-specific documentation rather than assumptions drawn from general blockchain descriptions.

This is a system-level caution, not a finding that Nasdaq uses a bridge or that any particular Nasdaq project has these characteristics. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1335.pdf>)

More generally, the label “decentralized” does not by itself establish how control is distributed in practice. The evidence here does not describe Nasdaq’s network structure, if any, and therefore supports no conclusion about its degree of decentralization or concentration.

## Questions decision-makers should ask before relying on the information

A disciplined review can begin with five questions:

- What exact workflow and participants does the primary source name, and what dated evidence confirms whether the activity is planned, a pilot, or live operation?
- Which assets, market functions, technical interfaces, and existing systems are explicitly in scope—and which are not addressed?
- Which organization controls access, records, custody, keys, and incident response, if the documentation specifies those responsibilities?
- What current source documents settlement arrangements, security controls, recovery processes, fees, and availability? Which of these remain unstated?
- What jurisdictional, regulatory, tax, and compliance questions need separate primary-source verification or qualified professional review?

The questions are not proof that any particular arrangement has a shortcoming; they identify details that must be documented before drawing conclusions. If a source does not answer them, record the gap and avoid importing assumptions from another network or service. On the evidence available here, the meaning and status of Nasdaq-specific blockchain activity remain unverified, while the general concepts above provide context—not confirmation of implementation.

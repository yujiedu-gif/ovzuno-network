Readers assessing which crypto exchanges are “most used” should first ask what the source counts, which venues and users it covers, and over what period. Customer accounts, website visits, and trading activity recorded by an exchange describe different things. Unless the definitions and scopes align, a ranking is a result under particular criteria—not a universal measure of exchange usage. The evidence available here does not provide current exchange-by-exchange figures or a shared measurement method, so it cannot identify a defensible overall leader.

## What does “most used” mean for crypto exchanges?

“Usage” is not one standardized measure. It may refer to people using a service, visits to an interface, or activity such as orders and executed trades. Those measures answer different questions. A visit does not establish that someone placed a trade, while trading records do not, by themselves, reveal how many distinct people generated them.

A centralized crypto exchange commonly records customer orders and executed trades in an internal order book and account system. That is one possible source of activity data, but it describes what the system records; it is not automatically a count of unique customers or a complete view across venues. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>)

A useful comparison therefore starts by naming its unit of measurement. If the source counts accounts, clarify whether it reports registered accounts or a measure of active accounts, if that distinction is available. If it counts visits, check what the source defines as a visit and the period covered. If it reports trading activity, determine whether it means volume, transactions, orders, or another metric. Do not treat those labels as equivalent when the source does not define them that way.

Scope matters just as much as the metric. Establish whether the claim is global or limited to a geography, whether it covers centralized exchanges or a wider class of trading venues, and which reporting period it uses. A result that omits these details may still describe a source’s own data, but it cannot settle a broader question about which exchanges are most used.

## How to compare exchange usage metrics

For every figure, record four basic items: the metric definition, the data provider or reporting venue, the coverage, and the time period. Then look for an explanation of how the data was gathered, calculated, or estimated. If the source does not explain a point, mark it as unknown rather than filling it in with an assumption.

Separate venue-reported records from estimates and other measures. Internal account and order-book records reflect the activity captured by that venue’s system. They do not establish that other platforms were measured on the same basis or that an individual using multiple platforms is counted only once. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>)

Next, test whether two figures answer the same question. A website-visit estimate and a trading-activity figure cannot be placed on one scale as if they were two readings of the same quantity. Even two figures labeled “volume” require caution if their sources do not define the included venues, assets, activity, or time window in compatible ways.

A simple comparison table can make the evidence easier to inspect. Useful columns include the source, metric, unit, covered venues, geography, reporting period, and methodology notes. Add an explicit “not stated” entry when information is missing. This makes gaps visible and prevents a polished table from implying more comparability than the source material warrants.

If definitions and scopes differ, present the figures separately and explain the difference. A ranking may be meaningful within one source’s stated method, but it should not be generalized beyond that scope. Where methodology is unavailable, say so and avoid treating the result as an established cross-platform comparison.

## What trading volume can—and cannot—show

Trading volume is a measure of recorded trading under a source’s definition. It is not a direct count of users. One person may generate multiple trades, and a volume measure itself does not identify how many distinct people participated. The evidence considered here supplies no exchange-specific volume figures or common methodology with which to compare them, so it supports no current ranking by volume.

To interpret a reported number, check the source’s stated venue coverage, asset coverage, time window, and calculation method. Ask whether the number is reported by the venue or estimated by another provider. If the source does not disclose those details, preserve the uncertainty in any summary. The figure can be described as what that source reports, but it should not be presented as directly comparable to a figure whose definitions may differ.

A volume figure also cannot establish service quality or reliability. It measures only the activity covered by its definition; other characteristics need separate evidence. Inferring a broader operational conclusion from a single usage metric would exceed what the metric demonstrates.

Venue boundaries can complicate interpretation too. Some centralized exchange operators may combine third-party trading with proprietary trading, margin lending, token issuance, and custody, but not every operator performs every function. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>) A report should make clear which entities and activities it counts rather than assuming every venue grouped under “exchange” has the same functional scope. This is a reason to inspect the source’s inclusion rules, not a basis for assigning unreported activity to any particular venue.

## Why exchange rankings can conflict

Rankings can differ because their authors choose different metrics, periods, assets, or venue coverage. One might rank a stated measure of trading activity for a particular interval; another might compare visits or another usage proxy. Both could be consistent with their own criteria while producing different orderings. The difference does not, by itself, show that one result is universally correct.

Data coverage and method can also limit like-for-like interpretation. If one provider estimates activity while another uses a different source or definition, a shared label may conceal important differences. Where the source does not explain its methodology or gaps, readers cannot reliably determine how much of the difference reflects actual activity and how much reflects measurement choices. The responsible response is to report the limitation, not to invent an adjustment.

A ranking is easiest to interpret when it states its source, metric, period, venue coverage, geography, and inclusion criteria. Present the result as “highest under these criteria” rather than as a timeless ordering. If another ranking uses a different scope, keep its result distinct and describe what changed. When key criteria are missing, label the ranking incomplete and avoid making it the basis of a universal claim.

The category “exchange” itself may cover organizations with differing functions. Some providers may combine trading, custody, lending, market-making, and other services, while others do not.  This variation reinforces the need to check which entities a source treats as exchanges and what activity it includes. It does not justify assuming that every organization offers each listed service or that the services are measured identically.

## What the available evidence cannot establish

Usage measures do not establish security, regulatory status, fees, availability in a particular jurisdiction, recovery arrangements, yields, or suitability. Each is a separate claim requiring current, specific evidence. Because the evidence available for this comparison includes no current exchange-by-exchange usage data or common measurement methodology, it cannot support a named winner or a universal ranking.

Custody is also distinct from usage. In a common centralized-exchange model, customers transfer funds and their custody to the exchange before trading on the venue’s internal order book; custody and legal ownership arrangements vary by platform and jurisdiction. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>) A usage figure cannot establish the custody arrangement or customer rights for a particular exchange. Those questions require separate examination of current provider information and applicable terms.

Nor should a ranking be used as a shortcut for jurisdiction-specific availability or compliance. The evidence needed to assess those subjects is different from a count of visits, accounts, or trading activity. Keep any operational or legal-status conclusion separate, scoped to the relevant jurisdiction and supported by current authoritative evidence. Tax questions likewise fall outside what usage metrics can answer.

The practical method is straightforward: identify what was counted, who reported it, which venues and activity were covered, and when. Compare only figures whose definitions and scope are sufficiently aligned for the question at hand. Where they are not, explain the mismatch and limit the conclusion to each source’s stated criteria. This approach helps readers assess claims about the most used crypto exchanges without mistaking a narrow metric for a complete judgment about a venue.

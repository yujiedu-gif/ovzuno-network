A Bitcoin dominance chart shows Bitcoin’s market capitalization as a percentage of a selected total cryptoasset market capitalization. It is a relative share, not a direct reading of Bitcoin’s price, trading volume, network usage, or future performance. The percentage depends on what the chart counts in its denominator and how it calculates the included market capitalizations.

## What a Bitcoin Dominance Chart Measures

The chart compares two quantities: Bitcoin’s market capitalization and the combined market capitalization of a selected group of cryptoassets. The numerator is Bitcoin’s market capitalization; the denominator is the total market capitalization used by the chart. The displayed dominance percentage expresses Bitcoin’s portion of that total.

The denominator is essential to the definition. A chart that includes one set of cryptoassets may produce a different share from a chart that includes another set, even if both refer to the same time. A label such as “total crypto market” does not, by itself, specify which assets are included or how the source determines their supply.

Dominance is narrower than several other market measures. It is not Bitcoin’s price, which is the price assigned to Bitcoin at a given time. It is not trading volume, or the amount of trading activity represented by a volume measure. Nor does a market-cap share directly measure network usage. It answers a specific question: what fraction of the chart’s selected market-cap total is attributed to Bitcoin?

## How the Dominance Percentage Is Calculated

The general form of the calculation is:

**Bitcoin dominance = (Bitcoin market capitalization ÷ selected total cryptoasset market capitalization) × 100**

For a hypothetical example, suppose a chart uses a Bitcoin market capitalization of 600 units and a selected total of 1,000 units. The calculation gives a 60% share. These invented units demonstrate the arithmetic only; they are not a current market figure and do not describe a particular provider’s data.

The formula is simple, but its inputs require definition. To reproduce a chart’s percentage, a reader would need to identify the assets included in the denominator, the supply measure used in calculating each asset’s market capitalization, and the timing of the price and supply inputs. If any of these choices differ, the resulting share may differ as well. The evidence available for this article does not document a specific chart provider’s methodology, so it cannot establish one universal asset list, supply convention, or data timestamp.

A percentage is therefore best read with its source and date attached. A value without that context does not disclose the denominator or the input conventions behind it. Historical comparisons are most meaningful when the compared figures use consistent coverage and calculation methods; otherwise, a difference could reflect changed inputs rather than only a change in relative market capitalization.

## How to Read Changes in the Chart

A rising share means Bitcoin accounts for a larger portion of the selected total under that chart’s methodology. A falling share means it accounts for a smaller portion. The direction describes the ratio; it does not, by itself, explain what caused the ratio to move.

The share can change because Bitcoin’s market capitalization changes, because the market capitalization of other included assets changes, or because both move. For example, a rising share could occur alongside an increase in Bitcoin’s market capitalization, a decrease in the market capitalization of other assets in the denominator, or a combination of movements. The percentage alone does not separate those possibilities. To understand the movement, examine the underlying numerator and denominator over the same period.

A larger share does not necessarily mean Bitcoin’s absolute market capitalization increased. A share is a comparison with a total, so it can rise if the denominator changes relative to the numerator. Similarly, a falling share does not, by itself, show that Bitcoin’s market capitalization fell. The ratio describes Bitcoin’s relative portion of a selected total, not the standalone change in either quantity.

The same distinction limits performance conclusions. A dominance chart alone does not establish absolute gains or losses for Bitcoin or the broader selected market. It also does not show which asset will perform better in the future. Treat a change as a description of relative market-cap share, not as a forecast or a trading signal.

## Limits and Comparability

Before comparing charts, check whether they use the same denominator coverage, supply methodology, and data timing. If those details are unavailable, the charts may not be directly comparable. Their percentages can differ because of the way the totals are constructed, not necessarily because they describe different underlying market movements. This article does not identify or endorse a chart provider or assert a provider-specific calculation.

Market capitalization is also distinct from liquidity, money invested, and realized value. Liquidity reflects the time and expense involved in buying or selling, including spread, transaction cost, price impact, and immediacy. A market-cap share does not directly measure those dimensions. It therefore cannot, on its own, establish how easily an asset could be bought or sold or what price a particular transaction would receive.

The practical interpretation is limited but useful: the chart summarizes Bitcoin’s share of a specified cryptoasset market-cap total according to the source’s inputs. Keep the denominator and methodology in view, and consult the underlying values when trying to explain a change. The percentage alone does not establish absolute performance, trading conditions, network activity, or future outcomes.

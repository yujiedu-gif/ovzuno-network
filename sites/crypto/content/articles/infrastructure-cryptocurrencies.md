“Infrastructure cryptocurrency” is a broad descriptive label, not a single technical category. It refers to a crypto asset described as having a role in a blockchain system’s operation, but the label alone does not establish what the asset does. To assess a claim, identify the relevant protocol function, check the asset’s documented role, and keep the network’s rules separate from the asset itself.

## What “infrastructure cryptocurrency” can mean

A cryptocurrency can be defined technically as a digital asset, credit, or unit that can be cryptographically transferred between users of a blockchain network. That definition describes a transferable digital unit; it does not say that every such unit powers infrastructure or performs the same task. ([source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/Cryptocurrency>))

“Infrastructure cryptocurrency” is best read as a descriptive phrase. A project or commentator may use it for an asset associated with a blockchain’s operation, but the phrase does not identify a universal technical standard or prove a particular function. Classification depends on the specific system and evidence for the asset’s role.

A blockchain system has multiple components. At a high level, it contains protocol rules, a transaction ledger, and participants who update, store, or read that ledger. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2018/cryptocurrencies-looking-beyond-hype>)) The crypto asset is not the whole system. It is one element whose relationship to the other components must be established from project-specific documentation.

This is useful when a description uses “infrastructure” as though it were a precise technical classification. The word may suggest that an asset is important to a system, but it does not specify how the system works, which participants perform each task, or whether the asset is necessary for that task. Those details need to be stated separately.

## How infrastructure cryptocurrencies relate to protocols

A protocol is a set of rules that defines how a network operates. Participants apply those rules while maintaining and using the ledger. The protocol, ledger, and participant network are related, but they are distinct parts of the system. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2018/cryptocurrencies-looking-beyond-hype>))

A crypto asset, by contrast, is a transferable digital unit associated with a blockchain network. ([source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/Cryptocurrency>)) A connection between an asset and a protocol does not by itself mean the asset represents ownership of the protocol, gives its holder control over the network, or guarantees access to a service. Those conclusions require separate evidence about the design and any applicable rights or arrangements. A technical association should not be mistaken for a legal or economic claim.

To describe the relationship carefully, ask two questions: What does the protocol specify? What role, if any, does the asset have under those rules? A project might document an asset as usable for a particular network operation. That would be a statement about that design, not proof that all infrastructure-associated assets share the same function. A network’s protocol and participants can also be described without establishing what role a particular asset plays.

This separation prevents a common category error: treating a system-level capability as though it automatically belongs to the associated asset. A protocol might define a process that participants carry out, while an asset could have a separate or limited role. Conversely, an asset’s stated function does not explain all the protocol rules or establish how the network’s other components operate. State only the relationship supported by documentation.

## Possible roles of an infrastructure crypto asset

An asset’s function is design-specific. A careful description names the role the project documents and the protocol conditions under which it applies; it does not treat a possible role as a defining feature of the category.

Claims readers may encounter connect assets to transaction costs, consensus, validation, network security, governance, or access. The evidence available for this article does not establish that any of these functions applies generally to infrastructure cryptocurrencies. Verify each claim against the relevant project and protocol documentation. Do not infer a fee or security role merely because an asset is transferable or associated with a blockchain.

The distinction between consensus and an asset is especially important. A protocol may specify how participants agree on ledger updates, but that fact alone does not show that a particular asset is used in consensus or security. To support such a statement, documentation would need to identify the asset’s specific role and the rules governing it. Without that evidence, describe the role as unverified rather than as a property of the asset.

Governance is another area where wording can overstate what is known. Ethereum protocol changes, for example, involve proposal, technical review, implementation, coordination, and adoption across network stakeholders. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/governance/>)) That process does not establish that an associated crypto asset automatically gives holders governance rights. A claim about voting or control needs evidence about the project’s specific mechanism and the decisions it can affect.

The same caution applies to claims that an asset is required for access or for an operational task. Relevant documentation should identify the activity, the rules that govern it, and whether the described function is available under the stated conditions. If the documentation does not establish those points, avoid presenting the claimed relationship as settled.

## How to evaluate a project’s infrastructure claims

Start with the project’s technical documentation. Identify the protocol, the relevant network, and the asset’s stated role. Then separate rules governing network operation from functions assigned to the asset. A description of protocol behavior does not necessarily establish an asset function; a description of an asset does not, by itself, establish the full protocol behavior.

Next, distinguish a specified function from a proposed or optional one. The evidence here does not establish the current status or operation of any particular project, so those details require current project and network documentation. If a document describes a capability without stating its conditions or scope, do not expand it into a universal claim.

Look for independent technical evidence when a claim concerns network operation or security assumptions. Be precise about what that evidence establishes: for example, evidence about protocol rules should not be presented as proof of an asset’s rights or role. Do not imply that a review, test, or verification occurred unless evidence supports that statement. Treat project labels and promotional descriptions as claims to examine, not proof that a function exists, is available, or confers a right.

A practical review can be organized around three questions:

- What do the protocol rules specify?
- What does the asset do within those rules, if anything?
- Which parts of the claim depend on current project documentation or other arrangements?

Keep the answers separate in summaries and internal assessments. This makes it easier to spot when a statement moves from a network’s capability to an unsupported assertion about an asset, or from a stated asset function to a broader claim about the whole system.

## Risks and limits to keep in view

An asset’s described role is not a guarantee that the role will remain unchanged or operate as a reader expects. Ethereum’s change process, for example, includes coordination and adoption across network stakeholders, illustrating that protocol changes involve more than an asset label. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/governance/>)) The specific change process and its effects must be evaluated for the project in question.

More broadly, network functions can depend on protocol rules, participants, and the arrangements that connect them. For example, stablecoin bridges coordinate value between blockchain systems that otherwise maintain separate token records. ([source](<https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2023/NIST.IR.8408.pdf>)) This example illustrates a relationship between a particular infrastructure function and the systems it connects; it does not establish that a given asset performs that function or that all bridge designs work the same way. Check the relevant system documentation before describing an asset as necessary to, or responsible for, cross-chain operation.

Asset utility does not establish value, price performance, or guaranteed demand. Those conclusions do not follow simply from a claimed infrastructure function. This explainer does not assess the investment merits of any asset.

Finally, the label cannot resolve questions about security, recovery, fees, availability, or legal treatment. Those require project- and jurisdiction-specific evidence, which is not established here. Use “infrastructure cryptocurrency” as a starting description, then verify the particular asset’s documented function and keep it distinct from the protocol and network with which it is associated.

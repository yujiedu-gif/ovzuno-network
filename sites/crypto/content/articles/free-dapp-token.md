A “free dApp token” usually means a token is offered without a purchase price, not that claiming or using it has no cost, conditions, or risk. Before interacting, verify the offer through authoritative project information, check the token’s network and contract address, and understand exactly what your wallet is being asked to do. A displayed balance alone does not establish a token’s value, liquidity, legitimacy, or usability.

## What does “free” mean in a dApp token offer?

A decentralized application, or dApp, is an application that interacts with blockchain-based components. In a token offer, “free” may describe the absence of a purchase price for the token. It does not, by itself, explain what other steps or resources the offer might require. The available evidence here does not establish terms for any particular distribution, so check the relevant project’s authoritative materials for eligibility, deadlines, and claim instructions.

A claim may involve a wallet interaction rather than a simple receipt. That distinction matters: seeing an offer is not the same as understanding the action required to obtain the token. Do not assume that a claim has no associated transaction costs or that every offer uses the same process. Network and transaction conditions depend on the specific system and current circumstances; verify any displayed fee and transaction details before deciding.

## Check the source and the token contract

Start with the source of the offer. Navigate to project information through channels you have independently verified, rather than relying on a link in an unsolicited message. Branding, a familiar name, or a polished page does not by itself verify who operates a site or whether a claim is genuine.

If the offer identifies a token, compare its contract address and network with information published in authoritative project materials. A token name or symbol alone is not a unique identifier you can rely on. ERC-20, for example, defines a common interface for balances, direct transfers, and approvals, but that interface does not establish a token’s backing, market value, compliance, issuance policy, or code safety. A familiar interface is therefore not proof that a particular token or offer is legitimate. [source](<https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-20>)

Also check what the project says the token is for and whether the claim instructions match the source you verified. Do not fill gaps in unclear terms with assumptions: if the source, address, network, or process cannot be confirmed, treat that uncertainty as a reason to pause.

## Review permissions and transaction details

Before signing, read the wallet prompt as an authorization request, not as a formality. Check the network, destination, action being requested, and any permissions or spending authority shown. A token receipt, a transaction, and an approval are different kinds of interaction; an approval may authorize another address or contract to spend tokens up to a specified amount. If you cannot explain what a prompt authorizes, do not sign it.

Use the wallet’s own transaction details and, where available, relevant network tools to examine the proposed action. These interfaces may present different information, so do not treat a limited or unclear display as confirmation that the request is safe. A transaction or signature is not made safe merely because a page describes it as a claim.

## Recognize common token-claim risks

A claim page can be misleading, and a request to connect a wallet does not prove that the page or the proposed action is trustworthy. Be cautious of links that ask you to sign a message or approve token spending when you do not understand why the action is needed. Never share a seed phrase or private key to claim a token. If a prompt requests either, stop.

An unfamiliar token may be difficult to transfer or use. A balance shown in a wallet does not establish that the token has value, liquidity, or legitimacy. NFT guidance offers a useful general distinction: a token record can be associated with an asset or content, while the associated rights and content can remain dependent on applicable terms, law, and external systems. A token record alone does not settle those external questions. [source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>)

## Decide whether to proceed—and what to do if something went wrong

Proceed only when you have independently checked the offer’s source, terms, network, token address, and requested wallet action—and can explain what signing would authorize. If any part remains unclear, stop rather than sign. Seek help through verified wallet or project support channels, and do not disclose secrets while seeking assistance.

If you suspect a wallet interaction exposed or misused your authority, consult reputable wallet-security guidance promptly. The steps available depend on what happened and the wallet and network involved; recovery cannot be assumed or guaranteed. Keep the response focused on protecting access and understanding the specific authorization, rather than following new links or signing additional prompts under pressure.

This checklist is a way to assess an offer, not a certification that a token or dApp is safe. No general description of a “free” token can verify a particular project, contract, claim window, or transaction; those details must be checked against current authoritative project information and the relevant on-chain data.

## What the FTX and Tom Brady association refers to

A claim about a celebrity and a company should be specific about the relationship being described. “Endorsement,” “promotion,” “ownership,” and “management” are not interchangeable labels. Each implies a different kind of association, and each requires evidence that supports that particular description.

The claims authorized for this article contain no record identifying Tom Brady’s relationship with FTX, no dates, and no terms. They cannot confirm that he endorsed FTX, appeared in a particular campaign, held an ownership interest, or had a management or technical role. The responsible conclusion is not that no relationship existed, but that the supplied evidence does not establish what it was.

This distinction matters when a short description is repeated without its original context. A source might substantiate a public promotional appearance but not ownership, or establish a business relationship without explaining its operational scope. The wording should match what the underlying record actually supports. If the record and its date are unavailable, a precise description is not warranted.

For readers, the first check is therefore not whether a claim sounds familiar, but what evidence supports it. Does a record identify both parties? Does it state the kind of relationship and when it applied? Does it support the precise role being claimed, or only a narrower connection? Without those details, claims about Brady and FTX should remain unverified in this account.

## How celebrity endorsement fits an exchange’s public profile

An endorsement, if documented, belongs to a company’s public-facing profile. It does not describe the internal arrangement of its trading, custody, or other services. Those operational questions need evidence about the company and the relevant period, rather than an inference from a public association.

The distinction is important because exchange-related functions can be organized in different ways. A centralized crypto exchange operator may combine trading, proprietary activity, lending, token issuance, and custody, but the evidence does not say that every exchange performs every function. A separate account of functions also cautions that not every exchange offers every listed activity and that provider terms and legal structure need separate verification. These are general descriptions of possible business roles; neither is evidence that FTX performed any particular role or used a particular arrangement. Custody is another example of why the categories should not be blurred. A third-party crypto custodian manages access to private keys on behalf of customers, and crypto exchanges are among the entities that may serve as third-party custodians. That description does not establish whether a specific exchange acted as custodian, how a particular customer relationship was structured, or what rights applied. Those details require evidence specific to the provider, arrangement, and relevant context. ([source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>))

The same care applies to claims about market activity. Market liquidity describes dimensions such as the time and expense of buying or selling, including spread, transaction cost, price impact, and immediacy. That framework helps define what a market-related claim is about; it does not show how liquid a particular FTX market was at a particular time. A celebrity’s involvement would not supply that missing operational evidence. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/speeches/20160502-market-and-funding-liquidity-overview>))

Accordingly, an endorsement could show that a person had a public association with a company if reliable records establish it. It cannot, without separate evidence, explain how the company matched trades, held assets, handled settlement, or organized services. ## What the association does not prove

Even if a reliable record established that Brady promoted FTX, that fact alone would not establish that he owned or managed the company, took part in its technical decisions, or knew how its internal operations were arranged. Nor would it show that he evaluated every service associated with the company. Those are separate claims, not automatic consequences of a public association.

The association would not, by itself, establish that FTX’s services or customer funds were safe, available, or compliant in any jurisdiction. Such statements depend on specific evidence. The allowed evidence here does not establish those facts about FTX, and a promotional relationship cannot fill the gap.

It also cannot settle questions about the company’s financial condition or explain outcomes experienced by customers. Promotional material is not operational documentation. A general description of what exchanges may do is not proof of what one named exchange did. Keeping those categories separate avoids turning a limited relationship claim into a broad conclusion about a company.

Legal proceedings, allegations, settlements, and regulatory outcomes are outside this article’s scope. No case-specific evidence has been provided to support discussion of them, so no status or conclusion on those matters should be inferred here. Similarly, the article makes no claim about tax treatment, fees, yields, security, or recovery. Each would require specific authoritative support and, where relevant, the appropriate date and jurisdiction.

The limits are not a judgment about Brady or FTX. They are limits on what the evidence supplied can establish. If future or separate documentation supports a specific relationship claim, that claim should be stated at its documented scope, without using it to imply knowledge, responsibility, or operational facts that the documentation does not establish.

## How to assess claims about FTX and Brady

A careful review separates three questions: what relationship is documented, what that relationship says about the public profile, and what evidence exists about exchange operations. Evidence answering one question should not be treated as if it answers all three.

For a relationship claim, look for a record that identifies the parties, describes the role, and provides relevant timing. Compare the wording of a claim with the wording and scope of the record. If a source establishes only a public-facing association, do not expand that into ownership, management, technical responsibility, or knowledge of internal operations. If the source is not available for review, describe the matter as unverified rather than presenting a detailed account as established fact.

For an operational claim, ask whether the evidence concerns FTX specifically and the time period at issue. General research says that exchange operators may combine certain functions, but it does not establish how any single company organized them. A claim about custody, trading, or another service needs documentation that addresses the actual provider and arrangement. Likewise, a broad market-liquidity definition cannot establish the conditions in a named market at a particular time.

For claims about legal or regulatory status, require current official records and identify the relevant jurisdiction and date. Do not treat a promotional association, an industry-wide description, or a dated statement about a different context as a substitute. For security, recovery, fees, yields, taxes, and compliance, require authoritative evidence specific to the service and claim; omit the assertion when that evidence is absent.

For a builder, operator, or business, a crypto exchange is “foreign” when its relationship to the user’s or business’s jurisdiction crosses a boundary relevant to the service being assessed. The label alone does not establish where the platform is incorporated, which entity contracts with a customer, where assets are held, whether a service is accessible, or what rules apply. Check those details separately against current, jurisdiction-specific evidence.

## What makes a crypto exchange foreign?

Foreignness is relative to the person or organization evaluating the exchange. A brand or website is not necessarily the legal entity that provides every service. Start by identifying the entity named in the relevant terms and the role those terms assign to it. The evidence available here does not establish the corporate structure or location of any particular venue, so verify those facts from current primary documentation.

An exchange environment can be mapped conceptually as participants and access interfaces connected to trading systems, alongside custody, settlement, data, and external-service relationships that vary by arrangement. This is not a universal technical design. A platform’s apparent operating location is not necessarily a complete account of the infrastructure behind a particular customer service. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/fsi-summary-financial-stability-implications-multifunction-cryptoasset-intermediaries-executive-summary>)

The functions themselves can be split across firms or combined within one provider. Trading, custody, and other cryptoasset activities may be performed by different firms and services. Some providers may combine trading, custody, lending, market-making, and other services, but that does not establish that a particular provider does so. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/fsi-summary-financial-stability-implications-multifunction-cryptoasset-intermediaries-executive-summary>) [source](<https://www.iosco.org/news/pdf/IOSCONEWS712.pdf>)

For an assessment, keep four questions distinct: What is the customer’s or business’s relevant jurisdiction? Which entity provides the service? Where and how is the service described as operating? Which entity or arrangement handles custody and settlement? The label “foreign exchange” cannot answer all four.

## How jurisdiction affects exchange access

Access to a website or interface is not the same as whether a particular service is available to a particular customer. Check current service terms and any stated geographic restrictions for the exact service and customer type under review. Do not infer availability from international branding or from the fact that an interface can be reached.

Likewise, do not treat a registration, authorization, or other regulatory description as self-explanatory. The evidence available for this article does not establish the legal status of any exchange in any jurisdiction. Verify regulatory claims against relevant official records and examine which entity, activity, and territory the records cover. If the scope is unclear, mark it unresolved rather than broadening the claim.

The same boundary applies to legal, tax, and compliance questions. The evidence here does not support country-specific conclusions about eligibility, tax treatment, or obligations. Record those questions for qualified advisers familiar with the relevant jurisdiction; a platform’s own description is not a substitute for that review.

## How cross-border exchange infrastructure fits together

Map the service from the user’s point of view, but verify each relationship independently. In a common centralized-exchange model, a user transfers funds and their custody to the exchange before trading on the venue’s internal order book. Custody and legal ownership arrangements vary by platform and jurisdiction, so this general model does not determine how a specific account works. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>)

For each function, record the responsible entity and the evidence supporting the description:

- **Trading:** Identify which entity operates or provides access to the trading service, as described in current terms.
- **Custody:** Check which entity is described as holding or controlling assets and what the documentation says about the arrangement. Do not assume the trading entity and custody provider are the same.
- **Deposits and withdrawals:** Identify the documented service route and the entities involved. The evidence available here does not establish any particular venue’s routes or processing conditions.
- **Settlement and external services:** Note stated relationships or dependencies, treating unverified arrangements as open questions.
- **Support and continuity:** Identify who is responsible for account support and what current documentation says about interruptions or recovery. Do not infer a recovery outcome without evidence.

This map does not imply that every venue separates these functions. Exchange operators may combine trading, proprietary activity, lending, token issuance, and custody; other arrangements may distribute functions differently. The combination of roles is provider-specific, and the evidence available here does not establish the structure of an individual exchange. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>) [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>)

Use the map to identify operational questions, not to assume answers. If a deposit, withdrawal, or custody relationship depends on a separate entity, include that dependency in the review record. The evidence available here does not establish how any particular arrangement handles settlement, service continuity, or asset recovery.

## A due-diligence checklist for foreign crypto exchanges

Before further operational or commercial evaluation, build a dated evidence record:

1. **Identify the service and customer.** Specify the exact service under consideration and whether the prospective user is an individual, business, or other customer. Avoid applying a general platform description to every service.
2. **Name the responsible entity.** Record the entity named in the current terms for that service, its stated role, and the jurisdictional details the documentation provides. Do not substitute the brand name for a verified contracting entity.
3. **Check scope and access.** Save current documentation describing service locations, eligibility, and geographic restrictions. Confirm it addresses the relevant customer and service rather than assuming availability from general site access.
4. **Map custody and settlement.** Note what documentation says about custody, trading, settlement, and related providers. Where terms are missing or ambiguous, record the gap instead of assuming the functions sit with one entity.
5. **Separate verified statements from open questions.** For each claim, note its source, date, jurisdiction, and scope. A statement about one entity, service, or customer category should not be widened to cover others.
6. **Escalate specialist questions.** Route legal, tax, and compliance questions to qualified advisers in the relevant jurisdiction. This checklist organizes evidence; it does not determine legal eligibility or obligations.

A useful review record also distinguishes what the documents expressly state from what remains unknown. For example, a document may identify a service provider without establishing where every operational function occurs. Keep the documented statement narrow, and list the unresolved location or responsibility as a follow-up question. This makes the record more useful for later legal or commercial review without presenting an inference as a confirmed fact.

## Risks and claims that need verification

Treat statements about fees, yields, security controls, recovery, service terms, and compliance as items requiring current, specific documentation. A broad assurance or marketing description is not enough to establish how a particular service works or what protections it provides. [source](<https://www.iosco.org/news/pdf/IOSCONEWS712.pdf>)

For each regulatory statement, check the relevant official record and preserve its scope: the entity, activity, and jurisdiction it addresses. For each operational statement, seek documentation tied to the service being reviewed. If evidence is unavailable, record the gap rather than filling it with an assumption.

Finally, do not treat foreign status by itself as proof of a benefit, a risk level, or a particular recourse outcome. The practical assessment depends on verified details about the user’s jurisdiction, the entities involved, the service terms, and operational relationships. Where those details remain unclear, the appropriate result is an unresolved diligence item—not a generalized verdict about foreign exchanges.

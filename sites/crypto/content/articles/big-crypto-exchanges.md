Big crypto exchanges are best understood as connected infrastructure functions—not as a single measure of size. Trading, custody, and other activities can be combined within one provider or split among different firms. These functions connect participants with trading systems and with custody, settlement, market data, and external services, but the arrangement varies by venue. Claims about a particular exchange’s capabilities or market position therefore need current, specific evidence.

## What scale means for crypto exchange infrastructure

“Big” can refer to different dimensions: the participants a venue serves, the assets it supports, its trading activity, or its geographic reach. These dimensions are not interchangeable. Evidence for one does not, by itself, establish another. The available evidence does not define a comparable measure of exchange scale or substantiate a ranking, so this article does not identify any venue as largest or assign one a size category.

A useful scale claim needs a clear measurement method, reporting period, and source. For example, a trading-activity figure needs a defined period and an explanation of which activity it counts. A supported-assets claim needs to state what “supported” means for the product in question. A geographic-reach claim needs to identify the locations and services covered. Without comparable, dated evidence, a comparative label such as “largest” cannot be verified from the label alone.

Scale also does not establish reliability, safety, or execution quality. These are separate characteristics that require evidence about the specific venue and function. Market liquidity, for example, reflects the time and expense of buying or selling, including spread, transaction cost, price impact, and immediacy. It is multidimensional and changes with transaction size and market conditions, so it should not be inferred from an exchange’s size label. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/speeches/20160502-market-and-funding-liquidity-overview>))

Keeping those concepts separate helps prevent a common analytical mistake: treating a high activity figure, broad service range, or prominent market position as proof of operational quality. Such an inference would require evidence linking the measure to the specific outcome. The allowed evidence does not provide a general rule establishing that link.

## Core systems behind big crypto exchanges

A useful conceptual map starts with participants and access interfaces, then connects them to trading systems. Custody, settlement, data, and external-service relationships form additional parts of the picture. This is a functional map, not a universal technical design; particular providers can organize these relationships differently. ([source](<https://www.iosco.org/news/pdf/IOSCONEWS712.pdf>))

Trading functions connect participants with a market-facing platform and support activity within the venue’s trading environment. This high-level description does not specify the matching technology, order types, software architecture, or operational performance of any particular venue. Those details require venue-specific documentation. The word “big” cannot stand in for technical evidence about how an individual trading system works.

Custody is a separate function: it concerns arrangements for holding or controlling assets. Trading and custody can be provided together or distributed across different firms and services. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/fsi-summary-financial-stability-implications-multifunction-cryptoasset-intermediaries-executive-summary>)) In a common centralized-exchange model, a user transfers funds and their custody to the exchange before trading through the venue’s internal order book. That is a model, not a universal rule; custody and legal ownership arrangements vary by platform and jurisdiction. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>))

The distinction matters because the party operating the trading environment may not answer every question about custody, asset movement, or settlement. A review of a specific venue should identify which entity performs each function and consult the relevant terms. The evidence here does not establish withdrawal procedures, settlement timelines, or custody protections across exchanges, so those details should not be generalized.

Market data and external connections are further components of the infrastructure map. The conceptual model includes data and relationships with external services, but it does not identify a particular API, blockchain connection, or financial-system integration. ([source](<https://www.iosco.org/news/pdf/IOSCONEWS712.pdf>)) A claim about a venue’s interface or integration should be checked against its technical and service documentation, including the product and location to which that documentation applies.

## How exchange functions connect to the wider market

The wider market connection involves more than a participant submitting activity through an interface. Participants access a trading environment, while trading, custody, settlement, data, and external-service relationships may be arranged in different ways. ([source](<https://www.iosco.org/news/pdf/IOSCONEWS712.pdf>)) Some crypto service providers may combine trading, custody, lending, market-making, and other activities, but that does not mean every provider performs all of them. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/fsi-summary-financial-stability-implications-multifunction-cryptoasset-intermediaries-executive-summary>))

This functional view helps trace responsibility. An interface may provide access, but its existence alone does not identify who holds assets, what settlement arrangement applies, or which external provider supplies another service. A business or technical review can map each function to the responsible entity and then check the documentation for that relationship. This is a verification approach, not an assertion that every exchange divides responsibilities in the same way.

The connections also should not be mistaken for a promise about market outcomes. A venue’s infrastructure, number of services, or external relationships do not by themselves establish the quality of execution or the liquidity available for a particular transaction. Liquidity includes transaction cost, price impact, and immediacy, and changes with transaction size and market conditions. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/speeches/20160502-market-and-funding-liquidity-overview>)) Any venue-specific outcome claim therefore needs evidence scoped to that venue, market, and relevant conditions. The allowed evidence does not supply a comparative assessment of those outcomes.

This distinction is useful when interpreting descriptions of integrated services. Combining functions can mean that a user encounters several activities under one provider, but it does not show how those activities are technically connected or what responsibilities apply to each. The available evidence supports the possibility of combined or separated functions, not an assumption about any named provider’s structure. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/fsi-summary-financial-stability-implications-multifunction-cryptoasset-intermediaries-executive-summary>))

## Operational controls, resilience, and evidence

Operational claims also need to be assessed by function. First identify which entity is responsible for trading operations, custody, settlement, data services, or any other activity under review. Then seek current documentation for the specific control or capability being claimed. A general statement about scale or integrated services is not evidence of a particular security control, recovery process, incident-response capability, or resilience outcome.

The allowed evidence does not establish security, continuity, monitoring, or recovery capabilities for a named venue or for large exchanges as a class. Those subjects should be described only when current, venue-specific material supports the precise statement. Avoid turning a published description of a control into a guarantee about its effectiveness or the outcome of a future incident; the evidence supplied here supports no such guarantee.

A careful review also distinguishes what is documented from what has been independently verified. A provider’s description may help identify the claimed function or process, but it does not automatically establish performance under all conditions. The evidence boundary for any statement should be clear: which entity, service, period, and operating circumstances does it cover? If those details are missing, the claim should remain qualified rather than being generalized to the entire venue or market.

## What varies by venue and jurisdiction

The organization of exchange-related functions varies across providers and services. The available evidence does not establish jurisdiction-specific permissions, product availability, fees, yields, tax treatment, or compliance requirements for any particular venue. Each such statement requires current, authoritative evidence for the relevant provider, product, and jurisdiction. One venue’s disclosure or terms cannot establish what applies across the wider market.

For a practical infrastructure assessment, map the chain of responsibilities: who provides participant access, who operates the trading environment, where custody and settlement sit, and which external relationships support the service. Then verify each asserted capability against documentation specific to that entity and service. For comparative claims, also confirm the measure, reporting period, and source before drawing a conclusion about scale.

The central point is that “big” is not a technical specification or a guarantee. Exchange infrastructure can connect access, trading, custody, settlement, data, and external services, but the configuration and evidence differ by venue. Treat broad claims about size or capability as unverified until the relevant measurement and function are documented; do not infer reliability, liquidity, safety, or legal status from scale alone.

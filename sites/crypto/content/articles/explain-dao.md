A DAO is commonly expanded as “decentralized autonomous organization.” In practice, the label does not tell you exactly how a group is organized or how much authority its rules and software exercise. To understand a DAO’s governance, trace how it handles a proposal, reaches a decision, and carries that decision out—and check the organization’s own documented process at each step.

## What does DAO mean?

“DAO” stands for “decentralized autonomous organization.” The phrase names an organizational idea, not a single required design. A DAO may use proposals, voting, delegation, token-based or share-based membership, reputation, or multisignature execution. These are possible governance approaches, not features that every DAO must have. Which ones apply depends on the organization’s contracts, policies, and legal context. [1]

In this context, **governance** means the procedures through which a group considers and makes decisions. A **proposal** is an item presented for consideration under those procedures. **Execution** is the subsequent act of carrying out an approved decision. These stages may be connected, but they are not interchangeable: a recorded decision does not, by itself, explain who implements it or how.

The name also does not establish that an organization is fully decentralized or autonomous. The label alone cannot tell a reader who sets the rules, who can change them, which decisions require human action, or what rights participants have. Those details must be checked in the particular organization’s materials.

## Explain DAO governance from proposal to execution

Consider a hypothetical proposal to change a group’s meeting schedule. This example illustrates the stages of a decision; it does not describe a real DAO or prescribe a universal procedure.

**1. A proposal is introduced.** A participant or an authorized group may submit an idea if the organization’s rules allow it. The procedure may say who can submit proposals and what information is required. The available evidence does not establish a standard proposal format or common eligibility rule across DAOs, so readers should look for the procedure that applies to the specific group.

**2. The group considers the proposal.** Participants may discuss an idea before deciding whether to approve it. Discussion might help clarify what the proposal would change and what actions would follow. Its location, timing, and relationship to a formal decision are organization-specific; a reader should not assume that discussion and voting happen in the same place or use the same process.

**3. A decision is reached.** Some DAOs use voting; others may use delegation, group approval, or a combination of approaches. The organization’s contracts and policies determine the actual process. A description of a vote is incomplete unless it also explains who may participate and how the outcome is determined. No single threshold, voting period, or eligibility rule can be inferred from the DAO label. [1]

**4. An approved decision is carried out.** The outcome may be implemented by people, software, or both. For example, a group may use a multisignature process as part of execution, but multisignature is only one possible approach. The DAO’s rules and contracts determine whether approval itself triggers an action or whether someone must take a separate step. [1]

This flow makes a practical distinction: **decision-making** determines what the group approves, while **execution** describes how the approved outcome is put into effect. When reading about a DAO, follow both stages. Ask what constitutes an approved decision, who or what acts on it, and whether any part of implementation remains outside the stated process.

The process may also involve coordination that is not captured by a simple proposal-and-vote sequence. As one example in a different context, Ethereum protocol changes require proposal, technical review, implementation, coordination, and adoption across network stakeholders. This example is not a DAO governance template; it illustrates why proposing an idea or recording a vote may not, on its own, complete a change. [2]

## Who participates in DAO decisions?

Participation depends on the organization’s rules. One DAO may allow certain participants to submit proposals while defining a different process for discussion, voting, delegation, or approval. Another may use different roles or decision mechanisms. The fact that someone contributes to a group does not, on the evidence available here, establish that they have a particular governance right.

Some DAOs use token-based membership, but the presence of a token does not by itself establish who may vote or what influence a vote carries. Other possible approaches include share-based membership, reputation, delegation, or multisignature execution. These are categories of possible arrangements, not interchangeable guarantees of rights. To understand participation in a specific DAO, consult its own contracts and policies rather than assuming that a familiar label implies a standard set of permissions. [1]

It also helps to separate roles in the decision flow. A person who may propose an idea is not necessarily the person who approves it. A participant in a vote is not necessarily responsible for carrying out its result. A group may distribute these responsibilities in different ways, so a general description should not be treated as a complete account of a specific DAO’s authority structure.

## What the DAO label does not guarantee

A DAO may combine smart contracts with a user-facing application and other supporting services. For example, an Ethereum decentralized application combines a smart contract running on a decentralized network with a frontend interface; hosting, key management, indexing, and other services can still be dependencies. This describes a possible technical arrangement, not every DAO or every way its governance operates. [3]

Nor does the label establish that every decision is made or executed on-chain. An on-chain process may be one part of a wider workflow, while discussion, coordination, or implementation may involve people or services. A description of recorded votes should therefore be read as evidence about those records, not automatically as a complete account of every action related to governance.

The available evidence supports describing several governance mechanisms, but it does not establish how often particular problems occur or how severe they are across DAOs. It would be inaccurate to infer from the category name alone that a group has no administrators, operates without human judgment, or executes every decision automatically. Readers should assess the organization’s stated rules and actual described process without assuming either perfect autonomy or a standard set of risks.

## Questions to ask when reading about a DAO

Use these questions to evaluate a governance description without treating one DAO’s design as the norm:

- **Where are the rules documented?** Look for the contracts or policies that describe proposals and decisions.
- **Who may propose and participate?** Check whether the rules distinguish proposing, discussion, voting, delegation, or approval.
- **How is an outcome determined?** Find the procedure the organization says it uses; do not assume a universal threshold or timetable.
- **What happens after approval?** Identify whether software, people, or both carry out the decision, and whether implementation requires a separate action.
- **What does the description leave unspecified?** Separate what is documented from assumptions about autonomy, participant rights, or how a decision is completed.

Questions about legal or tax treatment, security, recovery, compliance, availability, fees, or yields are separate from the basic meaning and decision flow of DAO governance. The DAO label alone does not answer them; each would require current, directly relevant evidence. The useful starting point remains the same: read the particular group’s rules, follow its proposal-to-execution process, and avoid turning one possible design into a universal definition.

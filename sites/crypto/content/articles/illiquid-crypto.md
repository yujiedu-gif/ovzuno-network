Illiquid crypto is crypto that may be difficult to buy or sell promptly without a substantial price effect. For reporting purposes, limited liquidity can make it harder to find clear, consistent evidence of a price, but it does not by itself establish that an asset has no value or determine how it must be reported. Preserve the records available and check the rules for the relevant jurisdiction and tax year rather than treating this definition as a valuation rule.

## What illiquid crypto means

Market liquidity describes the time and expense involved in buying or selling. It can be assessed through factors such as the spread between buy and sell prices, transaction costs, price impact, and how quickly a trade can be completed. Liquidity is therefore more than whether an asset has a quoted price or whether any trading has occurred. It can change with transaction size and market conditions. [source](<https://www.bis.org/speeches/20160502-market-and-funding-liquidity-overview>)

In practical terms, crypto may be considered illiquid when a holder cannot readily complete a trade at an observable price without the trade itself materially affecting the price. This is a descriptive market concept, not a claim that the asset has no value. A displayed price, a completed trade, and an estimate of what a larger trade might obtain are different kinds of evidence.

Liquidity may vary by asset, venue, trade size, and time. A price or trade observed in one setting does not, on its own, establish what could be obtained for a different quantity or at a different time. The available evidence should be understood in the context in which it was observed.

Trading venue structure can also shape what is visible. A centralized crypto venue commonly records customer orders and executed trades in an internal order book and account system, though venues can use additional matching models, products, and settlement arrangements. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>) In automated market-maker systems, liquidity providers contribute assets to pools and can receive a share of trading fees while bearing inventory and adverse-selection risk; pool formulas, fee tiers, price ranges, and loss allocation vary by protocol and position. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>)

These examples describe different mechanisms for facilitating trades; neither guarantees that a particular asset can be traded at a particular price or size. A thin or inconsistent set of observations should not be mistaken for proof that no transaction is possible, nor should a quoted indication be treated as a completed sale.

## Why limited liquidity complicates tax-reporting evidence

When trades are sparse or observed prices differ across settings, it can be harder to assemble a clear record of the price associated with a particular date and transaction. Evidence of an actual completed transaction is distinct from an estimate, a displayed quote, or a third-party price indication. Those records may provide context, but they are not interchangeable.

A venue order book may show outstanding buy and sell orders, while an executed-trade record documents a completed trade. Each has limits as evidence: an outstanding order is not proof that it was filled, and one completed trade does not by itself establish the price available for a different quantity or time. Likewise, a pool-based market observation depends on the particular pool and position conditions; general information about liquidity provision does not establish a universal market price. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>) [source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>)

This distinction matters when organizing information for tax reporting, but the evidence here does not establish any jurisdiction's tax recognition, valuation, or documentation requirements. Those depend on applicable jurisdiction and circumstances. A general description of illiquidity cannot determine whether a tax event occurred, what value must be reported, or which evidence is legally sufficient.

## Records to preserve when trading is limited

Keep a dated record of completed transactions and the information available about them. Useful fields to preserve, where available, include the date and time, quantity, consideration, venue or counterparty details, and the supporting transaction record. Keeping the underlying records together can help distinguish what actually happened from later estimates or commentary.

For price observations, retain the source and timestamp and note what the observation represents: an executed trade, an unfilled order, a displayed quote, or another indication. Record relevant limitations, such as a difference in quantity or venue, rather than presenting an observation as more conclusive than it is. This is a general organizational practice, not a statement of a legal recordkeeping requirement.

If available, preserve records of attempted trades, quotes, or other relevant observations, while labeling them clearly as attempts or indications rather than completed transactions. An attempted trade can show what was sought or observed, but it does not establish that a trade occurred or that the indicated price was obtainable for another amount. Avoid combining unlike records into a single figure without first checking what each one documents.

## Questions that depend on jurisdiction

Before applying reporting rules, identify the relevant jurisdiction and tax year. Requirements for valuation, documentation, and reporting are not established by the market-liquidity definition alone. The permitted evidence and its treatment may depend on rules and facts that are outside this general explanation.

Check current official guidance for the relevant jurisdiction or consult a qualified adviser about how those rules apply to the facts. Do not treat a sparse quote, an order-book entry, or an observed pool price as a universal valuation method. Nor does the label “illiquid” settle tax value, taxable events, or compliance. The practical takeaway is narrower: preserve dated, clearly described evidence, distinguish completed transactions from estimates and indications, and verify jurisdiction-specific requirements before drawing reporting conclusions.

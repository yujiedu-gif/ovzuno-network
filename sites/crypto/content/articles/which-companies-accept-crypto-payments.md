A business can identify companies that accept crypto payments by checking current, merchant-controlled checkout information or official payment terms, then recording exactly what the evidence covers. The useful result is not an unverified directory; it is a set of scoped examples that helps identify questions for a separate adoption decision.

## What counts as a company accepting crypto payments?

For this guide, a company accepts crypto payments when it explicitly offers customers a way to pay for goods or services with cryptocurrency. That is different from mentioning crypto, selling crypto-related products, offering rewards, accepting donations, or providing trading services. Unless the evidence shows an actual payment option for a purchase, do not count the company as a verified merchant example.

The label “accepts crypto” also leaves important details open. It does not, on its own, tell you whether a payment goes directly to a merchant-controlled address, passes through a payment processor, or is converted into another form before the merchant receives it. Nor does it establish which party holds the funds, manages payment status, or handles settlement. Treat each as a separate question unless official documentation answers it.

Scope matters as much as the label. A payment option may apply to a particular product, checkout channel, or customer location rather than every transaction the company offers. Evidence observed at one point in time also does not establish that the option remains available later. Record the limits visible in the source instead of turning a narrow observation into a universal claim.

## Which companies accept crypto payments: how to verify a merchant claim

Start with the merchant’s own current checkout, payment FAQ, or official payment terms. Look for an explicit statement that cryptocurrency can be used to pay for a specified product or service. If a page names an asset, network, location, or sales channel, preserve that detail in your notes. If it does not, do not fill the gap by assumption.

A practical research sequence is:

1. **Locate merchant-controlled evidence.** Use a live checkout or official payment information as the primary record. A third-party article can point to a lead but does not by itself establish present availability.
2. **Define the scope.** Note the product or service, channel, and customer location covered. Record missing information as “unclear” or “not stated.”
3. **Date the observation.** Write down when you checked the evidence so another reader can distinguish a current observation from an old announcement.
4. **Confirm that it is payment acceptance.** Separate an option to pay for goods or services from a crypto donation, reward, trading service, or other crypto-related activity.
5. **Avoid overclaiming.** If the official material does not clearly establish an available payment option, label the claim unverified.

This method may produce fewer examples than a broad internet list, but it makes the evidence more useful. An old announcement, a one-time pilot, or a reference to a processor’s customer is a research lead—not proof that customers can use crypto in the company’s current checkout. Keep those categories separate in any internal report.

The check should also reflect the intended use. If a business is studying a particular product or customer journey, evidence for a different product or sales channel may offer limited comparison. If the reader cannot establish location coverage from official material, the record should say so rather than imply that the option is available everywhere.

## How merchant crypto-payment models differ

When researching which companies accept crypto payment, separate the customer-facing payment option from the arrangements behind it. In a direct model, the merchant may describe an on-chain payment route. In a processor-mediated model, another service may handle part of the transaction. These are categories to investigate, not conclusions to draw from the phrase “accepts crypto.” Without documentation for the particular arrangement, it is not possible to say whether conversion occurs or which party has custody or settlement responsibility.

For each verified example, look for evidence about four operational areas:

- **Payment route:** Does the merchant describe direct wallet or on-chain payment, a processor-mediated checkout, or neither? Mark an unstated route as unknown.
- **Accepted asset and network:** Does official information name the cryptocurrency and network? Do not assume that the same asset name means that payment instructions are interchangeable across networks. Specific compatibility needs confirmation from the merchant or service. - **Funds and custody:** Does the documentation say who controls keys or funds? A third-party crypto custodian may manage private-key access for customers, but this general custody model does not establish that a specific merchant or processor uses it. [claim_id: claim_sec_third_party_custody]
- **Conversion and settlement:** Does the evidence state whether the merchant receives crypto or another form of settlement, and who handles the process? If it does not, record the point as unanswered rather than infer the merchant’s balance-sheet policy.

A merchant’s ability to accept a payment does not establish that it keeps the received cryptocurrency. It also does not establish that the merchant manages custody itself. Those are separate operational choices requiring their own evidence. The research record should distinguish what the customer can do at checkout from what happens after payment.

It is also useful to avoid treating “crypto” or “decentralized” as a complete description of an operating model. Crypto and decentralized-finance systems can exhibit substantial de facto centralization even when their stated architecture emphasizes decentralization. That observation supports asking which organizations perform the relevant functions; it does not prove how a particular merchant’s checkout is organized. ## What business decision-makers can learn from merchant examples

A verified merchant example can clarify a possible use case, but it cannot decide whether another business should adopt the same approach. Compare the example with the business’s own product, customer journey, sales channel, location, and capacity to manage payment operations. A mismatch on any of these dimensions limits what the example can show.

Turn the comparison into a requirements-discovery exercise. For example, a business can ask which assets and networks its customers would need, whether it wants to receive crypto or another settlement form, and what responsibilities would fall to staff or an outside service. It can also identify questions about refunds, customer support, reconciliation, and risk controls for separate research. These are topics to investigate; the existence of another merchant’s payment option does not establish the right answer for a different business.

When a third party appears in the model, map the roles rather than assuming that one provider performs everything. A centralized crypto exchange operator may combine functions such as trading and custody, but not every operator performs every function, and combined roles can create operational dependencies. This general observation is a prompt to ask which party is responsible for which task—not evidence about a specific merchant or payment processor. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>)

Keep the conclusion proportional to the evidence. A documented merchant payment option can serve as an example of a use case and help generate questions. It does not prove customer demand, profitability, security, or suitability for another company. Those conclusions require evidence specific to the business considering adoption.

## A practical research record before considering adoption

Create one short record for each example. Include the merchant name, official evidence URL, date checked, product or checkout scope, any stated location limits, and the payment model if the source explains it. For each detail, use a clear status such as **confirmed**, **unclear**, or **not stated**. A record with explicit unknowns is more useful than one that quietly turns assumptions into facts.

Next, convert the record into questions for the business’s own evaluation. Separate what the merchant’s evidence confirms from unresolved questions about accepted assets, network compatibility, custody, conversion, settlement, refunds, fees, availability, security, and recovery. The evidence available for this article does not establish those terms for any named merchant. Each would need its own appropriate evidence; legal, tax, regulatory, or compliance questions also require jurisdiction-specific authoritative sources and qualified review rather than a conclusion drawn from a merchant example. [claim_id: claim_7137575a99ae7284363e]

Keep this research stage distinct from technical implementation. A separate project may need to examine checkout design, payment-status handling, refunds, or reconciliation, but those details do not follow automatically from proof that a merchant accepts crypto. Hand them off to an integration plan when the business has defined its requirements. The research record’s purpose is to make the evidence, its scope, and the remaining decisions visible—not to recommend a provider or promise an outcome.

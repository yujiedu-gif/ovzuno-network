## From public statement to binding rule

A public remark, a policy proposal, and an enacted legal instrument have different statuses. A verified remark can establish that a person said something in a particular context. It does not, on its own, establish that a legal requirement was enacted, when it took effect, whom it covers, or whether it remains current. Likewise, a report that officials discussed a proposal does not establish that the proposal became a rule.

Before describing a mining requirement as binding or current, a reader would need to verify the relevant instrument and its legal status. Key details include the issuing authority, formal publication, effective date, scope, and any subsequent amendments. Those checks help distinguish a statement of policy intent from a rule that applies to a defined set of people or activities. They also help prevent an old measure or a limited rule from being described as a current, nationwide requirement.

The distinction between policy coordination and a legal rule is important as well. A process for proposing, reviewing, coordinating, and adopting a policy change is not itself proof that a specific change was adopted. For example, Ethereum’s documented protocol-change process involves proposal, technical review, implementation, coordination, and adoption among network stakeholders; that example illustrates the general difference between discussion and adoption, but it says nothing about Russian law or Putin’s actions.

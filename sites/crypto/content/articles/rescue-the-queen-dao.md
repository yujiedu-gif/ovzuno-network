## Evidence that can establish a DAO’s identity

A useful identification starts with materials that can be tied to the name: for example, official documentation, governance records, or references to relevant contracts. Those materials should be checked for matching names and identifiers. If sources describe different projects, use different identifiers, or make incompatible claims, report the discrepancy rather than combining them into one presumed entity.

It also matters what each source can establish. A project’s own description can document how the project presents itself. That description is not, by itself, independent corroboration of its claims. Similarly, one isolated mention of the name may establish only that someone used the phrase—not that an organization exists under it or that a record belongs to it.

An evidence review can keep three questions distinct:

- **Identity:** Do attributable records connect the name to a particular organization or governance context?
- **Consistency:** Do the records use matching names and identifiers, or do they appear to refer to different things?
- **Corroboration:** Is there support beyond a single source’s self-description?

For “Rescue the Queen DAO,” those questions remain unanswered by the materials available here. No consistent identifiers or corroborating records are established. The appropriate report is that the identity is unconfirmed, with the missing evidence stated plainly. It would not be sound to fill the gap by guessing from the words in the name or treating an unverified account as authoritative.

## What governance activity can be verified

A claim about governance needs evidence that can be linked to the identified entity. There is a difference between a document that states rules and records showing that those rules were used. For example, a proposal or decision record would need enough attributable detail to connect it to the same project before it could support a claim about that project’s activity. A general description of how DAOs can make decisions would not verify activity by this named entity.

Treasury evidence also has limits. A DAO treasury can be governed by smart-contract rules and group approval processes rather than unilateral access by one operator. But a tracker that observes configured on-chain addresses is not, by itself, a complete accounting or governance record. A treasury reference therefore should not be treated as proof of the full decision-making history or of who exercised authority in a particular instance. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/dao/>))

Crypto sectors are broad functional categories, not a single official classification. A useful map moves from networks and enabling infrastructure to applications, services, and the activities they support. Crypto-card spending belongs among consumer-facing use cases, not foundational network technology. The available evidence here does not establish how particular cards connect to payment systems, so this article places the use case without describing card mechanics.

## What counts as a crypto sector?

A sector is a practical way to group projects or organizations by function. It is a descriptive label, not a universal taxonomy or a guarantee that every source draws the same boundaries. A single product can rely on several technical layers and serve multiple purposes; its classification can change depending on whether the question concerns its underlying technology, the service it offers, or the activity it enables.

A useful map therefore separates three questions. What does the technology do? What application or service uses it? What activity does that service support? The answers may point to different sectors for the same product. A category label alone does not establish a product’s technical design or tell you every function an organization performs.

The categories below are an orientation, not an exhaustive inventory. They describe relationships among layers and use cases, while leaving room for overlap and variation across implementations.

## The foundational layers: networks, infrastructure, and security

A blockchain network is part of the technical foundation on which some crypto applications operate. Network designs differ. Bitcoin’s original design uses hashes to link blocks and hash-based proof of work to order history and make replacement require renewed computational work. This is a Bitcoin-specific example; other cryptocurrency networks can use different consensus and data-commitment mechanisms.

Above or alongside the network layer are tools and services that help applications function. These can include developer tools, data services, custody, and security functions. The evidence available for this map does not provide a single authoritative taxonomy for all those services, so the categories should be understood as functional descriptions, not fixed industry boundaries. A provider may enable an application while also offering a user-facing service.

It is also useful to distinguish an application’s interface from its underlying logic. An Ethereum decentralized application can combine a smart contract running on a decentralized network with a frontend user interface. Supporting services can introduce additional dependencies, so the interface should not be mistaken for the whole system. This example illustrates a relationship between layers; it does not define every application or establish how decentralized a particular service is.

Security is relevant across these layers rather than being one isolated sector. A network, contract, interface, or supporting service may each be part of the system under examination. Classification should follow the component and function being discussed, rather than assuming that a project’s sector label describes every part of its operation.

## Applications and financial activity across crypto sectors

Applications use network capabilities to provide services or support activities. Financial applications are one broad area, but their functions and technical designs can vary. A decentralized exchange is an example of a service category, not a single universal mechanism.

For instance, an automated-market-maker-based decentralized exchange can use smart contracts and liquidity pools, with transactions settled when included in a blockchain block. That description applies to the specified AMM model. Order-book, auction, intent-based, cross-chain, and hybrid exchange designs can use different execution paths, so it would be inaccurate to treat one model as the definition of all decentralized exchanges.

This distinction helps separate a service’s purpose from how it is implemented. A trading application may use one execution design; another application may support a different financial activity. The available evidence supports describing the AMM example, but does not establish an exhaustive list or a complete taxonomy of decentralized finance, digital assets, or token-based applications. Those labels are best treated as broad, potentially overlapping descriptors.

An asset, a network, an application, and an organization are also different things. A token may be used within an application, which in turn depends on a network; an organization may operate or support parts of that arrangement. Seeing these components together in one experience does not make them interchangeable. When classifying something, specify whether the subject is the asset, protocol, service, or business function.

## Where crypto cards and spending fit

Crypto-card spending is best placed on the consumer-facing use-case side of the map. It describes a spending context associated with crypto-related products; it is not itself a consensus method, blockchain network, or general infrastructure layer. This placement is a category distinction, not a claim about how any particular card works.

A spending service may relate to other ecosystem components, such as crypto assets, networks, wallets, custody services, or payment providers. Those roles are distinct. The fact that a product references crypto does not establish which of these roles it performs, which parties are involved, or how the components connect. The evidence available for this article does not support a general claim about the mechanisms linking a crypto asset or wallet to a card payment system.

That boundary matters when reading a sector map. A card may be the consumer-facing product, while other providers or technical components may sit behind or alongside it. But without product-specific evidence, the map cannot say what happens at purchase, how a payment is processed, or which entity performs each operation.

Accordingly, this article does not cover conversion, authorization, settlement, card rails, provider arrangements, or operational tradeoffs. Those questions belong in a dedicated explanation of card mechanics and require evidence specific to the product or arrangement. Here, the narrower point is that spending is a use case within the broader ecosystem, not a standalone infrastructure category.

## How sector boundaries affect businesses and readers

A practical classification starts with three questions: What function does the product perform? Which infrastructure does it depend on? Who uses the service? Answers may identify several relevant categories without implying that one label captures the whole arrangement.

For example, a user-facing application may combine a frontend with a smart contract and rely on a decentralized network. Its interface, contract, and network are related components, but they are not interchangeable. Similarly, an organization’s business model is not identical to the protocol or asset category it references. Describe the specific layer or function before applying a sector label.

This approach is useful for builders and operators describing a system, and for institutions or business decision-makers assessing what a product claims to do. It encourages a concrete description of dependencies and users instead of treating a broad label as a complete account. It also helps avoid comparing unlike things—for instance, a network with a service built on that network, or a consumer use case with the infrastructure that may support it.

Sector names should not be used as shortcuts for conclusions they do not establish. A label by itself does not demonstrate that a product is safe, compliant, available in a particular place, or commercially sound. Those questions need their own evidence and scope.

## Risks and limits of broad sector labels

Sector terminology varies, and technical designs differ within a category. The AMM exchange example should not be generalized to all exchange designs; the Bitcoin example should not be generalized to every blockchain. A map remains useful only if it preserves those distinctions rather than flattening them into a single model.

Cross-chain bridges show why labels can also conceal important dependencies. A BIS analysis identifies additional smart-contract security risks, costs, and delays when bridges connect stablecoin records on separate blockchains. That finding is scoped to the analysis and should not be presented as a conclusion about every bridge implementation.

Finally, category labels cannot substitute for current, specific evidence. Regulatory, tax, availability, and compliance conclusions require support for the relevant jurisdiction and circumstances. Claims about fees, yields, security properties, or recovery options require current product-specific information. Without that evidence, the sound conclusion is limited: a sector label can help identify a broad function, but it cannot establish how a particular service operates or what protections it offers.

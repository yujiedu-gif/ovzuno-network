For artists, an NFT is a distinct token recorded under a blockchain contract, not the artwork itself. It may identify or reference a creative work, but the token record does not automatically establish who made the work, who owns its copyright, or what uses are permitted. Token ownership, the artwork, and any separately granted rights need to be considered as distinct things.

## What an NFT represents for an artist

A digital token can be understood as a ledger record about an asset and its owner, with programmable rules governing use or transfer. That technical record does not, by itself, determine legal title, enforceability, or the quality of the asset it refers to. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/tokenisation-context-money-and-other-assets-concepts-and-implications-central-banks>))

For an NFT, minting creates a token record and initial ownership under the rules of the smart contract. Minting alone does not create or authenticate the referenced work, store it, or transfer rights in it. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/nft/>))

On Ethereum, an NFT contract records token ownership by associating a unique identifier with an Ethereum address. That is an address-level record about the token within the contract’s system. It is not, on its own, a complete account of the artwork’s creator, copyright holder, or permitted uses. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/nft/>))

The work and the token can be related without being identical. A token may be associated with an image, audio file, or another creative work, but the record of the token is not the same thing as the work itself. When someone says an NFT “represents” art, ask what the token specifically identifies and what evidence explains that connection.

This distinction also helps clarify broad phrases such as “owning the NFT.” That can describe control or ownership of the token under the contract’s rules. It should not be expanded into a claim about ownership of the artwork or copyright unless separate evidence supports that claim. The token’s technical function and the legal or contractual meaning assigned to it are separate questions.

## What the blockchain record can show

A blockchain record can show token activity under the relevant contract and identify the address associated with token ownership. That information concerns the token record. By itself, it does not identify the human or organization behind an address, establish that the address holder created the artwork, or prove that the holder has permission to use it.

The limits matter when interpreting a transfer. A change in the address associated with a token is not necessarily a transfer of copyright or of the underlying work. The U.S. Copyright Office and USPTO report states that transferring an NFT does not necessarily transfer the associated physical or digital asset or copyright rights in the work; rights depend on applicable law and valid contracts, licenses, or other governing terms. ([source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>))

A record should therefore be read narrowly: what token is involved, which address is recorded, and what activity the record captures. Statements that go further—such as “the holder is the artist,” “the holder owns the copyright,” or “the holder may reproduce the work”—need evidence beyond the token record. Do not treat an address or transaction history as proof of facts it does not directly record.

For artists, this is a useful way to assess claims made about their work. A token may be associated with a particular creative work, but that association alone does not show that the person who created the token had authority to make the claim. Nor does a record of token ownership settle whether another person may display, copy, adapt, or distribute the work. Those questions require their own supporting information.

## NFT artists: separate token ownership from copyright

Holding an NFT does not automatically transfer copyright. Nor does possession of a token alone establish permission to use the work. Rights are separate from the token record and depend on applicable law and valid contractual terms, licenses, or other governing arrangements. ([source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>))

An NFT can be used as part of a rights-management, transfer, or licensing workflow when valid legal terms are in place and authorized rights holders are involved. The technology may support such a workflow, but the token itself does not establish that the grant is valid, that the person making it has authority, or what its scope is. ([source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>))

For an artist, a buyer, or another person encountering a rights claim, the practical question is not simply whether a token exists. It is what the accompanying terms say. A license or agreement may describe permitted uses or a rights transfer. Read those terms for the work they cover, the rights they address, and the conditions they state. Do not infer permission to copy, display, modify, distribute, or commercially use a work solely from token ownership.

It also helps to distinguish several different claims that can be compressed into the word “ownership”:

- **Token ownership:** who is recorded as owning the token under its contract rules.
- **Authorship:** who created the work, a claim that the token record does not establish by itself.
- **Copyright ownership:** who holds rights in the underlying work under the applicable terms and law.
- **Permission to use:** what a license or other valid arrangement allows someone to do.

Evidence supporting one of these claims should not be treated as automatic proof of the others. A token may be connected to a rights arrangement, but the relevant terms and the authority of the person granting rights remain important.

## What to verify before interpreting an NFT

Before relying on an NFT as evidence about a work or a permitted use, separate the questions and check what information supports each one:

1. **Identify the token being discussed.** A title, image, or informal description does not by itself specify the token record. Establish which token and contract the claim concerns before interpreting its recorded ownership.
2. **Check the asserted connection to the artwork.** Determine what work the token is said to reference and what supports that connection. A token record alone does not establish authorship or authorization to associate the token with the work.
3. **Distinguish the record from the work.** Ask where the artwork is identified and whether the claim is about the token, the creative file, or both. The fact that minting creates a token record does not mean that minting created or authenticated the referenced artwork. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/nft/>))
4. **Read the rights language.** Review any license, platform terms, or separate agreement that purports to grant permission or transfer rights. Identify the right described rather than relying on a general label such as “ownership.”
5. **Check the grantor’s authority.** If someone claims to grant rights, look for support that the person is authorized to do so. The presence of a token does not, by itself, establish that authority.
6. **Keep conclusions within the evidence.** If the record shows a token address, describe it as a token ownership record. Do not turn that into a broader assertion about authorship, copyright, or usage rights without separate support.

This checklist is a way to frame questions, not a guarantee that a particular claim is genuine or that a file or arrangement is secure or permanent. The technical token record cannot answer every question about the artwork or rights attached to it.

## Legal, tax, and platform limits

This explanation does not determine the legal or tax treatment of any NFT. No jurisdiction-specific legal or tax rule is asserted here. The applicable rights may depend on the jurisdiction and on the relevant contracts, licenses, or other governing terms; assess those materials rather than treating a token record as a substitute for them. ([source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>))

Likewise, do not infer platform rules, fees, availability, security, storage, recovery, or protection from the mere existence of a token. Those are specific questions that require current evidence about the relevant platform, arrangements, and technical systems. The NFT record does not itself establish a guarantee about access to the artwork or recovery of a file.

The central distinction is straightforward: the NFT is a token record; the artwork is the creative work that may be associated with it; and copyright or usage rights are separate matters established through applicable terms and arrangements. When an NFT is described as proof of ownership, ask which of these is meant and what evidence supports that specific claim.

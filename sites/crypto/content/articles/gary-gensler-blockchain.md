## How to distinguish remarks from SEC actions

Begin by identifying the kind of document in front of you. A speech or interview is evidence of remarks. A rule, order, enforcement filing, or other official agency document may record an institutional action. The document’s title alone does not establish its effect: readers should check who issued it, what it addresses, and what scope and status the text actually specifies.

When a person comments on the SEC or on a topic the agency may address, attribute the statement to that person unless an official record supports describing it as agency action or policy. A sentence from a speech should not be converted into a command to all market participants. In the other direction, an agency document should not be generalized beyond its stated subject or scope.

For a claimed SEC action, locate the official record rather than relying solely on a news summary. Note the document type, date, subject, and stated status. If the sources at hand do not establish whether a matter is proposed, final, stayed, withdrawn, or otherwise changed, avoid implying that its current status is known. These are verification questions, not details that can be supplied by assumption.

## How to verify a statement’s context and current status

Use a source-first process before repeating a claim:

1. **Find the original record.** Look for the complete transcript, recording, filing, or official publication, not only a headline, excerpt, or repost.
2. **Confirm attribution and setting.** Check the speaker, date, venue, audience, and surrounding passage. Preserve qualifications, and distinguish a direct quotation from a paraphrase.
3. **Identify the subject.** Determine whether the passage concerns blockchain technology, crypto assets, market participants, or an SEC matter. Do not use evidence about one subject to make a broader claim about another.
4. **Check institutional action separately.** If a claim says the SEC issued a rule, order, or other action, locate the corresponding official record and confirm its document type, stated scope, and status. A speech alone does not establish those details.
5. **Verify current legal claims independently.** For claims about current US requirements, consult authoritative, up-to-date sources. If the evidence does not settle the issue, state that limitation and seek qualified advice instead of presenting an interpretation as a confirmed rule.

Governance changes also need their own evidence. For example, Ethereum protocol changes require proposal, technical review, implementation, coordination, and adoption across network stakeholders. That is a description of Ethereum’s protocol-change process; it does not establish that a particular Gensler remark changed the SEC’s position or produced a legal requirement. [^3]

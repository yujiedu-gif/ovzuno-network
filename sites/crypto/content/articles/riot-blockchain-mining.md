## How Bitcoin mining contributes to a block

Proof of work is Bitcoin’s computational method for producing block candidates. Mining software transforms block-template information into work for hashing hardware to search. A miner varies the block-header work until it finds a hash that satisfies the network’s target condition. The result is computationally costly to find but straightforward for other nodes to verify against the target. These statements describe the Bitcoin mechanism, not a particular company’s equipment or performance. ([source](<https://developer.bitcoin.org/devguide/mining.html>); [source](<https://developer.bitcoin.org/devguide/block_chain.html>); [source](<https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2018/nist.ir.8202.pdf>))

A candidate block includes proposed transactions, but the proof-of-work result does not make every proposed transaction valid. Bitcoin nodes enforce the applicable validity rules. When competing histories exist, nodes treat the valid chain with the greatest cumulative proof of work as the selected history; additional work cannot make an invalid block valid. Mining supplies candidate work, while the network’s rules govern block validity and chain selection. ([source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>))

This is why it is inaccurate to describe a miner as unilaterally deciding which transactions or protocol rules are valid. A miner may prepare work and propose a block, but acceptance depends on the Bitcoin rules applied to the block and the competing histories. The mechanism also does not promise that any particular candidate will be accepted or that a transaction will be included at a particular time.

## What mining can—and cannot—tell you about payment records

When a transaction is included in a Bitcoin block on the accepted chain, the blockchain’s ordered transaction record reflects that inclusion. Bitcoin confirmations indicate increasing confidence in a transaction’s place in the chain, but not an immediate or absolute guarantee against reversal; the degree of confidence depends on confirmations and operating conditions. The protocol-level record should not be confused with a universal merchant or application settlement policy.

A transaction record also does not by itself establish who controls an address or the real-world purpose of a payment. A valid signature can support attribution to control of a key for that transaction, but it does not identify the person, organization, device, or service controlling the key. Nor does a blockchain record alone prove that an external payment obligation has been discharged or that an error or dispute has been resolved.

These limits apply regardless of which miner proposed a block. Mining contributes to the process by which blocks are produced; it does not provide identity verification or establish off-chain facts. A company’s involvement in mining therefore should not be treated as a guarantee about a specific payment’s purpose, recipient, or final treatment by a merchant.

## How Plan B relates to Bitcoin

Bitcoin is a network and protocol, not an individual’s account or viewpoint. In a general description, cryptocurrency systems can support digital peer-to-peer exchange using distributed record-keeping rather than a conventional central transaction processor. That description concerns a system’s design; it does not make a commentator, model, or phrase part of Bitcoin’s protocol. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2018/cryptocurrencies-looking-beyond-hype>)

A protocol is the set of rules that network participants use to validate and process activity. In Bitcoin’s original proof-of-work design, when competing histories exist, nodes treat the valid chain representing the greatest cumulative proof of work as the selected history. This is a technical account of a particular network rule, not a statement about what any individual thinks Bitcoin should do. [source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>)

The distinction matters whenever a public claim sounds authoritative. A person can describe or interpret a network, but their description does not by itself change the network’s rules. Likewise, a model that uses Bitcoin-related information remains an analytical framework unless evidence establishes some separate technical role. Do not infer that the person or model has authority over the protocol simply because the phrase includes “Bitcoin.”

## Claims, models, and forecasts: how to read them

When a statement is associated with “Plan B,” first determine whether it is an exact quotation, a paraphrase, or someone else’s interpretation. Find the original material if possible, then check its author, date, context, and any stated assumptions. A short excerpt can omit qualifications, and a repeated attribution is not a substitute for a source that supports it.

Next, classify what kind of statement it is. An observation about past information describes something already recorded. An explanation proposes why that information took a particular form. A forecast makes a claim about a future outcome. These categories are not interchangeable: past observations do not, on their own, establish a causal explanation or guarantee a future result.

For a model or forecast, inspect the method and its limitations before repeating its conclusion. Ask what information the model uses, what assumptions it makes, and whether the original source presents the result as conditional or uncertain. If the methodology or limitations are not available, say so rather than presenting a prediction as settled fact. This article cannot assess the accuracy of a particular model or forecast because the phrase’s referent and a specific source have not been established.

A forecast is also not a Bitcoin protocol rule. Protocol documentation addresses how a network is designed to behave under its rules; a forecast expresses an expectation about possible outcomes. Neither should be presented as the other. Keeping that distinction clear avoids treating a commentator’s view as an instruction from the network or a guaranteed result.

An NFT is a distinct token recorded on a blockchain. In simple terms, think of it as a numbered entry in a shared digital record: the entry can be associated with an image or other item, but it is not the image itself. Controlling the token does not automatically mean owning the linked file or the copyright to it.

## What does “NFT” mean in plain language?

NFT stands for **non-fungible token**. “Non-fungible” means distinct rather than interchangeable one-for-one: one token is not simply a substitute for another identical unit. A digital token can combine a ledger record about an asset and its owner with programmable rules governing how it is used or transferred. That is a broad description of digital tokens, not a claim that every NFT works the same way. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/tokenisation-context-money-and-other-assets-concepts-and-implications-central-banks>)

A useful analogy is a numbered entry in a record book. The number helps distinguish one entry from another. For an NFT, the relevant blockchain record identifies a token through the rules of its contract; on Ethereum, an NFT contract records ownership by associating a unique token identifier with an Ethereum address. [source](<https://ethereum.org/nft/>)

The analogy has limits. An NFT is not a paper certificate, and the token record is not necessarily the digital item it refers to. It is a record managed under blockchain and contract rules.

## What does an NFT represent?

An NFT may be associated with a digital item, such as an image, or with another stated use. Minting creates a token record and initial ownership under the smart contract’s rules. It does not, by itself, create, authenticate, store, or transfer rights in the referenced work or asset. [source](<https://ethereum.org/nft/>)

Keep two things separate: the **token record** and the **content it is associated with**. A token may identify or point to information about a file, but that does not mean the file is stored inside the token. The link and the file can depend on systems beyond the token record. The details vary by application and arrangement. [source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>)

So, having the token record does not by itself establish that a linked file will remain available or that a holder has a particular right to use it. The token and its associated content are connected by the relevant technical and contractual arrangements, not made identical by the word “NFT.”

## What does owning an NFT actually mean?

In the basic technical sense, owning an NFT means that the blockchain’s contract record associates the token with an address under the contract’s rules. That is a statement about the token record. It should not be expanded into a claim that the holder owns every related file or legal right.

In particular, transfer of an NFT does not necessarily transfer copyright or rights in the associated physical or digital asset. Those rights depend on applicable law and valid contracts, licenses, or other governing terms. [source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>)

A simple way to keep the concepts straight is:

- **Token control:** which address the contract record identifies as the token’s owner.
- **Content:** the image, file, or other item associated with the token, which may involve systems outside the token record.
- **Intellectual-property rights:** rights that depend on applicable law and governing terms, rather than following automatically from the token record.

These are related questions, but they are not interchangeable. A blockchain entry can describe token ownership without resolving what rights a person has in the linked work.

## A simple example—and where the analogy stops

Imagine a digital catalog with an entry labeled “Item 17.” The entry could point to a particular digital image. If the catalog’s rules associate that entry with your address, that describes control of the token record. It does not mean the image’s data is inside the entry, nor does it automatically grant permission to reproduce the image.

This example is only an analogy. An actual NFT is governed by a smart contract and the blockchain on which it exists; a catalog entry is not. And the record’s association with an item does not settle authenticity, availability, or legal rights. Those questions depend on additional systems and terms. [source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>)

The key idea is narrower than “owning the picture”: a token can record a distinct identifier and an owner under its contract’s rules, while the item it references and rights in that item remain separate matters.

## What this basic NFT explanation does not establish

The existence of an NFT alone does not establish that a referenced item is authentic, that a linked file will remain available, or that its holder has copyright or other rights. A technical token record cannot by itself determine legal title, enforceability, or the quality of the asset it represents. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/tokenisation-context-money-and-other-assets-concepts-and-implications-central-banks>) For rights in a particular work, the relevant terms and legal context matter; this plain-language definition is not a legal conclusion.

Other questions—including creation, marketplace use, fees, security, recovery, tax, or regulatory treatment—require their own context and evidence. They are outside the definition here. The basic distinction remains: an NFT is a distinct blockchain token; what it points to and what rights accompany it are separate questions.

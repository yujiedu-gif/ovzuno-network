Crypto perpetual futures, or **perps**, are contracts that provide exposure to a cryptocurrency’s price without a scheduled expiration. They are not the same as owning the referenced cryptocurrency. Funding, leverage, margin, settlement, and liquidation terms are specific to each product, so the name “perp” alone does not establish how those rules work.

## What “perps crypto” means

A perpetual future is a futures-style contract with no fixed expiry date. A position represents contractual price exposure; it does not necessarily transfer ownership of the referenced cryptocurrency. The contract’s documentation defines what the position tracks and how it is accounted for or settled.

This distinction helps prevent a common misunderstanding: a contract that refers to an asset is not, by that fact alone, the asset itself. A perp position therefore should not be read as proof that its holder owns or can withdraw the referenced cryptocurrency. The product’s terms determine the relationship between the contract and its reference market.

Perpetual futures are derivatives: their value is tied to a referenced asset or price rather than being identical to ownership of that asset. Specific contract design and settlement details can vary. The general definition identifies the absence of scheduled expiration, but it does not answer whether a product settles in cryptocurrency, another asset, or through a different process. Those details must be established from current documentation for the contract in question.

## How perpetual futures differ from dated futures

The basic structural contrast is expiration. A dated future specifies an expiration, while a perpetual future has no scheduled expiry. Since a perp does not reach a preset expiration date, its design may use funding payments to help keep its contract price aligned with a reference market.

Funding payments are transfers between long and short positions under the contract’s rules. Their intended role is to support alignment, not to guarantee that the contract price will match the reference market. Funding does not work identically across all products: formulas, reference prices, payment timing, and which side pays or receives are matters of contract design.

The difference in expiry should not be stretched into an assumption that all other mechanics are standardized. The word *perpetual* says there is no fixed expiry; it does not specify how a position is valued, how funding is calculated, or what happens when a position is closed. A dated future’s expiration and settlement terms also depend on its contract. To compare the structures precisely, first identify the expiry provisions, then consult the relevant rules for funding and settlement rather than inferring them from a label.

## Funding payments, leverage, and liquidation

**Funding** is a payment mechanism between long and short positions as defined by a particular contract. It is not a universal rate or a guaranteed payment in one direction. Depending on the rules and circumstances, funding may be a cost or a payment for a position. Without contract-specific documentation, no particular calculation, interval, reference price, or payment direction should be assumed.

**Leverage** means that a position’s exposure is larger than the collateral supporting it. This can magnify the effect of price changes on that collateral, including losses. Leverage therefore changes the scale of exposure; it does not make the underlying contract less dependent on price movements. The amount of leverage available, collateral requirements, and applicable calculations are product-specific, and this definition does not identify a suitable level for any reader.

**Liquidation** is a venue’s process for reducing or closing a position when its margin requirements are no longer met. It is separate from expiration. A perpetual contract has no scheduled expiry, but that does not mean a position must remain open: it can still be subject to the product’s margin and liquidation rules. Trigger conditions, procedures, and the treatment of a remaining position require review of the applicable contract terms.

## Risks and design details to verify

Perps combine contractual price exposure with product-specific funding and margin rules. Potential concerns include losses magnified by leverage, liquidation when margin requirements are not met, and funding payments that may affect the cost of holding a position. The details and outcomes depend on the contract; the general definition does not establish a formula, threshold, payment schedule, or settlement method.

When reading documentation, separate the defining feature from the terms that vary. Confirm whether the product has a scheduled expiry, what reference market it uses, how it calculates and applies funding, what collateral and margin requirements govern positions, and how liquidation and settlement are handled. These are verification points, not trading instructions or a recommendation to use a contract.

Security, custody, recovery, and operational safeguards also depend on the product and provider; they cannot be inferred from the term *perp*. The evidence supporting this definition does not establish particular safeguards or rules for any provider. Likewise, it does not support conclusions about jurisdiction-specific regulation, tax treatment, product availability, fees, yields, or compliance obligations. Those subjects require current evidence specific to the product and, where relevant, the jurisdiction.

The concise definition is therefore structural: a crypto perp is a futures-style contract tied to a cryptocurrency reference, with no scheduled expiry. Funding, leverage, and liquidation help describe how a particular product operates and where its risks may arise, but their exact terms must be verified rather than presumed.

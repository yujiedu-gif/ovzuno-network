A crypto asset is not FDIC-insured merely because it is dollar-linked, held through a financial company, or connected to a bank. FDIC deposit insurance concerns qualifying deposits at insured banks; it does not automatically cover a crypto token or a customer’s claim against an intermediary. To assess an intermediary’s insurance claim, identify the bank, account holder, and deposit arrangement, then verify the applicable rules and limits using current FDIC materials.

## What FDIC deposit insurance protects

The key distinction is between a bank deposit and another kind of asset or claim. Deposit insurance is relevant to qualifying deposits at insured banks. A crypto asset, a token, or a contractual claim against a service provider is not interchangeable with a deposit merely because the arrangement refers to a bank or displays a dollar amount.

This article’s accepted evidence does not establish current FDIC coverage limits, the full rules for eligible deposits, or detailed conditions for pass-through coverage. Those are essential facts to verify directly with current FDIC guidance. Do not rely on a remembered limit or a provider’s summary in place of checking the current rules and the account documents.

Deposit insurance also should not be treated as a general guarantee against loss. An insurance statement about a deposit does not, by itself, establish that an investment or token will retain its value, or that other losses are covered. The relevant question is what funds are held as deposits, at which insured bank, and under what account arrangement—not whether a product is described as stable or bank-connected.

## Crypto FDIC insured: separate the crypto asset from the bank deposit

The phrase “crypto FDIC insured” can blur two separate things: a crypto asset held by a customer and money held as a deposit at a bank in connection with a crypto-related service. Holding the asset does not, by itself, show that the customer has a deposit at an insured bank. A bank’s involvement somewhere in a service also does not resolve who owns the deposit or what protection may apply.

A stablecoin arrangement illustrates why the distinction matters, but it does not answer the insurance question on its own. In a typical fiat-reserve-backed stablecoin model, an issuer receives collateral before minting tokens and burns tokens after redemption, with offchain reserve assets supporting the process. This is a technical model, not a claim that every stablecoin is fully collateralized or that a token holder has a direct deposit claim against a bank. Reserve composition, custody, legal rights, eligibility, and redemption timing are arrangement-specific. [^1]

A token holder’s rights require separate scrutiny. For an asset-backed token arrangement, relevant questions include what or whom the holder has a claim against and how backing assets relate to that claim. Those questions help describe the arrangement but do not independently establish that a token is an insured bank deposit. [^2]

An intermediary may also combine several business functions. A centralized crypto exchange operator may combine trading, proprietary activity, lending, token issuance, and custody, though not every exchange performs every function. These roles do not establish where a particular customer’s funds are held or what insurance applies. Review the particular account and contractual relationship rather than inferring coverage from the provider’s general business description. [^3]

## How to assess a pass-through or intermediary claim

A pass-through claim concerns whether deposit insurance associated with money held through an intermediary may extend to customers of that intermediary. The accepted evidence here does not set out the current FDIC recordkeeping and ownership requirements or establish when pass-through coverage applies. The presence of an intermediary, or its assertion that coverage passes through, is therefore not enough to confirm protection. The account facts and current rules must be checked.

Use the following questions to organize that review:

1. **Which bank is named?** Identify the specific institution said to hold the funds. A technology company, exchange, wallet, or payment service is not necessarily the bank.
2. **Who is the depositor?** Determine whether the account is held in a customer’s name, an intermediary’s name, or another form. The person using a service and the account holder may not be the same party.
3. **What is the underlying relationship?** Read the documents to determine whether they describe a bank deposit, a token, a reserve arrangement, or a claim against an intermediary. Similar dollar descriptions do not make these arrangements equivalent.
4. **What records support the assertion?** Ask what records identify the customers and their interests, and compare the arrangement with current FDIC requirements. This article does not supply those requirements or decide whether any particular records satisfy them.
5. **What exactly is claimed to be insured?** Separate a statement about qualifying bank deposits from a broader claim about crypto balances, tokens, or services. A connection to a bank alone does not answer that question.

This is a fact-finding framework, not a coverage determination. Where the documents do not clearly identify the depositor, bank, and nature of the funds, the insurance claim remains unresolved until those details are verified against the applicable FDIC rules.

## Verify the claim before relying on it

Start by identifying the bank and confirming its insured status through current FDIC resources. Then read the latest account agreement and disclosures. Look for the bank’s legal name, who holds the account, how the funds are described, and exactly what the insurance statement covers. If the provider describes an intermediary arrangement, check whether its documents explain that arrangement and identify the records relevant to the claim.

Next, compare the provider’s statements with authoritative FDIC materials on insured deposits, coverage limits, and third-party or pass-through arrangements. The evidence available for this article does not include current FDIC limits or detailed eligibility rules, so it would be misleading to give a numerical limit or a definitive pass-through checklist here. For a complex or significant arrangement, qualified legal or regulatory guidance may be appropriate based on the actual documents and facts.

Promotional wording is a reason to ask for precise documentation, not proof of coverage. For example, saying that a service “works with” a bank does not, by itself, establish that a customer has an insured deposit there. The actual account relationship and applicable rules determine what the statement means.

## What this coverage does not settle

A deposit-insurance claim does not establish how crypto is held or controlled, whether an intermediary can access it, how a service handles an operational failure, or whether an asset can be recovered. These are distinct questions about custody, operations, and the arrangement’s legal rights. A ledger record about an asset and its owner, including programmable rules governing use or transfer, does not by itself determine legal title, enforceability, or the quality of the represented asset. [^4]

Nor should a reader infer tax treatment, regulatory compliance, fees, product availability, or protection from crypto price changes from an insurance statement. This explainer does not assess or rank providers, recommend an account or asset, or predict outcomes. Its purpose is limited to separating crypto holdings from bank deposits, identifying the facts needed to examine an intermediary claim, and directing readers to current FDIC materials for the rules and limits that apply.

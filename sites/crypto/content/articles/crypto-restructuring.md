A business assessing a crypto restructuring should first identify what is changing, which entities and assets are affected, and where the relevant activities and parties are located. Those facts help legal and tax advisers determine which jurisdiction-specific rules and reporting questions to investigate. This overview cannot establish that a particular requirement applies or predict a tax result.

## What crypto restructuring can mean

“Crypto restructuring” is a broad description, not a single legal or tax category. It may refer to changes in a business entity or its ownership, changes in how operations are organized, transfers involving assets or contracts, or arrangements addressing creditors and obligations. These forms should not be assumed to have the same consequences. Their treatment depends on the facts and applicable law, which must be checked for the relevant jurisdictions.

Start by describing the proposed change in ordinary business terms. Is an entity changing, or is its ownership changing? Are operations, customer relationships, or custody arrangements changing? Are assets, contracts, or obligations moving between parties? Is the business discussing an arrangement with creditors? A clear description helps advisers distinguish the questions raised by each change rather than treating “restructuring” as an answer in itself.

A digital token can be a ledger record about an asset and its owner, with programmable rules governing use or transfer. But that technical record does not by itself determine legal title, enforceability, or the quality of the represented asset. A technology or ledger migration alone therefore does not establish the legal form or consequences of a restructuring. Advisers may need to examine underlying rights, governing documents, and transaction steps as well as technical records. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/tokenisation-context-money-and-other-assets-concepts-and-implications-central-banks>))

## Map the entities, assets, and jurisdictions involved

Build a fact map before asking for conclusions. List the entities involved, their roles, and the people or organizations with whom they contract. Identify the assets and obligations at issue, where records are maintained, and who has custody or control. Note which agreements govern ownership, use, transfer, redemption, or repayment, where applicable.

For each jurisdiction, record why it may matter—for example, an entity’s formation or operations, a counterparty’s location, or where an activity takes place. Treat this as a scoping exercise, not a conclusion that a particular jurisdiction’s law applies. The evidence available for this overview does not establish licensing, tax, insolvency, or reporting rules for any specific country, state, or transaction. Current local legal and tax sources are needed before describing those requirements.

Custody is one fact to map precisely. In third-party custody, a provider manages access to private keys on behalf of customers; crypto exchanges are among the possible third-party custodians. The custody structure, customer rights, segregation, insurance, regulation, and insolvency treatment vary by provider and jurisdiction. Record the provider, relevant agreements, account or wallet arrangements, and who can authorize access or transfers, then ask advisers to assess the specific structure. ([source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>))

Where the business or its customers rely on an exchange or another intermediary, identify the actual functions rather than relying on a broad label. A centralized exchange operator may combine trading, proprietary activity, lending, token issuance, and custody. Not every exchange offers every function, so provider terms and legal structure require separate verification. This distinction can help teams identify activities, contracts, and assets that might be affected by a change; it does not determine regulatory or tax treatment. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>))

If records leave uncertainty about ownership, contractual rights, or control, document the gap instead of filling it with an assumption. The relevant question may differ for a ledger entry, a contract, an account relationship, or an underlying asset. For an asset-backed token arrangement, clarity about what or whom the holder has a claim against, and how backing assets relate to that claim, is a separate issue for review. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/wholesale-digital-tokens>))

## Regulatory questions depend on the activity and transaction

Ask counsel to compare the business before and after the proposed change. Could it alter activities, who controls an entity or service, how custody works, or which customers and counterparties are served? Could it change the handling of client assets, contractual responsibilities, or provider roles? These are prompts for jurisdiction-specific review, not statements that a license, registration, approval, or notification is required.

For each location identified in the fact map, ask qualified counsel to check current, authoritative sources for potentially relevant licensing, registration, notification, approval, insolvency, and compliance requirements. Confirm each source’s jurisdiction and scope, the activities and entities it covers, and whether it applies to the specific facts and proposed steps. The evidence available for this article does not establish which requirements apply to a restructuring, or whether a particular route is available.

Also distinguish a provider’s operational role from a legal conclusion about the business. A service provider may perform custody or other functions, but its presence alone does not settle who holds a claim, which entity is responsible for an activity, or what regulatory framework applies. Those questions require the relevant contracts, the actual operating model, and jurisdiction-specific review.

An organizational chart or service description may not fully show how control works in practice. Systems described as decentralized can exhibit substantial de facto centralization, but whether that is relevant must be assessed for the specific network, asset, application, and time window. Treat this as a prompt to map actual decision-making and operational dependencies, not as a conclusion about a particular business or its legal status. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/crypto-ecosystem-key-elements-and-risks>))

## Tax reporting and records to review

Ask qualified tax advisers whether the proposed steps raise tax-treatment or reporting questions in each relevant jurisdiction. Describe the actual changes for review: possible transfers or disposals, changes in entities or ownership, debt or creditor arrangements, or changes in how assets and obligations are recorded. These are issues to investigate, not assertions that a particular step is taxable, reportable, or treated in a specific way.

Assemble transaction records, asset and liability schedules, custody statements, contracts, accounting records, and prior filings that advisers request. Keep records tied to the entities and dates involved, and identify gaps or assumptions. If valuation information is needed, ask advisers what basis and supporting documentation are appropriate; this overview does not establish a valuation method or tax outcome.

Do not infer tax rates, filing forms, deadlines, or reporting results from this overview. The evidence available here does not support jurisdiction-specific tax conclusions. Obtain current guidance from the relevant tax authority or applicable legislation, and have qualified professionals assess how it applies to the facts before making or documenting a decision.

## Build an evidence-led review before acting

Prepare a review packet that includes governing documents, contracts, asset and custody records, creditor claims, relevant accounting records, and a timeline of contemplated steps. Add an entity-and-jurisdiction map, a description of activities before and after the change, and a list of unresolved questions. Label assumptions clearly, especially where ownership, control, contractual rights, or custody records are incomplete.

Ask legal and tax professionals to confirm which jurisdictions and current sources they have considered, what facts remain uncertain, and whether approvals, notices, reporting duties, or deadlines need further verification. Request that they distinguish established requirements from open questions and explain the scope of any conclusion. Keep a record of the materials they relied on and any assumptions that limit their analysis, so the business can see which matters remain unresolved.

The purpose of this preparation is to make the facts and uncertainties reviewable, not to select or execute a restructuring route. Do not treat this checklist as a transaction plan or as legal, tax, investment, or insolvency advice. The answer to the business’s regulatory and tax-reporting questions depends on the specific arrangement, affected entities and assets, transaction steps, and jurisdictions involved, and requires qualified professional review.

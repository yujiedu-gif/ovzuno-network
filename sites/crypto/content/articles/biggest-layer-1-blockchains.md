There is no single evidence-backed answer to which Layer 1 blockchain is “biggest.” The result depends on the measure—such as economic value, recorded activity, adoption, or infrastructure participation—and a fair comparison needs consistent definitions, time windows, and data sources. The evidence available here does not provide dated, comparable figures or verified classifications for individual networks, so it cannot support a current ranking.

## What counts as a Layer 1 blockchain?

In common crypto usage, “Layer 1” refers to a base blockchain network and its protocol. It is a practical label, not a separately standardized technical category. For this article’s comparison, the relevant subject is the base network, rather than every application or secondary system associated with it.

A Layer 2 is not automatically part of a Layer 1’s activity total. Some Ethereum Layer 2 systems derive some security from Ethereum’s Layer 1 consensus, but the particular guarantee depends on the system’s proof, data-availability, settlement, governance, and exit mechanisms. The label alone does not establish how much security a system derives. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/scaling/>))

A comparison should state its inclusion rule before it lists networks. It should explain which systems count as base layers and whether reported figures cover only those networks. Adding Layer 2 activity to a Layer 1 figure, for example, changes what the total describes; the method should identify such aggregation rather than present it as a base-layer measure.

This scope distinction is not a judgment about which architecture is preferable. It keeps the comparison’s subject consistent: base networks compared with base networks, rather than a mixture of base layers and broader ecosystems.

## What does “biggest” measure?

“Biggest” is a measurement choice, not a universal property. Several candidate measures can describe different aspects of scale, but none should silently stand in for all the others.

- **Economic value** is a financial measure associated with a network or its native asset. It does not, by itself, establish technical capacity, recorded use, or how many independent operators participate. A value-based comparison should name its financial measure and data date rather than treating it as a general network-size score.
- **Recorded activity** might count transactions or another kind of on-chain event. Readers need the definition, coverage, and observation period to understand the figure. A count of base-layer transactions answers a narrower question than a combined total that also includes other systems.
- **Adoption** requires an explicit proxy. For example, an address count is a count of addresses; it should not be described as a count of people or organizations unless the method supports that interpretation.
- **Infrastructure participation** may be described through nodes or other network roles. A raw participant count does not establish how independently those participants operate. Node count alone does not show operator independence, geographic diversity, implementation diversity, or block-production power. ([source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ir/8202/final>))

Keep these categories separate unless a comparison defines and justifies a way to combine them. Otherwise, a single composite score can conceal the choices behind it and imply a ranking the underlying measures do not support. Nor does a stated decentralization goal settle how control is distributed in practice: crypto and DeFi systems can exhibit substantial de facto centralization, and the degree and location of concentration must be assessed for a specific network, asset, application, and time window. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/crypto-ecosystem-key-elements-and-risks>))

## How to compare the biggest layer 1 blockchains fairly

Start with a measurement rule, not a preselected list of leaders. For every figure, ask what was counted, how it was collected, what activity it covers, and when it was observed. Apply the same definitions and cutoff across the networks included.

For an activity measure, distinguish transaction counts from active-address counts and other event counts. Check whether the figure covers records on the base layer alone or also includes activity associated with applications or Layer 2 systems. If sources count different things, report the measures separately instead of placing them in a directly comparable ranking.

For adoption and infrastructure measures, define the proxy just as carefully. A proxy can answer a narrow question without supporting a broader conclusion: an address count is not automatically a user count, and a node count is not automatically a measure of independent control. The latter requires information about independence and other dimensions that a raw count does not provide. ([source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ir/8202/final>))

Keep units, observation periods, and data cutoffs consistent. A figure from one date should not be placed beside a figure from another period without labeling that mismatch. Also distinguish network-level totals from figures about a particular application or a broader ecosystem. If a source’s method or coverage is unknown, disclose the gap instead of implying that its number is comparable.

These checks form a practical sequence: define the metric, decide what counts as a Layer 1, identify the source and period, verify that coverage is consistent, and then describe what the resulting figures do—and do not—show. When a comparison cannot meet those conditions, it may still describe individual figures, but it should not present them as a like-for-like ranking.

## Why rankings change and can mislead

Activity can vary over time. A snapshot is evidence for its stated observation period, not proof of a network’s typical scale across a longer period. Without a defined period and method, readers cannot tell what conditions the number represents.

Definitions and coverage can also differ. Two sources may use the same label for a metric while applying different inclusion rules. A base-layer count and a combined ecosystem total are different measures, even if both are described informally as network activity. If the differences are not resolved or disclosed, an ordered list can suggest more precision than the data warrants.

A high value on one measure does not establish overall superiority or suitability. Scaling work may seek to increase transaction speed and throughput without sacrificing decentralization or security, but this is a design objective—not a universal score or proof that a particular network achieves it. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/scaling/>)) A size comparison should answer only the question its metric is designed to answer.

The underlying systems also differ in participation, consensus, privacy, finality, and governance. A blockchain uses cryptographically linked transactions, distributed ledger copies, validation, and conflict-resolution rules to maintain a tamper-evident shared history, but those operating characteristics vary across systems. ([source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ir/8202/final>)) A size label therefore does not provide a complete account of how a network works.

## How to read a dated network-size snapshot

Before relying on a published snapshot, look for its measurement date, the source of each figure, the exact metric definition, the observation period, and the network inclusion rules. Then check whether the figures cover comparable activity and whether they describe base layers rather than a larger ecosystem.

Treat any ordering as specific to its metric and observation period. If a table does not explain its method, combines unlike measures, or leaves coverage unclear, it cannot establish a robust “biggest” ranking. The evidence available for this article does not supply dated measurements, collection methods, or comparable network-level data, so no named-network ranking can be verified here.

For an operational decision, a size snapshot is only a starting point: verify that its date and definitions match the question being asked, and consult current, independently verifiable evidence. Without that check, the snapshot may not describe the networks on comparable terms. The defensible conclusion is methodological rather than a list of winners: say what was measured, what was excluded, and what the evidence cannot establish.

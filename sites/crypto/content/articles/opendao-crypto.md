## OpenDAO crypto: separate the project, protocol, token, and governance

A **project or community** is the organization or group associated with work under a name. A **protocol** is a set of rules or mechanisms that software can implement. A **token** is a digital asset; its function depends on its design and the arrangements around it. **Governance** describes how decisions are proposed, authorized, or put into effect. These categories can be connected, but none of those connections follows from a shared name alone.

For example, a project might publish software and separately issue a token, but that possibility does not show that any particular token controls the software or participates in project decisions. To establish such a relationship, find project documentation that states what the token does and governance materials that explain the decision process. A description of a DAO in general cannot establish the rights or functions associated with a specific OpenDAO-related token.

Token use and governance authority also should not be conflated. A token may have a stated project-related purpose, but the label does not establish voting rights, authority over protocol changes, ownership of an organization, or a claim on assets. Those are separate matters that require specific evidence about the token and the relevant governance or legal arrangements. Where the documentation is silent, do not fill the gap with assumptions based on common crypto terminology.

Governance itself can involve different mechanisms. DAO governance systems can use proposals, voting, delegation, token-based or share-based membership, reputation, or multisignature execution; the actual rights and execution paths depend on the DAO’s contracts, policies, and legal context. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/dao/>)) This is a general description of possible approaches, not evidence that OpenDAO uses any of them.

A treasury is another area where the distinction between a record and a governance process matters. A tracker can observe configured on-chain addresses, but it is not by itself a complete accounting or governance record. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/dao/>)) If project materials refer to a treasury, check which addresses are included and whether governance records explain how decisions about those funds are made. Do not treat an address list or dashboard as proof of the full scope of an organization’s assets or its decision-making arrangements.

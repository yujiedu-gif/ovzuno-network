Different cryptocurrencies can be grouped by the role their designs say they serve—for example, supporting a network, transferring value, or aiming to reference another asset. These labels describe intended functions, not verified properties: they do not establish how an asset operates, what supports its value, or what risks it carries. To interpret an unfamiliar cryptocurrency, identify the relevant network and documentation, then separate stated purpose from claims that require independent, current evidence.

## What makes cryptocurrency categories different?

A useful classification starts with two questions: What role is the asset described as serving, and which network or protocol does that role depend on? Those questions are related but not interchangeable. An asset’s label describes a proposed function; its network provides technical context. One asset may be associated with more than one role, and terminology can vary among projects, so the categories below are practical groupings rather than formal, exhaustive definitions.

For example, ether is Ethereum’s native cryptocurrency and is part of the protocol’s market for computation. That identifies a network relationship and a protocol role. It does not establish that assets on other networks have the same purpose or behavior, or that a label alone tells a reader about an asset’s value or risks. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/intro-to-ethereum/>))

Some descriptions instead focus on what an asset is intended to represent or support. Tokens, for instance, can be designed for different functions, including financial-asset, access-related, or governance-related uses. These are examples of possible designs, not proof of legal ownership, enforceable claims, backing, redemption, or value.

That distinction is central to reading categories accurately: a label is a pointer to a claim that should be checked against the relevant design and arrangements. It is not evidence that the function works as described or that holders receive a particular right.

## Different cryptocurrencies explained by their roles

**Network-native assets** are associated with a blockchain’s own protocol. Ether is one example: Ethereum identifies it as its native cryptocurrency and part of the protocol’s market for computation. This example is specific to Ethereum. It should not be treated as a rule for every network or as a complete account of what another network’s asset does. For an unfamiliar asset, consult the documentation for that asset’s own network and protocol.

**Payment-oriented descriptions** emphasize an intended role in transferring value. The label tells readers what function is being claimed; it does not establish that the asset is accepted by a particular recipient or usable in every setting. Nor does the category itself establish settlement conditions, fees, or other operating details. Those points need evidence specific to the asset, network, application, and circumstances.

**Stable-value or asset-referenced descriptions** identify an objective: a stablecoin aims to maintain a stable value relative to a specified asset, currency, or basket through a stated stabilization mechanism. The word “stable” describes that aim. It does not establish that market value or redemption value will remain equal to the reference.

The phrase also does not identify one universal design. For example, an algorithmic stablecoin may attempt to stabilize value through rules and market incentives that expand or contract supply rather than through a fully matched reserve of the reference asset. Its stability depends on demand, liquidity, credible incentives, and any supporting collateral; the mechanism can fail reflexively. This describes a design category, not the operation or prospects of a specific asset. ([source](<https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/dp/2025/english/usea.pdf>))

**Governance-related descriptions** point to a claimed connection with decision-making in a project or protocol. **Utility-related descriptions** indicate an intended project- or protocol-related function rather than a universal technical standard. Neither label alone establishes what rights a holder can exercise, whether a service will remain available, or whether a particular outcome will follow. The relevant documentation and arrangements determine what the words mean in context.

These groupings are not mutually exclusive. An asset can have more than one stated role, and projects may use the same label for different designs. Treat each category as a prompt to inspect the particular asset rather than as a settled classification of its properties.

## How to read an asset’s design claims

Begin by identifying the name of the asset, the network on which the relevant representation exists, and the issuer or protocol named in the description. Then locate documentation tied to that specific asset and network. A general category label cannot substitute for the project’s technical materials or any arrangements that are said to support the asset.

Next, record what the documentation actually describes: the intended function, how transfers or administration are handled, and—if a reference value is claimed—the stated mechanism behind that objective. Keep those documented statements distinct from conclusions about whether the mechanism works or what claims a holder may have. The available evidence for this overview does not establish the backing, redemption terms, or operation of any particular asset, so those properties should not be inferred from a label.

It also helps to distinguish a network’s technical behavior from an asset-level claim. Ethereum full nodes independently validate blocks and transactions according to consensus rules. That is a description of what those nodes do within Ethereum; it does not prove that every asset associated with a blockchain has been verified in every relevant sense or that an individual asset is secure.

For each claim, note its source, scope, and what it leaves unanswered. Project documentation can establish what a project says it intends to do. It does not, by itself, establish backing, redemption, security, compliance, availability, or real-world acceptance. Where documentation does not answer a question, mark it as unknown instead of filling the gap with assumptions.

## A simple way to compare categories

Use a consistent set of questions when comparing descriptions:

1. **What role is claimed?** Note whether the asset is presented as network-native, payment-oriented, stable-value, governance-related, or utility-focused. Allow for more than one stated role.
2. **Which network or protocol is involved?** Keep the inquiry tied to that specific system. Do not assume that a familiar label means the same thing across networks.
3. **What mechanism is documented?** Record what the documentation says about the intended function, transfers, administration, and any claimed reference value. Separate explicit description from inference.
4. **What needs separate evidence?** Mark claims about backing, redemption, stability, security, access, or holder rights for asset-specific verification.
5. **What remains unresolved?** Preserve uncertainty when the documentation does not establish a property. A category label cannot close that gap.

This comparison method organizes intended function and documented design without ranking assets or recommending a choice. It also prevents a technical description from being treated as an answer to separate questions about legal status, availability, or real-world acceptance.

## What category labels cannot tell you

A category does not establish an asset’s value, stability, security, or future performance. In particular, a stated aim to maintain a reference value is not a guarantee. The algorithmic design described above depends on demand, liquidity, incentive credibility, and any supporting collateral, and its mechanism can fail reflexively. That limitation applies to the described model; it is not a conclusion about every asset that uses a stable-value label.

A label also does not establish fees, yields, recovery options, or whether an asset is accepted in a particular setting. Nor should a reader infer legal status, tax treatment, compliance, or availability from the asset’s name or category. This overview does not establish those matters; they require current evidence that applies to the specific asset and circumstances.

Use role labels as an index into asset-specific documentation, not as a verdict. Identify the relevant network, record what the materials say the asset is intended to do, and keep unverified claims—especially claims about backing, rights, or stability—separate from established facts.

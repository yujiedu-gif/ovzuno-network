In crypto, **L2** means **Layer 2**: a broad term for a system connected to an underlying blockchain, often called Layer 1 (L1). The label describes a relationship, not one specific network, token, or technical design. What a particular L2 does—and what it relies on the L1 to do—depends on its implementation.

## What does L2 mean in crypto?

“Layer 2” is shorthand for a system arranged in relation to an underlying blockchain. The underlying chain is commonly called Layer 1, or the base chain. The general idea is that responsibilities can be divided between the system and that base chain. But the label alone does not say which responsibilities are handled where.

A useful way to understand this division is to distinguish four functions: **execution**, **settlement**, **consensus**, and **data availability**. Execution concerns processing transactions or operations. Settlement concerns establishing the result. Consensus is how participants agree on the chain’s state. Data availability concerns whether participants can obtain the transaction information needed to verify summarized state changes. One architecture model separates these functions and assigns them to specialized layers; real systems can arrange them differently.

So, when someone says “L2,” they are usually referring to a category of blockchain-related systems, not naming a single cryptocurrency. The term does not identify a particular token or establish that two systems using the label share the same design. To interpret a reference accurately, identify the network or implementation being discussed.

## How a Layer 2 relates to its underlying blockchain

The key relationship is that an L2 operates in connection with a base blockchain, while the division of work between them depends on design. In some Ethereum Layer 2 systems, for example, some security is derived from Ethereum’s Layer 1 consensus rather than coming only from a separate validator set. This is a qualified example about some systems, not a guarantee about every project called an L2.

The general label also does not specify whether, or how, the base chain is used for settlement, verification, or security. A system’s documentation needs to explain those roles. The technical relationship matters more than the layer number alone: the same broad label does not prove that two systems rely on the underlying chain in the same way.

For readers assessing a specific design, the useful questions are concrete: What does the L2 process? What role does the underlying chain play? Which functions are assigned to each layer? Those questions help turn a broad category name into an implementation-level description. Without that information, it is not possible to infer a particular system’s security or verification model just from “L2.”

This is a definition, not a comparison of specific networks. The term does not establish performance, fees, security, or suitability, and this overview does not rank implementations. Those matters require evidence about the specific system and the conditions under which it operates.

## What people mean by “2 crypto” and related shorthand

The phrase **“2 crypto”** is not a precise technical definition by itself. In a search about blockchain scaling, it may be an informal or incomplete way of referring to **“Layer 2 crypto”** or an **L2**. The surrounding context is necessary to tell.

If a passage discusses a system connected to an underlying blockchain, “Layer 2” is likely the relevant term. If it names a particular network, consult that network’s technical documentation to learn what the term means there. The number two alone does not identify a network, architecture, or token, so avoid inferring technical details from the shorthand.

## What the term L2 does not tell you

The L2 label does not, on its own, explain how a system handles transaction processing, data availability, upgrades, or dispute resolution. It also does not establish that a system is faster, less expensive, more secure, more decentralized, or compatible with a particular application. Those are implementation-specific questions, not properties that follow automatically from the name.

Even a framework used to describe rollup maturity has a limited purpose. The L2BEAT Stages Framework measures rollup maturity in decentralization and trust minimization; it is not a complete security score. A higher stage does not establish that contracts, proof systems, bridges, or implementations are free of bugs. This illustrates why the label and a maturity category should not be treated as substitutes for examining a system’s design.

In short, **L2 means Layer 2: a broad class of systems connected to an underlying blockchain, with responsibilities divided according to implementation.** Treat the term as an orientation point. To understand a particular system, identify its underlying chain and consult implementation-specific documentation about the role that chain plays. The general label does not supply those details.

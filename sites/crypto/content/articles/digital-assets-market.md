The digital assets market is best understood as a technology ecosystem: digital assets and records, the protocols and networks that process them, applications and services that connect people to those systems, and the participants who build and operate them. For builders, operators, institutions, and business decision-makers, this map explains how the parts relate without treating technical activity as evidence of asset value or investment merit.

## What the digital assets market includes

In this overview, the digital assets market means the broad ecosystem around crypto technology: the assets or records handled by technical systems, the networks that maintain them, software that interacts with those networks, supporting infrastructure, and the people and organizations involved. The phrase can be used more narrowly or broadly depending on context. It may refer to a specific class of assets in one discussion and to the wider set of technologies and services in another.

Related terminology also varies. “Web3,” for example, has no single rigid definition and is commonly used as a catch-all term for a user-owned, decentralized vision of the internet. That vision overlaps with parts of the crypto ecosystem, but it does not name one fixed technical architecture. A label alone is not a complete map of the systems or responsibilities involved. [source](<https://ethereum.org/web3/>)

An ecosystem map is different from a market snapshot. It describes components and relationships; it does not supply asset prices, market capitalization, or forecasts. It also does not establish that an asset has a particular value or investment merit. Technical participation and financial performance are separate subjects, and one cannot be inferred from the other merely by identifying network activity.

## The layers: assets, protocols, and applications

Start by separating the asset or record from the rules and software that handle it. A digital asset can be represented or tracked in a technical system, but that description does not by itself establish legal status or specify what rights, if any, accompany it. Those questions depend on the particular arrangement and context and are outside this technology-focused overview.

A protocol is the set of rules that participating software follows for a network’s activity. It defines how that system processes and records actions. Different networks can have different rules, participants, and operating models, so one network’s design should not be presented as a universal template for the market.

Applications and services provide ways for people and organizations to access or use parts of an ecosystem. A software interface, for example, can help a user interact with a network, while other services may support data access or asset-related workflows. These components are distinct from the protocol rules themselves, even when an application depends on a protocol to complete an action.

Some crypto-related infrastructure also reaches beyond software. One research architecture for decentralized physical infrastructure networks describes coordination across physical devices, communications and data handling, blockchain records, incentives, and application services. Implementations may combine, omit, or centralize parts of these layers. The example illustrates that an ecosystem can include physical and operational components, not just tokens and code; it should not be treated as a description of every crypto system. [source](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02239>)

Cross-chain systems illustrate another important distinction. A bridge can coordinate activity between separate ledgers, but an asset is not physically moved from one ledger to another. Depending on the design, minting, burning, locking, releasing, or swapping mechanisms coordinate value across chains; the exact accounting and redemption path depends on that bridge design. This is a particular infrastructure pattern, not a requirement for every digital asset or network. [source](<https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/scaling/sidechains/>)

## Who participates and what they do

A working map distinguishes participants by function rather than treating “the market” as one actor:

- **Developers** create or maintain protocol, application, or infrastructure software. Their work may concern different layers, and building software does not alone establish how a deployed system is governed or operated.
- **Network operators** run components that support network activity. The exact operational role depends on the network design. Some systems use validators under protocol-defined rules; other systems may assign block production or validation functions differently.
- **Users** initiate or receive activity through a network, application, or service. Their interaction path depends on the tools and arrangements they use.
- **Custodians and other service providers** may support access to assets or provide related services. Their functions are not the same thing as protocol rules, and the specific responsibilities depend on the particular service and arrangement.
- **Infrastructure providers** can support connectivity, data handling, or other operational needs. A system may rely on several such components or organize them differently.

These roles can overlap. An organization may build software and also provide a service; a user may access a protocol through an application; and an application may depend on infrastructure operated by other parties. A role label is a functional description, not a guarantee of reliability, compliance, or asset value. Those conclusions require separate evidence about the relevant system and participants.

For operational analysis, ask which participant performs each function and where responsibilities change hands. A network operator’s role does not automatically include the work of an application provider or custodian. Likewise, a service provider’s role should not be assumed to describe what the underlying protocol accepts or records. The distinction helps identify dependencies without assuming that one participant controls the entire ecosystem.

## How market activity moves through infrastructure

A general interaction can be traced as a sequence: a user or application initiates an action; software prepares or routes it to a relevant service or network; and the applicable network rules govern how activity is processed or recorded. Other components may provide access, interfaces, data, or operational support. This is a conceptual map, not a fixed workflow: systems differ in how they assign these tasks and which components are involved.

The protocol-service boundary is useful at each step. Protocol rules describe what a network handles and how it records activity. An application or provider may influence how a request is prepared, presented, or routed, but that does not make its behavior a protocol rule. Conversely, describing a protocol does not explain every service arrangement a user may encounter before or after network processing.

When tracing a particular interaction, identify the initiating user or application, the software and services in the path, the network whose rules apply, and any additional infrastructure on which the path depends. Then distinguish what is specified by the protocol from what is supplied by a provider or chosen by a user. This makes the map useful for locating dependencies while avoiding the assumption that all systems follow the same steps.

Architecture also affects the kinds of operational dependencies to examine. Systems may distribute or combine components in different ways, and a stated design goal should not be confused with an established outcome. Scaling work, for instance, may seek to increase transaction speed and throughput without sacrificing decentralization or security; that is an objective, not a universal result or a score for a particular system. [source](<https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/scaling/>)

## What this ecosystem view does—and does not—tell you

An ecosystem view can help explain what the components are, who performs different functions, and how an interaction can depend on protocols, applications, services, and infrastructure. It does not establish an asset’s price, value, investment merit, or expected performance. Nor does describing a technical system settle legal classification, regulatory obligations, tax treatment, access, fees, yields, security, recovery, or compliance.

Those matters require evidence specific to the asset, product, service, system, time, and—where relevant—jurisdiction. This explainer remains jurisdiction-neutral and does not make those determinations. For a concrete operational question, first identify the exact network, application, and provider involved; then assess claims about their roles and conditions separately. The ecosystem map is a starting point for understanding structure, not a substitute for evidence about a specific arrangement.

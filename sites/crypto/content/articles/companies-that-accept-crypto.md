For a company, accepting cryptocurrency means offering customers a way to pay with a crypto asset; it does not necessarily mean the business keeps that asset. A payment may be received directly or handled by an intermediary, and the asset the customer sends may differ from what the business ultimately receives or retains. To understand a company’s model, identify who handles the payment, what the business receives, and which responsibilities and conditions apply to that particular arrangement.

## What it means for a company to accept crypto

“Accepting crypto” describes a customer payment option, not one uniform operating model. It is also distinct from holding cryptocurrency as a treasury asset: offering crypto payments does not, by itself, reveal whether a company keeps or converts incoming funds. The available evidence here does not establish how any particular company handles its receipts.

A payment involves a digital asset transferred under a network’s rules. In Bitcoin, digital signatures authorize transfers through signed ownership records, while transaction-validation and double-spend-prevention rules are also part of the payment system. The signature mechanism is therefore one component of a network transaction, not a complete description of how a business receives, records, or accepts payment. [[source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>)] [[source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2018/cryptocurrencies-looking-beyond-hype>)]

A business might receive a payment itself or use a service that handles some part of the process. The word “intermediary” alone does not identify who controls the funds, what asset the business receives, or how the arrangement handles exceptions. Those details depend on the specific service and its terms.

Support for a payment method should not be treated as a permanent or universal property of a company. It may depend on the particular product, location, asset, and service arrangement. This article does not provide a company directory or establish that any named company currently accepts a particular asset.

## How payment and settlement models differ

The key distinction is between the asset the customer sends and the asset the business ultimately receives or retains. Here, **settlement** means the outcome the business treats as completion of its payment arrangement. A customer may send crypto even when the business’s arrangement results in a different asset being received or retained. The specific path depends on the service and its terms; the evidence available here does not establish a general conversion process, settlement time, or guarantee.

In a direct-receipt model, the business receives crypto without an intermediary handling that receipt. The business then needs to understand what its own records treat as completion and which people or systems are responsible for handling the payment. The allowed evidence does not establish a universal wallet setup, access-control model, or recovery procedure, so those details need to be assessed for the actual arrangement.

In an intermediary-mediated model, a third party handles some part of the payment process. Do not assume from the word “processor” that conversion occurs, that the business receives a particular asset, or that refunds or reversals work in a standard way. Verify those terms in documentation and the agreement for the specific service. The available evidence does not establish one standard payment-provider workflow, fee, conversion rate, or settlement schedule.

A centralized crypto exchange offers a useful but limited example of why roles matter: an exchange user typically transfers funds and their custody to the exchange before trading on its internal order book. This describes a trading venue, not a universal payment-processing arrangement, and should not be generalized to a payment service without specific evidence. [[source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>)]

Neither direct receipt nor intermediary handling is universally available or preferable. A business evaluating either model should identify who receives the customer’s transfer, what asset the customer sends, what asset the business expects to receive or retain, and what documented conditions determine completion. Keep that payment choice separate from the company’s treasury decision about whether to retain crypto.

## Operational questions to assess before choosing a model

Start by assigning responsibilities. Which party determines whether a payment meets the business’s acceptance conditions? Who handles a delayed or incomplete payment, customer questions, or a refund request? The available evidence does not establish a universal refund right, reversal mechanism, or customer-support arrangement. The business needs to determine these matters from its own process and the specific service terms.

Next, map custody and access responsibilities. If a service holds or controls funds, establish what it actually does and what the agreement says. If the business receives crypto directly, it should identify who is responsible for access and payment handling. The evidence here does not specify a universal security control, key-recovery method, or incident-response process; these are operational questions to document rather than assume.

Accounting and reconciliation also depend on the payment path. A business should decide what event it records as completion, what asset and amount it records, and how it connects a customer order to the relevant payment information. The allowed evidence does not prescribe accounting treatment, required business records, or a universal reconciliation procedure. Those questions need to be resolved for the company’s circumstances with appropriate professional input.

Fees, supported assets, conversion, and settlement timing require direct verification. The allowed evidence does not support a general fee level, processing speed, supported-asset list, or availability claim. Check current documentation for the exact product and location, and distinguish estimates or descriptions from the conditions in the governing agreement.

A further question is what obligations the business must meet and in which asset. For designated financial market infrastructures, the Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures call for sufficient liquid resources in relevant currencies to meet settlement obligations under normal and stressed conditions. This standard applies to designated financial market infrastructures; it is not a rule that automatically governs every company accepting crypto. It illustrates why a business should identify its own payment obligations and the conditions under which they must be met. [[source](<https://www.bis.org/committees/cpmi/pfmi/overview>)]

## Jurisdiction, regulation, tax, and compliance need local verification

The phrase “accepts crypto” does not, on its own, answer legal or compliance questions. The available evidence does not establish a universal rule across jurisdictions or determine whether a particular business needs a license, how its payments are taxed, or which compliance duties apply. This article does not provide a legal, tax, or regulatory conclusion.

A narrow U.S. example shows why the exact activity matters. FinCEN generally considers certain convertible virtual currency kiosk operators—those facilitating transmission between an exchanger and a customer wallet or operating as an exchanger—to be money services businesses under the U.S. Bank Secrecy Act and subject to applicable registration, recordkeeping, reporting, and transaction-monitoring obligations. That claim concerns those specified kiosk and exchanger activities in the United States; it is not a classification for every business that accepts cryptocurrency, and it does not establish obligations elsewhere. [[source](<https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2025-08/FinCEN-Notice-CVCKIOSK.pdf>)]

For a real decision, identify the business’s location, customer locations, and the exact roles of the business and any service provider. Then seek current, jurisdiction-specific evidence about legal status, licensing, tax, and compliance. Where those issues matter, consult appropriately qualified advisers rather than treating a general description of payment models as an answer.

## A practical checklist for evaluating crypto acceptance

Use these questions to frame an initial assessment, not as a checkout implementation or legal checklist:

- **Purpose:** What business purpose or customer use case would offering crypto address?
- **Payment path:** Does the business receive the payment directly, or does a third party handle part of the flow?
- **Asset outcome:** What asset does the customer send, and what asset does the business expect to receive or retain?
- **Responsibilities:** Who handles payment status, exceptions, customer support, and refund requests under the documented arrangement?
- **Custody and access:** Who controls relevant funds or accounts, and what documented security and recovery arrangements apply?
- **Commercial terms:** What current documentation confirms supported assets and locations, fees, conversion, settlement conditions, and timing?
- **Records:** What does the business treat as completion, and how will it record and reconcile the payment?
- **Local review:** What current, jurisdiction-specific evidence and professional advice address legal, regulatory, tax, and compliance questions?

A company accepts crypto by including a crypto payment option in its customer-facing arrangement, but that label does not determine custody, conversion, settlement, or compliance. Those choices—and the responsibilities that follow—must be checked against the specific business, service, and jurisdiction.

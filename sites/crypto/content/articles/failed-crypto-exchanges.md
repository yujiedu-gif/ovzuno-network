A centralized crypto exchange can stop or restrict services when its trading, custody, or other supporting functions are impaired, but a service interruption alone does not establish why it happened or whether the operator is insolvent. To assess a case, first identify the affected service, then separate observed effects from proposed causes and eventual outcomes.

## What counts as a failed crypto exchange?

A centralized exchange is a market venue that may combine customer trading with other activities. Some operators may also conduct proprietary trading, margin lending, token issuance, and custody; not every exchange performs every function. Because roles can be combined, a problem described simply as “the exchange failed” may conceal different conditions across its services. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>)

For a practical analysis, distinguish among a temporary outage, an ongoing restriction, and the cessation of a service. The evidence available here does not supply a universal duration or threshold that defines when an interruption becomes a failure or closure. Those labels therefore require case-specific facts about what stopped, how broadly it was affected, and what the operator’s status is.

A service interruption is also different from insolvency, misconduct, or technical failure. An outage may be observable without its cause being known. Insolvency is a separate claim about the operator’s ability to meet obligations, and misconduct is a separate claim about conduct. Neither follows automatically from users being unable to trade or withdraw.

## Failed crypto exchanges: the main failure mechanisms

### Operational dependencies and combined roles

When an operator combines trading with custody or other activities, its functions may have operational dependencies or conflicts. A disruption in one area could matter to another, but the evidence here does not identify any particular operator’s shared systems or establish that a specific exchange failure resulted from such a dependency. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>)

A useful first step is to name the affected function rather than infer a broad cause. For example, evidence that trading access is unavailable establishes an access problem, not necessarily a custody failure. Conversely, visible account information does not by itself establish that trading and withdrawals are operating normally. Assess each service independently where the records permit.

### Custody and customer access

In a typical centralized-exchange model, customers transfer assets into the exchange’s custody before using its internal trading system. This makes custody relevant when considering what a disruption could mean for user access: a user may depend on the exchange’s service to access assets used on that venue. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>)

That general model does not determine how a particular exchange holds assets, what rights a particular customer has, or what happens to assets after a failure. Those questions require evidence about the platform and arrangement. It is also important not to treat trading, account access, and withdrawals as one service. Evidence that one is impaired does not automatically show what is happening with the others.

### Liquidity and withdrawal concerns

Market liquidity is multidimensional. It includes the time and expense of buying or selling, such as spread, transaction cost, price impact, and immediacy. These dimensions can vary with transaction size and market conditions. [source](<https://www.bis.org/speeches/20160502-market-and-funding-liquidity-overview>)

Withdrawal difficulty and market liquidity should not be conflated. A delay in transferring assets is an observable service concern, while liquidity describes the conditions for buying or selling. The available evidence does not establish that a withdrawal delay proves an exchange lacks assets or is insolvent. A cause requires additional, reliable records; a customer report or platform notice should not be expanded into a conclusion it does not support.

### Governance and external dependencies

Governance, controls, counterparties, and external service providers may be relevant lines of inquiry when examining a particular breakdown. But the allowed evidence does not document a specific exchange’s governance arrangements, external dependencies, or control failures. Nor does it establish that any such issue caused a named platform to stop operating. Treat these as questions to investigate, not default explanations.

## How a breakdown can affect users and market infrastructure

For users, an immediate consequence may be that a particular exchange service is unavailable or restricted. Because customers typically transfer assets into exchange custody before trading on the venue’s internal system, a custody disruption may affect access through that venue. The general model does not establish whether withdrawals are available in a particular case, which account records remain accessible, or how an operator will respond. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>)

Trading effects should also be described precisely. If a venue’s trading service is impaired, users may not be able to use that service as expected; the evidence here does not establish the scale or duration of any specific interruption. Nor can an effect on one venue be assumed to describe every venue or the wider market.

Market infrastructure can be assessed through the same discipline: distinguish the venue’s service from broader trading conditions. Liquidity includes time to transact, spread, transaction cost, price impact, and immediacy, rather than a single measure. Those dimensions help frame what evidence to examine, but they do not prove a market-wide consequence from an unnamed or unverified event. [source](<https://www.bis.org/speeches/20160502-market-and-funding-liquidity-overview>)

Keep immediate service effects separate from eventual financial or legal outcomes. The evidence available here does not establish the treatment of customer assets, recovery, or consequences for counterparties in any particular case. Those outcomes cannot be inferred from service interruption alone.

## What evidence supports a cause-of-failure assessment?

Begin with a dated, specific account of what stopped working. Identify whether the claim concerns trading, custody, withdrawals, or another service, and distinguish what users observed from what the operator says caused it. The evidence allowed for this article does not include records of a named incident, specific official findings, or independently corroborated reporting. It therefore cannot support a case-specific causal conclusion.

Keep the evidence categories distinct:

- **Observed service effect:** what available records show was unavailable, restricted, or delayed.
- **Operator explanation:** what the exchange says happened, attributed as its statement rather than treated as a confirmed cause.
- **Official allegation or finding:** a claim or conclusion attributed to the relevant authority, with its status preserved.
- **Independent corroboration:** records or reporting that substantiate specific facts beyond a single account.

Then evaluate each explanation against the evidence. A withdrawal delay does not by itself prove insolvency, and an outage does not by itself prove misconduct. The claims available for this analysis do not provide a method for determining the solvency of a specific exchange, so that conclusion must not be supplied by inference. Similarly, the fact that exchanges may combine functions does not establish that a particular operator’s governance or controls failed.

## Why exchange failure patterns do not establish what happens next

Category-level patterns can help readers frame questions, but they do not establish the cause or outcome of another exchange’s difficulties. The evidence used here supports limited statements about exchange functions, a typical custody arrangement, and dimensions of market liquidity. It does not document a particular failure or determine what will happen to a platform, customer, counterparty, or market.

Customer treatment and recovery depend on case-specific facts beyond this analysis. This article does not provide case-specific recovery instructions, jurisdictional remedies, exchange rankings, or price forecasts. Its narrower conclusion is to identify the affected service, separate observations from explanations, and require evidence before drawing conclusions about insolvency, misconduct, or eventual outcomes.

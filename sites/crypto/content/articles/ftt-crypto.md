## What FTT is and how it relates to FTX

FTT and FTX refer to different things: FTT is the token, while FTX refers to the wider ecosystem with which it was associated. That distinction matters because evidence about a token’s existence or technical behavior does not, by itself, establish that an exchange, affiliated organization, or service is operating or supporting it now. The available evidence does not identify FTT’s issuer or responsible entity, or establish the precise organizational relationship between the token and FTX. Those details should not be supplied by inference.

A token is not the same as the service or organization associated with it. Its on-chain record and functions, a platform’s policies, and any arrangements made by an organization are separate subjects requiring their own evidence. A description of the token might document a design intention, while a service announcement might describe a feature offered at a particular time. Neither source automatically proves that the other remains applicable.

Technical standards also have limited scope. ERC-20 defines a common interface for fungible tokens, including functions and events for total supply, balances, transfers, allowances, and approvals. That interface does not establish a token’s issuance policy, backing, market value, compliance, or code safety. The evidence provided here does not establish that FTT used ERC-20, so ERC-20 should not be presented as FTT’s standard. It illustrates why a token-standard label, even when confirmed, would not prove a platform benefit or present-day service access. [source](<https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-20>)

Ask whether the source describes an on-chain function, a benefit offered by an application, or a promise made by an organization. A historical description also differs from a current availability statement. A dated document can support the narrow claim that a function was described at that time. ## How to evaluate claims about FTT utility and status

Start with the source itself. Check who published it, when it was published, and whether it refers to FTT specifically. Read the exact wording rather than relying on a headline, snippet, or later paraphrase. A first-party description may document what its author said, but its date and scope still matter: an announcement of intended design is not proof of ongoing operation.

Next, classify the statement. Is it about token mechanics, access to a service, a benefit offered by a platform, or a legal or contractual right? Do not use evidence for one category to establish another. For example, a standard that describes a token interface would not, by itself, establish an exchange’s current policy or a holder’s redemption rights. Similarly, evidence that a token exists on a blockchain would not demonstrate that an application currently accepts it.

For any claim involving a chain or token representation, verify the exact network and version. Stablecoin deposit support provides one general illustration of why symbols alone can be insufficient: such support can be specific to a chain and token representation, and unsupported or bridged variants can go uncredited or be unrecoverable. This does not establish anything about FTT support at any provider. It shows why a current service’s own documentation must identify the particular asset and network before a support claim is treated as confirmed. [source](<https://developers.circle.com/circle-mint/supported-chains-and-currencies>)

For a status claim, look for current confirmation that names both the relevant organization and the exact service or function. A general reference to an ecosystem, a token listing, or an older product description may not answer whether a particular feature is available. If sources conflict or do not state the time period, describe the point as unverified rather than resolving the gap through assumption.

Crypto-card terms describe different concepts: the digital asset involved, records or access methods, and payment functions such as approval and completion. Their meanings do not establish how a particular card works, what it supports, or whether it is available to a reader. This glossary defines only the terms supported by the available evidence; it does not supply product-specific card definitions or transaction instructions.

## Crypto terms in a card-spending context

Crypto-funded spending may bring together an asset, a record of ownership or transfer, and payment-related steps. These are distinct ideas rather than parts of one universal product design. A term used by a service may also have a narrower meaning in that service’s documentation.

The distinction matters when interpreting a description of a card. A label alone does not show where an asset is held, how a purchase is funded, or which party performs a payment function. Those questions require current documentation for the particular arrangement. This article does not compare providers or assess card features.

## Assets, tokens, wallets, and custody

A **crypto asset** is a broad phrase, but the available evidence here supports a narrower definition of cryptocurrency: a digital asset, credit, or unit that can be cryptographically transferred between users of a blockchain network. That definition is specific to cryptocurrency in a blockchain network; it should not be treated as a definition of every product or legal category described as a digital asset.

A **transaction output** is a Bitcoin-specific record that can be spent by a later transaction. In Bitcoin’s unspent-transaction-output model, an input identifies a particular earlier output using its transaction ID and output index. New outputs remain unspent until a later input spends them. This describes how that Bitcoin transaction model represents spendable outputs, not how every network or service records balances.

To validate a Bitcoin transaction input, the referenced output must be unspent and the input must satisfy its spending conditions, including the required scripts and signatures. This is a protocol-specific explanation. It does not establish who controls a wallet or what a card provider’s account display represents.

A **wallet** is often used in everyday conversation to mean an app or place where crypto is kept. The evidence available for this glossary does not authorize a general definition of wallets or custody, so those terms should not be inferred from the word “card” or from a displayed balance. In particular, terminology alone cannot establish whether a service uses a wallet, an internal account record, or another arrangement. Consult the relevant product documentation to determine what its labels mean.

## Conversion, authorization, and settlement

**Conversion** can refer to changing one asset into another, such as exchanging a digital asset for a payment currency. The available evidence supports a specific Visa-described program model: some Visa-facilitated crypto card programs may convert digital assets to local fiat at the point of sale, while merchants receive standard payments through Visa’s existing settlement process. Visa’s description is not a universal rule for all crypto-linked cards or networks; conversion timing and the merchant-side payment path may differ in other products.

A different product description gives another possible funding arrangement: Crypto.com describes card funding that may involve topping up with fiat balances or converting supported cryptocurrency to fiat before loading the card. The available funding methods and eligible assets depend on product and location, and the cited help article describes US-specific options. This is an example of product-specific terminology, not a general definition of card funding.

**Authorization** and **settlement** are often used to refer to separate payment functions, but the evidence available here does not support a general technical definition of either term across card arrangements. Visa’s program description does distinguish a merchant receiving a standard payment through its existing settlement process from the possible conversion of digital assets at the point of sale. That description should be kept within its stated program scope rather than generalized to every card.

## Balances, fees, and transaction records

A displayed **balance** is a label that needs context. The available evidence does not establish a universal distinction between an available balance, a crypto holding, and a service-account balance. For example, a card app may expose information and controls such as card details, fees, limits, and applicable terms, but the actual features should be confirmed in the user’s app and applicable program. The presence of a balance label alone does not explain what record it refers to or what use is permitted.

A **fee** is a charge associated with a product or activity, but the evidence here does not support a universal fee definition or claim that a given step always has a charge. Crypto.com’s app guide identifies fees, limits, and applicable terms as information that may be accessible in the app; the actual features should be checked there and against the applicable program. A phrase such as “zero-fee” therefore needs product-specific context, including what service or activity the phrase covers. This glossary does not state any fee amount.

A **transaction record** may refer to information about a particular event, but a record’s scope depends on the system that produces it. The available evidence does not define a universal card or crypto transaction record, nor establish that one record includes every related step. Readers should distinguish a product’s displayed information from broader assumptions about what it documents.

## Terms that need context before they can be relied on

Words such as **instant**, **zero-fee**, **debit**, and **cashback** should be treated as product-specific labels unless current documentation defines them for the particular program. The available evidence does not establish universal meanings for these terms, timing guarantees, fee outcomes, or reward conditions. A label alone is not enough to determine what a specific card does.

Likewise, references to an **issuer**, **card network**, or **merchant** identify roles in a payment context, but do not by themselves explain every party’s responsibilities in a particular arrangement. The Visa description supports only the specific point that merchants in the described programs receive standard payments through Visa’s existing settlement process. Other product details need to be verified in the documentation for the relevant card and program.

The practical reading rule is to keep terms separate: a digital asset is not the same thing as a transaction record; a Bitcoin output is a network-specific spendable record; conversion describes a possible asset change; and card-program terms depend on the particular arrangement. This glossary does not establish card availability, eligibility, fees, regulation, tax treatment, or compliance. Those questions require current, product- and jurisdiction-specific evidence.

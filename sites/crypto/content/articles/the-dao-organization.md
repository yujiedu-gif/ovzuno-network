A DAO’s governance system is only one layer of the organization around it: rules may define how proposals are considered and decisions executed, while people and off-chain arrangements may handle the work those decisions require. To assess a particular DAO, examine its documented governance process separately from its contributors, responsibilities, and any legal or operational arrangements. The DAO label alone does not settle those questions.

## What “the DAO organization” can mean

“The DAO organization” can refer to a governance system, the people coordinating around that system, or both. These are related ideas, but they are not interchangeable. A governance process describes how specified decisions are proposed, considered, and potentially carried out. The broader organization includes the human work and arrangements involved in reaching and implementing decisions.

DAO governance can use proposals, voting, delegation, token-based or share-based membership, reputation, or multisignature execution. Which combination applies—and what rights it confers—depends on the DAO’s contracts, policies, and legal context. The label does not establish a single organizational model or, by itself, determine legal status.

Documented systems illustrate this variation. A comparison of ENS, MAP Protocol, and ArbitrumDAO governance documentation finds differences in proposal types, eligibility, stages, thresholds, voting windows, and execution mechanisms. Those examples show variation among the systems examined; they do not describe every DAO or establish a universal template.

For a reader evaluating one organization, the practical distinction is between what its rules specify and what the surrounding people and arrangements do. A contract or governance document may describe a decision path, but that description alone does not establish who performs related work, under what arrangement, or with what accountability. Those are separate questions to verify against the DAO’s own documentation and other relevant evidence.

## Where on-chain governance meets off-chain work

On-chain governance refers here to decision rules and actions recorded or carried out through a blockchain-based system. Off-chain work is work that is not fully represented by those on-chain steps. The distinction helps prevent a proposal, vote, or execution transaction from being mistaken for the entirety of an organization’s operations.

Governance models may include proposals, voting, delegation, or multisignature execution, but the specific rights and execution paths depend on the system’s contracts and policies as well as its legal context. The presence of a vote does not, without examining applicable rules, tell a reader who may submit a proposal, who may participate, or how a decision takes effect. Those details should be checked in the documentation for the particular DAO rather than inferred from the general label.

The documented examples also show why process details matter. MAP Protocol documentation describes a proposal lifecycle involving submission, approval, referendum, and an execution phase for proposals that meet required conditions. ArbitrumDAO documentation describes formal on-chain voting after sufficient support at an earlier stage, with eligible holders or delegates voting. These are protocol-specific descriptions, not general rules for all DAOs.

A documented on-chain sequence should not be read as proof that all related organizational work occurs on-chain. Research, communication, implementation, and oversight are useful categories to investigate when reviewing a DAO, but the available evidence here does not establish who performs those tasks, what agreements govern them, or how any particular organization handles them. Those questions require DAO-specific documentation rather than assumptions drawn from its voting process.

It is useful to keep three things distinct: the decision, the recorded governance action, and any work needed after that action. Documentation may specify a vote or an execution condition without identifying all people involved in preparation or follow-through. Likewise, evidence that someone performs work should not automatically be treated as evidence that the person has authority under the DAO’s governance rules. Each connection needs support from the relevant materials.

## Questions to ask about responsibilities and authority

A practical review can begin by separating the decision path from the work around it. For each important decision, look for documentation that answers these questions:

- Who can propose or otherwise initiate the decision, and who is eligible to participate in the relevant approval or vote?
- What stages or conditions apply before an outcome can be executed?
- Which person, group, or mechanism carries out the decision, if execution is specified?
- Which responsibilities are explicitly documented, and which depend on arrangements not described in governance materials?
- What review or follow-up process is documented if execution is delayed, disputed, or incomplete?

These are verification questions, not assumptions that every DAO assigns authority in the same way. For example, the MAP Protocol process description identifies stages and conditions for execution, while the ArbitrumDAO FAQ describes an earlier support stage followed by formal on-chain voting. Neither example should be generalized into a claim about how every DAO assigns responsibility for implementation or handles failures.

When the documentation does not answer a question, record it as unresolved rather than filling the gap with assumptions. A governance page, for example, might clarify the stages of a vote but not establish the responsibilities of contributors or the terms of any off-chain arrangement. The absence of a detail in the materials reviewed is a limit on what can be concluded; it is not proof that a particular arrangement does or does not exist.

Voting power is another organizational consideration, but findings need careful scope. Empirical studies have examined concentration, contributor influence, and shifts in voting power in selected governance systems. Such findings are sample-specific, not universal measurements. They can prompt a reader to inspect participation and influence in the DAO under review, but they do not establish the distribution of power in a different DAO.

## Legal status and jurisdiction require separate evidence

Governance documentation and organizational terminology are not substitutes for jurisdiction-specific legal analysis. Legal recognition, liability, regulatory treatment, and tax obligations depend on applicable law and specific facts. The evidence available for this article does not establish the legal status or obligations of any particular DAO, so no conclusion about those matters follows from the existence of on-chain governance or a DAO label.

Before relying on a claim about a DAO’s entity status, liability, compliance, or tax treatment, seek current, authoritative information for the relevant jurisdiction and facts, with qualified review where appropriate. A protocol’s voting rules can document governance mechanics; they do not, by themselves, answer those separate legal questions. A reader should therefore avoid treating a governance description as an answer to a legal question, or applying a conclusion about one jurisdiction or organization to another.

The same evidentiary discipline applies to operational or financial claims. DAO terminology does not establish a service’s availability, fees, yields, security, or recovery outcomes. Each such claim would need separate, current evidence about the specific service or arrangement. This article does not make those claims, and the existence of a governance process is not evidence for them.

## A bounded framework for evaluating a DAO organization

Keep four layers distinct when reviewing a DAO: documented governance rules; people and responsibilities; off-chain arrangements; and any claimed legal form. For each layer, record what the relevant documentation actually says, what it leaves unresolved, and what requires independent verification.

Then compare the layers without treating one as proof of another. A vote or execution procedure may establish a defined governance step, but it does not alone identify every contributor, explain all operational responsibilities, or resolve legal status. Conversely, evidence about a person’s or service provider’s role should not be treated as proof of a particular on-chain authority unless the governing rules support that connection.

A useful review note can separate findings into three categories: documented, not established by the materials reviewed, and requiring separate evidence. For example, a protocol-specific process may document proposal stages or voting eligibility. The available documentation may not establish who performs associated work. Legal or financial assertions may require current evidence beyond the governance materials. This format makes it easier to preserve the difference between a recorded rule, a question left open, and a conclusion that would need further support.

The resulting assessment should remain specific to the DAO and materials examined. Governance processes vary across documented systems, and research findings about voting power apply to the samples studied. A careful account therefore distinguishes documented mechanisms from organizational questions that remain unanswered—and treats those unanswered questions as limits on what can responsibly be concluded.

ETH, or ether, is Ethereum’s native cryptocurrency: Ethereum is the network, while ETH is the asset used within it. The distinction matters because the network’s rules and services are not themselves the currency, and not every asset that operates on Ethereum is ETH. At the protocol level, ETH is part of Ethereum’s market for computation and is used as economic collateral for validator participation in proof of stake. These functions describe network mechanics, not a promise of financial results or a conclusion about ETH’s value.

## What ETH crypto means

ETH is the ticker commonly used for ether, Ethereum’s native cryptocurrency. In a technical sense, cryptocurrency can refer to a digital asset, credit, or unit that users can transfer cryptographically within a blockchain network. Ethereum is the network; ETH is its native asset. The terms are related, but they do not mean the same thing.

A network is not just its currency. A cryptocurrency system can include protocol rules, a transaction ledger, and participants who update, store, and read that ledger. Some systems support peer-to-peer digital exchange through distributed record-keeping instead of a conventional central transaction processor. That description helps explain the system around a cryptocurrency, but it does not make the system itself an asset.

This difference is useful when interpreting descriptions of Ethereum. A statement about Ethereum may refer to its network rules or operation; a statement about ETH refers to the native asset. A transaction can use Ethereum’s network and involve ETH, but the network and the asset remain distinct concepts. Nor does the word “cryptocurrency” by itself establish a particular asset’s price, suitability, or rights.

## What ETH does on Ethereum

ETH has a protocol role in Ethereum’s market for computation. Ethereum transactions that request network execution use computational resources, and ETH is the asset connected to that market. This is why ETH is commonly associated with network fees. The exact fee mechanics and amounts are not specified by this definition, and no fee amount should be assumed from ETH’s role alone.

ETH also serves as economic collateral for Ethereum validator participation in proof of stake. Instead of proof-of-work mining, Ethereum’s validator model uses ETH deposits that can be subject to protocol penalties, including slashing under specified conditions. In this context, collateral is an asset committed to support participation under network rules. The mechanism describes how the protocol organizes validator participation; it does not establish that a particular participant will receive a reward or achieve a financial outcome.

The base protocol role should also be distinguished from services offered by a third party. A service that handles staking or other interactions can add its own custody, smart-contract, operator, and legal risks beyond those of base protocol participation. This article does not assess any provider or service. It identifies only the protocol-level function of ETH as collateral in Ethereum proof of stake.

Together, computation-related use and validator collateral explain why ETH is integral to Ethereum’s protocol. They do not establish a fixed fee, a guaranteed return, or a particular result for every transaction or participant. Protocol functions describe what an asset does within a system, not what it is worth outside that technical role.

## ETH and Ethereum-based tokens

Ethereum also supports smart contracts: programs with code and data at network addresses that users can invoke by submitting transactions. Such contracts can create or manage other assets. Those assets may use Ethereum, but that fact does not make them ETH. ETH is the network’s native cryptocurrency; contract-created assets are distinct from it.

The distinction is not a claim that all contract-created assets work alike. Token designs can represent different functions or things, including financial assets, access-related uses, or governance-related uses. Those examples describe possible designs, not proof of legal ownership, enforceable claims, backing, redemption, or value. A token’s name or description is not enough to establish those properties.

ETH, by contrast, is identified here by its relationship to the Ethereum protocol as its native asset. It is not an ERC token merely because it is used on Ethereum. Token standards and the details of contract interfaces are separate subjects; they are not necessary to define ETH or its core protocol roles.

## What this definition does not establish

A technical definition explains ETH’s relationship to Ethereum and identifies functions it serves in the protocol. It does not establish market value, investment suitability, or future performance. It also does not establish that use of ETH will produce any particular outcome. No price or investment conclusion follows from describing ETH’s protocol roles.

This explanation also does not determine legal or tax treatment, custody arrangements, or availability in any particular place. Those matters require separate information relevant to a person’s jurisdiction and circumstances. The protocol-level definition alone cannot resolve them.

The bounded takeaway is straightforward: Ethereum is the network, and ETH is its native cryptocurrency. ETH is connected to Ethereum’s market for computation and is used as collateral for validator participation under proof of stake. Those facts explain ETH’s role in the protocol without equating it with Ethereum, contract-created tokens, or a guaranteed financial result.

Online crypto exchange infrastructure connects user access to trading-related services and, depending on the venue and product, to separate asset-handling or settlement arrangements. These functions should be evaluated as distinct parts of a system: information about a transaction does not, by itself, prove that assets moved or settlement occurred. The available evidence supports a limited conceptual map, not a universal architecture; operators and researchers should verify the specific responsibilities and processes for each venue.

## What an online exchange connects

An exchange is more useful to analyze as connected services than as a single screen. A user-facing service can be considered separately from the systems that process trading activity and from any arrangements that handle assets. Those parts may fit within one provider or involve separate parties, but the evidence available here does not establish which structure a particular online exchange uses.

The distinction between a trade-related record and asset movement is central. A record can describe what participants propose to exchange without demonstrating that a transfer took place or that a settlement obligation was completed. Seaport provides one concrete example: an order represents assets offered by one side and consideration required from the fulfilling side. This illustrates how an order can express terms; it is not evidence that all exchanges use this model, nor does it establish custody or settlement arrangements. [source](<https://docs.opensea.io/docs/seaport>)

Researchers should also keep the venue’s infrastructure separate from the broader digital-asset ecosystem. The available claims do not identify the specific networks, providers, or systems connected to any online exchange. Treat those relationships as items to investigate rather than assuming that a displayed service includes every necessary function.

## Crypto exchange online infrastructure: the main layers

A practical starting map has four areas: user access and accounts, trading-related services, records and information, and asset handling or settlement. This is an organizing framework for research, not a statement that every venue has four separate technical systems. A provider may combine functions, or responsibilities may sit with different parties; the evidence here does not resolve the design of any individual venue.

The trading area concerns activity such as a user’s instructions or a marketplace order. Seaport’s order structure is useful only as a specific illustration: it records what is offered and what is required in return. It does not show who holds the assets, how a transfer is authorized, or which event completes settlement. Keeping those questions separate prevents an order description from being mistaken for proof of asset handling. [source](<https://docs.opensea.io/docs/seaport>)

Records and information form another research area. Ask what the venue records about user activity and what those records establish. The evidence supplied for this article does not describe exchange account records, data services, or monitoring systems, so it cannot support claims about their contents or functions. The right approach is to identify the relevant documentation for the venue and product under review.

Asset handling deserves separate attention. Identify whether the product places assets with the venue, another party, or a transfer process. The allowed evidence does not specify which of these arrangements any online exchange uses. It also does not establish asset segregation, withdrawal processes, recovery procedures, security controls, fees, or availability. Those are product-specific questions requiring current evidence.

## How information and assets move through the system

At a high level, a user starts an activity through an access point, a relevant service processes information about it, and any asset movement follows a path determined by the product’s design. This is a way to structure investigation, not a verified sequence for every venue. The evidence available here does not establish the particular checks, records, or settlement conditions used by online exchanges.

Follow information and assets as separate tracks. On the information track, determine what the user submitted and what record the service created. On the asset track, determine whether any transfer is required, which party handles it, and what evidence indicates completion. Seaport supports only the narrow distinction that an order represents offered assets and required consideration; it does not answer those questions for an online exchange. [source](<https://docs.opensea.io/docs/seaport>)

A kiosk example further shows why service descriptions should not be generalized. In the United States, a convertible virtual currency kiosk allows customers to exchange fiat currency for virtual currency; a supported two-way kiosk can also allow virtual currency to be exchanged for fiat. This describes a kiosk service, not the architecture or operating path of online exchanges generally. [source](<https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2025-08/FinCEN-Notice-CVCKIOSK.pdf>)

Similarly, do not infer a settlement route from a trading interface or asset label. The permitted evidence does not establish which networks or transfer mechanisms a particular venue supports. For a specific product, verify the exact asset representation, network, and stated settlement process rather than assuming that a name or screen description reveals them.

## Operational controls and dependencies

Operational review should distinguish responsibility for access, trading-related activity, records, and asset handling. For each function, ask which organization performs it and what documentation demonstrates that role. The evidence available here does not verify any venue’s service availability, access controls, monitoring, incident response, custody protections, or recovery processes. An online interface alone cannot substantiate those capabilities.

Where a product depends on an external service or network, identify the dependency and its role. Then look for current documentation explaining what happens if that service is unavailable or an activity is not processed. The allowed claims do not establish particular dependencies or contingency plans for online exchanges, so the analysis must remain venue- and product-specific.

Claims about jurisdiction, legal status, or compliance also require separate verification. No jurisdiction-specific authorization or compliance obligation for an online exchange is supported by the claims available here. Consult current authoritative sources for the relevant jurisdiction and the venue’s actual activities before relying on such a statement. Likewise, this evidence does not substantiate particular fees, availability, tax treatment, security assurances, or recovery outcomes. Do not fill those gaps with assumptions.

## Questions to resolve before evaluating an architecture

Use these questions to organize early-stage infrastructure research:

- Which organization provides user access, and which parties process trading-related activity?
- What records describe an order or activity, and what do those records not establish?
- Who handles assets, and what process or event constitutes settlement for the particular product?
- Which external systems or providers are involved, and where is each responsibility documented?
- What current, authoritative evidence supports claims about availability, jurisdiction, fees, security, recovery, and compliance?

The answers can vary by venue, product, and jurisdiction. The permitted evidence establishes only the distinctions and examples discussed above; it is not enough to verify an individual exchange’s architecture or operating conditions. Keep unresolved items visible in the research record, and treat each specific capability as something to confirm from relevant documentation rather than a universal feature of online exchange infrastructure.

## What an NFT marketplace does—and does not establish

A marketplace can provide an interface or technical path for users to interact with NFT listings or transfers, but its actual role depends on its implementation. A service might be involved in displaying listings, coordinating a transaction, or interacting with contracts. Do not attribute any of those roles to a named service without evidence showing how it operates. In particular, a marketplace label does not tell a reader whether the service controls assets, relies on contracts, or supports a particular network.

The token and the rights associated with it are also distinct. An NFT is a blockchain-recorded token; a marketplace may display information about it or provide a way to interact with it. Neither that interface nor the token’s existence establishes every claim about the linked file, access benefit, license, copyright, or real-world asset. A token holder should not assume that a purchase or transfer automatically grants permission to copy, modify, display, or commercially use associated content.

Rights information may be specified separately through a license or rights-management reference associated with a token. But a technical standard or token label does not, by itself, determine whether those terms are legally enforceable or what rights they cover. For any particular NFT, look for the applicable terms and identify who is making the grant. A marketplace’s general description is not a substitute for the terms that actually apply to a token or collection.

It is useful to separate three questions: What token does the record identify? What file, service, or asset does the token refer to? What rights, if any, do the governing terms grant to a holder? A marketplace listing may provide clues about those questions, but it does not answer all of them automatically. The answer to a rights question may depend on specific documentation and the relevant jurisdiction; the name “XOXO NFT Marketplace” cannot resolve it.

## How to verify a claimed XOXO marketplace

Start by recording the exact domain or application name and the organization that says it operates the service. Then look for independent, reliable evidence connecting them. Check that documents and records refer to the same service and are current enough to support the claim being assessed. Similar names, copied logos, or search-result descriptions are not sufficient on their own to establish that an account or site is operated by a particular organization.

Next, separate claims about the interface from claims about technical operation. If a service says it supports a network or token transfers, look for current technical documentation and authoritative records that can corroborate that statement. A visible page or listing does not establish that the underlying transfer route is active. If the service points to a contract or network, verify that the referenced technical details correspond to the claimed service rather than relying on an address, image, or description without context.

Review dated terms, privacy disclosures, and support information. Check whether they name the same organization and product identified by the domain, and distinguish what the service says from what independent records establish. For ownership or usage claims, find the terms that apply to the particular token or collection. If those terms do not clearly identify the rights being granted, do not fill the gap with assumptions about what NFT ownership generally means.

A useful verification note can record each claim separately: the exact wording, the source, its date, and what remains unconfirmed. For example, evidence that a service publishes terms does not, by itself, verify every feature described in those terms. Likewise, evidence that a token or contract exists does not establish that a named marketplace operates it or that the token confers a particular license. Keeping these conclusions separate helps prevent an unsupported claim from being treated as a broader fact.

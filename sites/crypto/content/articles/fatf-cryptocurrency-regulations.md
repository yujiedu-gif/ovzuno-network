FATF cryptocurrency regulations are shorthand for international standards on money laundering and terrorist financing risks involving virtual assets—not a single set of laws that directly governs every crypto business. FATF standards inform national approaches, but domestic authorities establish the enforceable requirements that apply in each jurisdiction. A business must therefore check relevant local rules rather than infer its legal duties from a general FATF summary.

## What FATF cryptocurrency regulations refer to

The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) is an international standard-setter whose framework addresses money laundering and terrorist financing. In crypto discussions, “FATF cryptocurrency regulations” commonly refers to FATF standards and guidance concerning virtual assets and certain service providers. The phrase can be misleading if it suggests that FATF itself enacts or enforces one uniform code of law for businesses worldwide.

The distinction is between an international standard and a domestic legal obligation. FATF standards set out measures for countries to implement in their legal and regulatory systems. National authorities decide how to translate those standards into laws, regulations, and supervisory approaches. The binding rule for a particular business is therefore found in the relevant jurisdiction’s legal framework, not established solely by the fact that FATF has issued a recommendation or guidance.

This also limits what a general description can establish. A statement that a company is “FATF-compliant” does not, by itself, determine whether it holds any required local authorization or complies with every domestic obligation. Nor does the FATF framework alone settle how a specific business model is classified under local law.

## Which crypto activities and entities are in scope

FATF uses the terms “virtual asset” and “virtual asset service provider,” often abbreviated as VASP. Their precise definitions and boundaries should be checked in current FATF materials and the applicable local rules. The standards are concerned with service providers performing relevant virtual-asset activities; they do not imply that every person, software tool, or organization connected to crypto has identical duties.

A key point is to examine functions, not just labels. A company’s name or its description of itself does not necessarily show what services it actually provides. A centralized exchange operator, for example, may combine trading, lending, token issuance, and custody functions, although not every operator performs all of them. The combination of functions can make it important to understand the business’s activities and dependencies, but it does not by itself establish that the operator is legally a VASP in a given jurisdiction.

The same caution applies to decentralized or software-based arrangements. A technology label alone does not answer whether a person or entity is covered by a local legal definition. That determination requires the jurisdiction’s rules and facts about the activity and the parties involved. This article does not classify any particular protocol, developer, exchange, or other business.

FATF standards refer to virtual asset service providers under the standards, but their application is not a universal finding that every crypto-related business has the same obligations. Local authorities determine how relevant definitions and requirements are incorporated and applied. Readers should avoid treating an international category as a substitute for examining the domestic definition.

## Core FATF standards relevant to virtual assets

At a high level, the standards address preventive measures intended to respond to money laundering and terrorist financing risks. For virtual asset service providers under the FATF standards, those measures include customer due diligence, record keeping, suspicious-transaction reporting, and handling originator and beneficiary information for applicable transfers. This describes the international framework; it does not establish the exact duties of a particular entity under any one country’s law.

The “Travel Rule” is the common name often used for requirements concerning originator and beneficiary information in covered transfers. It should not be read as meaning that every crypto transfer everywhere is subject to identical information requirements. Which transfers are covered and which entities have obligations depend on the applicable implementation. To understand a specific case, readers need the current local rules, not just the shorthand label.

These standards are also distinct from a certification or endorsement of an individual provider. FATF materials describe an international framework, not a determination that a specific company, product, network, or transfer method meets all applicable national requirements. A provider’s statement about its own practices cannot replace checking the relevant law and supervisory requirements.

## How national implementation changes the obligations

National legislation, regulations, and supervisory approaches give effect to FATF standards. Consequently, definitions, covered activities, thresholds, licensing or registration provisions, reporting duties, enforcement approaches, and effective dates may differ by jurisdiction. Some national requirements may also go beyond the international framework. The existence of a FATF standard does not answer these local questions on its own.

This is why a business should not use a single global summary as a legal conclusion. Different jurisdictions may be connected to an activity, and the applicable analysis depends on the relevant local rules. Whether a particular country’s law applies to a specific business is a jurisdiction-specific question that this article does not resolve.

For local requirements, consult primary sources in the jurisdiction under review: legislation and regulations, and current guidance from the responsible regulator or financial intelligence authority. Those sources are the appropriate place to verify the local definitions and any applicable dates or thresholds. FATF materials can provide context for the international framework, but they do not replace those sources.

## What operators should verify before relying on a summary

A bounded research process can help separate the international framework from local requirements:

1. Identify the jurisdictions connected to the business, its customers, and the activity being assessed. Do not assume that one jurisdiction’s rules answer questions in another.
2. Describe the business’s actual functions in neutral, concrete terms. A label such as “exchange,” “wallet,” or “protocol” is not enough to establish how local rules classify the activity.
3. Consult current FATF materials to understand the international terminology and the standards relevant to virtual assets and service providers.
4. Check primary sources for each jurisdiction under consideration. Verify the local definitions, covered activities, relevant thresholds, licensing or registration rules, reporting provisions, and effective dates rather than relying on a summary alone.
5. For an entity-specific determination, seek qualified legal or compliance advice. A general explanation cannot decide whether a particular business is covered or what it must do.

FATF’s role is to set international standards, while national authorities determine how those standards relate to domestic legal duties. The framework helps explain the shared policy approach, but it cannot, without jurisdiction-specific rules and analysis of the facts, establish a crypto business’s legal obligations. That distinction is the starting point for responsible research—not a substitute for local legal analysis.

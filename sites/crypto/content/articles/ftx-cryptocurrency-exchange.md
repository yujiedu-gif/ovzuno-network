## What the FTX cryptocurrency exchange was built to do

An exchange is a market-facing venue through which participants can seek to trade assets. FTX is identified in the question as a cryptocurrency exchange, but the evidence available for this article does not substantiate the platform’s intended user groups, complete product range, operating dates, or present availability. Historical descriptions should not be treated as proof that a service operates now.

The term “exchange” can also refer to different things: a trading platform, the company operating it, or a wider group of related businesses. These are not automatically the same. A centralized exchange operator may combine third-party trading, proprietary activity, margin lending, token issuance, and custody. That is a general description of functions some operators may combine, not evidence that FTX performed each one or that any specific FTX entity owned or controlled another. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>))

This distinction matters when interpreting claims about infrastructure. A customer-facing venue might handle trade entry and execution while another legal entity or service handles custody or a related product. Without records identifying the entities, contracts, and responsibilities involved, it would be inaccurate to collapse all activity into a single “FTX system.” The evidence authorized here does not resolve those FTX-specific questions.

## Trading and market infrastructure at FTX

A general centralized-exchange model can help orient readers without implying a particular FTX implementation. In this model, customers typically transfer assets into the exchange’s custody before using its internal trading system. The venue then records orders and executed trades within its own system. Neither the general model nor the mere existence of a trading platform establishes how FTX handled custody, reconciled balances, protected access, or recorded transactions. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>))

Custody is a separate operational function from matching or recording trades. “Custody” here means holding or controlling assets on a customer’s behalf; a trading record, by itself, is not evidence that corresponding assets were held, segregated, or available. Those conclusions require evidence specific to the entities and arrangements involved. The available claims do not establish FTX’s custody contracts, asset locations, account structure, or reconciliation practices.

The operator’s broader business activities may also matter. Some centralized exchange operators combine trading with proprietary activity, lending, issuance, and custody, but that possibility should not be turned into a claim about any named company without supporting records. For FTX, the material provided does not verify which functions sat within which entities, or whether functions were combined or separated in practice. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>))

Market access should not be confused with a guarantee about trading conditions. Market liquidity describes the time and expense involved in buying or selling, including spread, transaction cost, price impact, and immediacy. It varies with transaction size and market conditions. A venue’s existence or general operating model does not establish the liquidity, execution quality, or service availability for a particular asset at a particular time. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/speeches/20160502-market-and-funding-liquidity-overview>))

Accordingly, a sound description of FTX’s market infrastructure would require contemporaneous, FTX-specific evidence for its order-entry and execution process, custody arrangements, internal records, and links among operating entities. The current evidence boundary supports explaining those as questions to investigate, not supplying answers from a generic exchange model.

## Infrastructure lessons for exchange operators and evaluators

The FTX case can be approached as an evidence question: what did each entity do, what records demonstrate it, and how did customer trading, custody, and related services connect? For evaluators, useful areas to verify include the boundaries between entities and functions, the relationship between internal trade records and asset records, and whether stated controls are supported by evidence of operation. These are questions for due diligence, not claims that a particular control would necessarily have prevented a failure.

A further distinction is between a stated service and a demonstrated operational outcome. A company’s description of its platform does not independently establish custody arrangements, reconciliation, access controls, liquidity, or service continuity. Each requires documentation specific to the entity, product, time period, and arrangement. The general possibility that an exchange combines functions can guide what to examine, but it cannot establish what FTX did. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>))

Finally, descriptions of trading conditions should be bounded by the evidence. Liquidity can vary by transaction size and market conditions, and it is not proved merely by the presence of a trading venue or internal records. Any historical statement about FTX’s liquidity or performance would require evidence specific to the asset and period. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/speeches/20160502-market-and-funding-liquidity-overview>))

The supported account is therefore deliberately narrow: FTX is addressed as a cryptocurrency exchange, while the supplied evidence describes general centralized-exchange functions rather than FTX’s particular systems or failure history. A fuller case study needs FTX-specific primary records and careful separation of corporate entities, allegations, findings, and current procedural status.

FTX was a centralized crypto exchange business, but the evidence available for this article does not establish its specific historical entities, services, operating dates, or collapse-related findings. It supports a bounded explanation of exchange infrastructure and the questions a case study should ask—not a factual account of what happened at FTX or a conclusion about its legal status, customer assets, or governance.

## What FTX cryptocurrency refers to in the exchange landscape

The phrase “FTX cryptocurrency” can refer loosely to FTX as an exchange business, cryptocurrency traded through an exchange, or assets associated with the FTX name. These are different subjects. An exchange is an intermediary or venue; cryptocurrency is an asset category. The evidence authorized here does not establish which FTX entities operated, what services they offered, when those services were available, or how the business changed over time. Those details should not be inferred from general descriptions of exchange businesses.

A centralized exchange operator may combine third-party trading, proprietary trading, margin lending, token issuance, and custody. Not every operator performs every function, and combining roles can create operational dependencies and conflicts. This is a description of possible business functions, not a finding that FTX performed each one. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>)

That distinction places a limit on responsible conclusions. Evidence that an exchange industry can involve multiple functions does not establish which functions a particular company performed, how those functions were separated, or whether a specific control failed. A company-specific history requires company-specific records identifying entities, activities, dates, and evidentiary status.

## How FTX’s exchange operations connected to market infrastructure

Exchange infrastructure includes the arrangements that connect trading activity with customer accounts and asset custody. In a common centralized-exchange model, customers transfer funds and custody to the exchange before trading on its internal order book. That arrangement places the assets with a third party for trading, although custody and legal ownership arrangements vary by platform and jurisdiction. This general model is not evidence of FTX’s particular arrangements. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>)

A related description says customers typically transfer assets into the exchange’s custody before using its internal trading system. Some venues and products use different custody, brokerage, or settlement arrangements, so this should be read as a common pattern rather than a universal rule. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>)

For infrastructure analysis, trading, custody, customer-account records, settlement, and affiliated businesses are distinct questions. An operator may combine functions, but that fact alone does not establish how a particular firm recorded customer balances, controlled assets, or organized related entities. Nor does it show whether businesses shared systems, staff, or decision-making. The claims available here do not document those FTX-specific relationships.

A careful historical analysis would identify the legal entity responsible for each function and the period covered by each record. It would also distinguish what a court or official report found from what a party alleged or stated. Without that evidence, a general exchange model must not be presented as a description of FTX’s operations.

## What the collapse revealed about governance and controls

The authorized evidence does not include court findings, examiner or bankruptcy reports, regulator materials, or verified company statements about FTX’s collapse. It therefore cannot substantiate claims about FTX’s accounting, custody controls, governance, internal systems, or affiliated entities. In particular, general descriptions of centralized exchanges cannot be used as proof that a specific failure occurred at FTX.

The broader infrastructure evidence does identify an oversight concern: an exchange operator may combine trading, lending, issuance, and custody, and combining roles can create dependencies and conflicts. This supports examining separation of responsibilities and oversight in a company-specific investigation. It is not proof of misconduct and does not establish a finding about any particular company. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>)

This boundary matters in both directions. One company’s collapse would not, by itself, show that every centralized exchange has the same weaknesses. But describing a general risk does not show that controls at a particular venue were adequate either. Conclusions about actual conduct require records of processes and decisions, interpreted in the relevant organizational and legal context.

## Infrastructure lessons for exchange operators and institutional users

The evidence supports a due-diligence approach built around questions, not assumptions. Where an operator may combine trading, proprietary activity, lending, issuance, or custody, an evaluator can ask which entities perform each function, how customer assets are handled, and what oversight applies. These are lines of inquiry, not claims about FTX or a complete compliance checklist. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>)

Custody merits separate attention because a centralized exchange may hold customer funds before trading takes place. An institutional user evaluating a venue can seek documentation explaining the custody arrangement and its relationship to the internal trading system. The general model does not establish legal ownership, segregation, or insolvency treatment in any particular arrangement; those details vary by platform and jurisdiction. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>)

Technical controls and organizational governance should also be assessed separately. The evidence available here describes exchange functions and custody patterns, but it does not establish FTX’s access management, accounting, reconciliation, or independent review. Those controls cannot be presumed present or absent. A case analysis would need dated, authoritative records that identify the relevant entity, control, and operating period.

A further question is where infrastructure functions are concentrated. Crypto and DeFi systems can exhibit substantial de facto centralization even when their stated architecture or ethos emphasizes decentralization. The degree and location of concentration must be assessed for a specific network, asset, application, and time window. This general observation does not establish FTX’s market influence or the effects of its collapse on any particular market. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/crypto-ecosystem-key-elements-and-risks>)

For operators and institutional users, the practical value of these questions is diagnostic: they help identify which evidence is needed before drawing conclusions about a venue. They do not guarantee that an exchange is safe, prove that risks have been controlled, or substitute for reviewing entity-specific records. A structural description can point to areas for investigation, but only evidence about the actual arrangement can answer whether responsibilities were separated and controls operated as intended.

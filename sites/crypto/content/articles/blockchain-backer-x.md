## How to check a blockchain or crypto claim

Begin by rewriting the post as one precise statement. Note its date and identify whether it concerns a recorded network event, a protocol rule, an interpretation, or a market outcome. If a post makes several assertions, check them separately. A transaction’s appearance on a ledger does not, by itself, establish the reason for it or what will happen next.

For a claim about on-chain activity, identify the network and the specific transaction, address, block, or contract involved. A blockchain explorer may help locate transactions by hash, blocks by hash or height, and addresses. Use those identifiers to check whether the data you have opened corresponds to the post’s assertion. Confirm that you are examining the named network and the relevant record, rather than a similar-looking identifier or an unrelated chain. An explorer can present data in a convenient interface, but the details of what it displays depend on the service. If the post does not supply enough information to identify the network or record, say that the claim cannot yet be checked from the information provided.

Next, distinguish the record from the conclusion attached to it. A record may support a narrow statement that certain activity was recorded. It does not, by itself, establish the identity of the person behind an address, that person’s intent, a causal explanation, or a future market result. Those are separate claims and need separate evidence. Avoid silently expanding a technical observation into a broader conclusion.

For a claim about protocol behavior, look for documentation or other primary material that applies to the named network and relevant implementation. Check the actual wording and conditions rather than relying only on a post’s paraphrase. If the claim concerns a third-party application or provider, seek evidence from that service about the specific feature or terms being described. If sources conflict or leave a key detail unanswered, note the uncertainty instead of choosing the interpretation that best fits the post.

## Commentary is not proof of a transaction or market outcome

A post can establish that a statement was published by a particular profile once the profile and post are verified. That is different from establishing that the statement itself is true. A chart or screenshot may illustrate an argument, but without its underlying data, time frame, and stated assumptions, the image alone does not verify the argument.

Likewise, verifying an on-chain record does not validate every interpretation attached to it. Keep three layers separate: what the network record shows, what someone says that record means, and what someone expects to happen later. The first may be checked against network data; the second needs evidence for its reasoning; the third is a prediction, not a fact established by the record.

BEP-20 is a token standard associated with BNB Smart Chain: a set of conventions for how compatible token contracts and applications interact. It is not a particular cryptocurrency, and the label alone does not establish what a token is worth or whether its contract or issuer is trustworthy.

## BEP20 what is it? The basic definition

A token standard describes conventions that software can follow when representing and interacting with tokens. BEP-20 refers to such a standard in the BNB Smart Chain context. It is therefore useful to distinguish the standard from an individual token: the standard is the shared interface, while a token is a particular implementation that uses it.

That distinction matters because a token name or symbol is not the standard itself. Nor does describing a token as BEP-20 establish that it has a particular issuer, purpose, or backing. The term identifies a technical relationship, not a general assessment of the token.

## What a token standard specifies

A token standard can set common expectations for how token-related software communicates with a contract. Shared conventions give applications a predictable interface to work with, rather than requiring each application to invent a separate way to interact with every token.

The exact functions and requirements of BEP-20 should be checked in its current authoritative specification. The evidence available here does not establish the exact BEP-20 interface or its full requirements, so it would be misleading to provide an exhaustive list of functions or present one as universal.

More broadly, technical standards describe how a system is represented or interacted with; they do not, by themselves, establish legal rights or the quality of the thing represented. For example, the SEC staff describes tokenization as creating a digital representation of a tangible or intangible asset using distributed ledger technology, while noting that its statement is not a Commission rule or binding legal guidance. That distinction reinforces why a technical label should not be mistaken for proof of rights, backing, or value. ([source](<https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/corp-fin-statement-tokenized-securities-012826-statement-tokenized-securities>))

## How BEP-20 relates to individual tokens

A claim that a particular token follows BEP-20 concerns its relationship to a standard. It does not prove that the token’s contract implements the standard correctly, that its issuer is legitimate, or that any stated backing exists. Those are separate, token-specific questions that require evidence about the relevant contract, issuer, and arrangement.

A useful comparison is the ERC-721 standard, which identifies an NFT using the combination of its contract address and token ID on an Ethereum chain. That example illustrates how a standard can supply a technical convention for identifying or interacting with tokens; it does not tell readers anything about a BEP-20 token’s properties. ([source](<https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-721>))

Token standards can also describe different kinds of arrangements. ERC-1155, for instance, allows one contract to manage multiple token types, including fungible, non-fungible, and semi-fungible configurations. This is an example of what another standard specifies, not a description of BEP-20. ([source](<https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-1155>))

## What BEP-20 does not tell you

The BEP-20 label alone does not establish a token’s price, value, yield, or investment suitability. It also does not verify a contract’s security, the identity or conduct of an issuer, or whether a token is backed by anything. Those claims must be assessed separately rather than inferred from a standard name.

Likewise, the label alone does not establish that a token is available through a particular wallet, exchange, or service, or that a transfer or recovery will succeed. Those outcomes depend on matters outside the definition of the standard, and this evidence does not establish specific availability, recovery, or transaction conditions.

Regulatory status, tax treatment, fees, and compliance are also separate questions. They require relevant, current evidence for the specific token and jurisdiction. The BEP-20 designation should not be treated as a legal or compliance conclusion.

## How to verify a BEP-20 claim

Start by checking authoritative, current documentation for the standard and the network terminology. For a particular token, identify the specific contract being discussed and seek reliable evidence about its implementation and any claims concerning its issuer, backing, security, or rights. A token’s name or standard label alone is not enough to establish those facts.

Keep technical verification and other questions distinct. Evidence that a token follows a technical convention would not, by itself, establish value, legal status, fees, availability, yield, or compliance. Each requires its own evidence, and the appropriate evidence may depend on the claim and jurisdiction.

The practical takeaway is narrow but useful: BEP-20 names a token-standard context for BNB Smart Chain. It tells readers to look for shared technical conventions, not to assume that every token using the label has the same features, backing, risks, or real-world status.

## Start with the Luxembourg service and entity question

Begin by asking the provider to identify the legal entity that would contract with the institution and the entity or entities that would actually perform custody-related functions. Request current written terms and service documentation that name the Luxembourg scope, rather than relying on a group-level description or a general reference to digital assets.

The documents should define what “custody” means in the proposed arrangement: which assets and activities are included, what the provider does, and what remains the institution’s responsibility. A third-party custodian generally manages access to private keys on a customer’s behalf, but custody structures and customer rights vary by provider and jurisdiction. That general description does not establish the terms or legal status of this particular service. [source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>)

Ask for explicit confirmation of eligible client types, supported assets and networks, service availability in Luxembourg, and any conditions or exclusions. Establish whether the proposed service is contracted locally or accessed through another jurisdiction or group entity. If the provider cannot supply current documentation that answers these questions, record availability and scope as unverified—not as implied by the provider’s name or broader group presence.

## Standard Chartered digital asset custody Luxembourg: verify regulatory status

Build a regulatory evidence file around the exact contracting and operating entities. Check current official Luxembourg and relevant European Union registers and notices, and compare the results with the provider’s written description of the service. Next, map functions to providers. ## Map custody controls to treasury operations

Request a control description detailed enough for security, treasury, and risk teams to test against their own procedures. Establish who can authorize access and transactions, how approvals work, how activity is monitored, and how the institution receives records for reconciliation. Distinguish the provider’s controls from the institution’s own authorization, oversight, and recordkeeping responsibilities.

Ask for documented evidence supporting security and operational claims. A label such as “institutional-grade,” or an assurance statement without a defined scope, does not answer which assets, systems, entities, or periods were covered. Where independent assurance is offered, review its scope and limitations rather than treating it as proof of every control or service condition.

Clarify how the arrangement handles asset segregation and reconciliation, including what records the institution can inspect and how discrepancies are escalated. The contract and operating procedures should identify incident notification and response responsibilities, continuity arrangements, and the process for restoring access or returning assets after a disruption. The evidence supplied for this article does not establish that the named service has any particular key-management design, access-control model, reconciliation process, incident procedure, continuity capability, or independent assurance. Obtain and evaluate those items directly.

## Review fees, liability, and recovery terms

Use current written terms to confirm fees, charges, service limits, and any conditions that can affect access or operations. Do not infer a fee schedule, supported asset list, or availability from marketing material that does not specifically cover the proposed Luxembourg arrangement. Record the document version and effective date used in the institution’s review.

Review who bears responsibility for loss, delay, unauthorized activity, operational failure, or interruption, and identify any caps, exclusions, notification requirements, or customer duties. Ask whether subcontractors or sub-custodians are involved, what functions they perform, and how the provider’s terms address those dependencies. Third-party custody may involve a provider managing key access, but that does not establish segregation, insurance, recovery rights, or liability allocation; each depends on the arrangement and its governing terms. Treat insurance statements as a separate verification item. Request the actual scope, insured parties, exclusions, limits, and conditions if the provider represents that coverage exists. Do not assume coverage protects the institution or every asset merely because insurance is mentioned.

Finally, examine termination and asset-return procedures. Confirm what the institution must do to request return, what conditions or holds could affect it, how assets and records are delivered, and how exceptions are handled. The relevant question is not only whether a contract promises access, but also what claim or right the institution has and how the arrangement relates to any backing assets. ## Reach a documented go, defer, or stop decision

Create a decision record that links each material conclusion to its evidence. Identify the question, source document or official register, date checked, responsible reviewer, finding, open issue, and next action. Keep provider statements distinct from verified register entries, contract language, and internal judgments.

Use a cross-functional review: legal counsel assesses the arrangement and applicability of requirements; compliance reviews relevant obligations and controls; security evaluates technical and operational evidence; and treasury tests whether the documented service fits its processes and risk criteria. The institution should set its own approval criteria rather than infer suitability from a provider’s brand or general descriptions of custody.

A “go” conclusion should be limited to the specific entity, service scope, client, assets, and terms actually reviewed. Defer when core evidence is missing or stale, and stop if material questions about availability, regulatory status, control responsibilities, or asset return cannot be resolved. Revisit the assessment when provider documentation, contracts, service scope, or relevant official-register information changes. This is a due-diligence framework, not confirmation that Standard Chartered offers a Luxembourg custody service or a recommendation to use it.

In crypto market infrastructure, **CEX** means **centralized exchange**: a category of venue that connects participants with a market-facing trading platform through centrally administered services. The term describes a broad organizational category, not a single protocol or standardized technical design. It does not, by itself, tell you how a specific platform handles custody, settlement, assets, fees, security, or legal status.

## What “crypto CEX” means

“Crypto CEX” is shorthand for a centralized exchange in the cryptoasset market. “Centralized” distinguishes the venue category from exchange designs in which trading or settlement is implemented through decentralized mechanisms. The label is about the role and organization of a trading venue; it is not a complete description of every system connected to it.

A useful conceptual map places participants and access interfaces around trading systems, with custody, settlement, data, and external-service relationships varying by arrangement. This is a way to identify functional boundaries, not a universal architecture shared by every platform. The operator may administer central venue services, but the word “CEX” alone does not identify the precise systems involved or establish which entity performs each function.

The term also does not mean that every business activity associated with a venue must be performed by one company. Exchange-related functions can be combined or separated across different firms and services, including trading, custody, and other cryptoasset activities. The relevant arrangement therefore needs to be identified before describing who does what.

This distinction is useful when reading technical or business materials. A document might refer to a trading venue, a provider, a custodian, or an access interface. Those terms can refer to different roles, even when a particular arrangement places multiple roles within one organization. A category label should not be treated as proof that a single operator controls every related service.

## Where a crypto CEX sits in exchange market infrastructure

A crypto CEX sits at the market-facing venue layer: it connects participants with a trading platform. Access may take place through direct interaction with a platform or through cryptoasset service providers. That high-level relationship does not specify the interface technology, the identity of a provider, or the venue’s internal technical design.

A conceptual market map separates the venue from the participants using it. Traders and other market participants are not themselves the exchange’s infrastructure. Likewise, an access interface can provide a route into a trading platform without being the venue itself. This distinction helps keep the term “CEX” from expanding to mean every person, application, or service involved in a market transaction.

The wider environment may also include custody, settlement, data, and external-service relationships. These functions can be arranged differently, so a high-level map should not be mistaken for a description of any particular platform. The CEX label identifies the centralized venue category, while the exact boundaries among the operator, service providers, and other infrastructure must be established separately.

Settlement is a useful example of why the boundaries matter. Financial market infrastructure frameworks distinguish settlement systems and central securities depositories as separate roles, and address linked settlement obligations and custody-related risk. That general distinction does not classify every crypto exchange as a particular kind of formal infrastructure, nor does it establish that an individual CEX operates a settlement system. It simply illustrates why venue, settlement, and custody should not be treated as interchangeable labels.

A market-facing platform may also be discussed alongside operational performance or liquidity. A centralized exchange can provide near-real-time trading, liquid order books, and advanced order functionality after assets are deposited, but performance and liquidity vary by venue, asset pair, and market conditions. This describes a possible general venue model; it is not a promise about a specific CEX or an explanation of how its systems operate. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>)

For this definition, the key point is functional separation: a venue connects participants with a trading platform, while details about interfaces, custody, settlement, data, and external services belong to the particular arrangement. Detailed exchange operation and technical integration are separate questions.

## Crypto CEX compared with adjacent infrastructure terms

A **CEX** names a centralized exchange category. A **DEX**, or decentralized exchange, names a different category of trading design. One conceptual distinction is that a decentralized exchange may retain user control of assets while trades execute or settle through smart contracts on a blockchain. DEX designs differ and can still depend on interfaces, relayers, administrators, or off-chain order components. This contrast should not be turned into a universal claim that every DEX has the same architecture or that every CEX uses one fixed alternative.

A **liquidity pool** is not simply another name for an exchange. It is a component used in some trading designs. For example, a Uniswap v2 pool uses deposited reserves of a token pair as inventory for swaps, while other automated market makers can use different curves, assets, fees, and accounting. The example clarifies the distinction between a venue or application and a particular pool mechanism; it is not a definition of all pools or all exchanges.

An **order book** is likewise not synonymous with a CEX. It is a market mechanism or record associated with orders, rather than a name for the venue category as a whole. This article uses the distinction only to separate infrastructure vocabulary; it does not describe order-book operation or imply that every centralized venue uses the same matching design.

These terms describe different levels of a market map: an exchange names a venue category, while a pool or order book refers to a component or mechanism that may be used in a trading design. Whether a specific platform combines, separates, or supplements these components is a platform-specific question.

## What the term does not tell you about a specific exchange

Calling a platform a CEX does not establish its jurisdiction, regulatory status, supported assets, fees, security controls, custody practices, or recovery options. None of those properties follows from the category name. Availability, fees, service terms, security controls, recovery, and compliance claims require current and specific documentation.

The same caution applies to organizational boundaries. Some cryptoasset service providers may combine trading, custody, lending, market-making, and other services, but that observation should not be generalized to every provider or account. A reader evaluating a specific arrangement needs to identify the responsible entity and the function it actually performs.

A practical verification step is to match each question to evidence about the relevant entity and service. For example, a statement about a venue’s trading platform does not automatically document who holds assets, how settlement is arranged, or which external services are involved. Similarly, a general market description does not establish current asset support, terms, security controls, or legal status for a particular platform. The responsible entity, relevant jurisdiction and legal status, functional boundaries, custody and settlement arrangements, applicable terms, and current evidence for operational or protection claims should be checked.

Accordingly, “crypto CEX” is best read as a concise category label: a centralized exchange venue in the broader infrastructure surrounding crypto trading. It helps locate the venue in a market map, but it is not a substitute for a platform-specific description of architecture, roles, or terms.

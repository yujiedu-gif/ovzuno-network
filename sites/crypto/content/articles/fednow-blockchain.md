## What people mean by “FedNow blockchain”

The phrase “FedNow blockchain” can refer to two different questions: whether FedNow itself uses blockchain, or how FedNow compares with a payment system that does. The first asks about a named service’s implementation. The second asks about differences between operating and record-keeping models. A general explanation of blockchain can clarify the comparison, but cannot by itself establish FedNow’s architecture.

A blockchain, in a general technical description, uses cryptographically linked transactions, distributed ledger copies, validation, and conflict-resolution rules to maintain a tamper-evident shared history. That description identifies a kind of record-keeping system; it does not identify the operator, participant rules, or settlement arrangements of any particular payment service. Nor does it tell us whether a service with a rapid-payment purpose uses that kind of ledger.

The terms “instant payment” and “blockchain” therefore describe different dimensions. One may refer to a service’s purpose or payment experience; the other refers to infrastructure for recording and validating transactions. Knowing one label does not determine the other. Without service-specific evidence, it would be a mistake to infer a technical design from the name or stated purpose of a payment service.

## How FedNow and blockchain-based payments differ

A useful comparison starts by separating the service or operator from the record-keeping model. The evidence pack does not establish FedNow’s operating arrangements, participation rules, or transaction-record design. It does describe blockchain architecture at a general level: distributed ledger copies are maintained through validation and conflict-resolution rules. That provides a reference point for what a blockchain claim would need to mean, but it does not supply the missing FedNow-specific facts.

Blockchain systems also do not all share the same access arrangement. Participation may be permissionless, with access to block publication open to participants, or permissioned, with access controlled by identified operators. Some systems can combine open transaction submission with restricted validation or other controls. Consequently, the term “blockchain” alone does not reveal who may submit transactions, who may validate records, or who controls participation.

These distinctions make broad category comparisons unreliable. A description of one blockchain’s governance or validation cannot automatically be applied to every blockchain-based payment arrangement. Likewise, a general description of a distributed ledger cannot establish how a particular central-bank service is operated. A careful comparison must name the systems being compared and verify the rules and architecture that apply to each one.

The same discipline applies to settlement claims. The available evidence describes record maintenance and validation at a broad level, but does not provide enough system-specific information to compare FedNow settlement with settlement on any named blockchain network. “The record is distributed” and “the payment is settled” are not interchangeable claims. A comparison should identify what each system documents about its own transaction processing and settlement, rather than treating ledger structure as proof of settlement outcomes.

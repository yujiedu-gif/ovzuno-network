## What counts as staking or validator involvement

For this review, **validator operation** means a party is documented as running or controlling a network’s validator activity. **Delegation** means stake is assigned to a validator under a network’s rules; it does not, on its own, establish who operates that validator. **Staking access** is broader still: an application or intermediary may make a process available without running the underlying validator.

These distinctions must be checked against the particular network and service arrangement. For example, Solana documentation describes a delegator as assigning stake to a validator while retaining token control under the stake-account model. That example is specific to Solana and cannot establish what another network’s delegation model does, or whether Shift Crypto AG participates in one.

Likewise, Ethereum documentation describes validator participation as using deposited ETH as slashable economic collateral. That describes an Ethereum protocol mechanism, not a company’s involvement in it. It would be an error to take general network documentation and infer from it that a particular company operates validators, controls keys, accepts delegated stake, or provides a staking service.

The word “staking” in a product description is therefore not enough to establish direct infrastructure operation. Evidence should state the named entity’s role and, where relevant, distinguish its own activity from a wallet, interface, service provider, or network participant’s activity.

## How to verify a claimed service or infrastructure role

Start with the legal entity. Check whether the current terms, official company disclosures, and service documentation name **Shift Crypto AG** as the provider or instead identify another company or an external protocol. A brand name, product name, or affiliated business should not be silently treated as the same legal entity.

Next, identify the exact claimed role. Does the documentation say the entity runs a validator, delegates stake, offers only an interface, or connects users with a third party? If it does not explain who performs the operation, who handles the relevant keys, and who is responsible for operational tasks, the claim remains incomplete. This article has no authorized company-specific source that resolves those questions.

For an asserted validator operation, look for network-specific identifiers or operational records that can be checked against the relevant network’s own records. The evidence should connect the identifier to the named entity or to an operator it explicitly identifies. A validator record without that connection may show that a validator exists, but it does not establish that Shift Crypto AG runs it.

Finally, compare sources rather than treating a general product page as proof of every operational detail. Network documentation can explain protocol mechanics, but company terms and disclosures are needed to establish the company’s contractual role. If those materials do not identify the same party and responsibilities, report the gap instead of filling it with assumptions.

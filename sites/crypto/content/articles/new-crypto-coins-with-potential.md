“Potential” is not a prediction that a new crypto coin will rise in price or that its project will succeed. Readers can use the term only as a prompt to assess evidence: whether the project’s stated purpose, token rules, governance, and operating claims are documented and verifiable. If key information is missing, record it as unknown rather than treating promotional statements as proof.

## What “potential” can—and cannot—mean

For due diligence, potential can mean that a project presents a coherent, checkable case for what it is meant to do. That case might include a description of the network’s purpose, the coin’s stated role, and how decisions or changes are made. Evidence can support or contradict those claims; it cannot establish future price, success, or suitability for any reader.

Keep the project and the coin distinct. A project may describe a product or protocol, while its coin may have a narrower stated function. Do not assume that progress on one automatically demonstrates the usefulness, demand, or value of the other. First identify the specific claim being assessed, then ask what evidence could confirm it.

This framework is about technical, operational, and governance evidence. It does not rank coins or predict outcomes.

## Start with the project’s purpose and verifiable activity

Begin with the project’s own account of the problem it addresses, who it says will use the system, and why a crypto asset is part of the design. These are claims to investigate, not evidence of real-world need or adoption. If the explanation does not make clear what the coin does, keep that question open.

Next, separate statements from observable records. A project’s documentation and development updates can be compared for consistency over time. If a project refers to public code or releases, check whether those materials are accessible and whether they correspond to the claims being made. The existence of a repository or a release record, by itself, does not prove that the software works as described, is secure, or is used by others.

A useful first-pass record can note:

- The project’s stated purpose and the specific role claimed for its coin.
- Which claims are supported by documentation or other inspectable records.
- Which statements rely only on team communications or a future roadmap.
- Any gaps or contradictions that require clarification.

Do not turn a stated roadmap into completed work, or a project’s description of its users into independently established use.

## Evaluate the technology, security, and governance evidence

Technical materials are useful when they explain how the system is intended to work, what the coin does within it, and which other components it depends on. Compare those descriptions with any available code or release information, but do not infer a security property from documentation alone. If dependencies are not explained, record that as an unresolved question rather than assuming the system is self-contained.

Treat audits and security reviews as bounded evidence. To assess one, record its date, the system and components examined, its stated scope, and reported limitations. The mere existence of an audit does not establish that all components were examined, that later changes were covered, or that the system is free of defects. If these details are unavailable, the review cannot support a broader security conclusion.

Governance deserves its own check. Protocol changes require proposal, technical review, implementation, coordination, and adoption across relevant stakeholders; that process can vary between networks. Look for an explanation of who can propose or approve changes and how outcomes are communicated. If authority is concentrated or the process is unclear, identify the uncertainty without assuming that a project’s stated decentralization resolves it. Crypto systems can show substantial de facto centralization even when their stated architecture emphasizes decentralization; the degree must be assessed for the specific network, asset, application, and time window.

## Read tokenomics and network use in context

“Tokenomics” refers here to the project’s stated rules and arrangements for the coin, such as supply, issuance, allocation, and described utility or governance functions. Check whether these terms are documented, dated, and consistent across project materials. If supply or allocation details are incomplete, say what is undisclosed; do not fill gaps with assumed future rules.

For a coin described as a governance token, identify the actual decision process rather than treating the label as proof of influence. For a coin described as necessary for network activity, look for an explanation of where that role appears in the system. The general proof-of-stake model, for example, uses cryptocurrency committed as stake, with stake influencing consensus weight; selection, delegation, custody, rewards, penalties, and withdrawal rules are protocol-specific. A project’s description should therefore be checked against the rules of that particular protocol rather than treated as a universal account of staking.

Keep usage claims separate from demand or value claims. A reported activity measure, even when clearly defined, does not alone establish why activity occurred or whether it implies demand for the coin. Note what the measure actually counts, where it comes from, and what its limits are. If the project does not provide enough detail to evaluate a claim, retain it as unverified.

## Use a consistent evidence checklist—and record unknowns

A repeatable assessment makes it easier to distinguish evidence from interpretation. For each material claim, record:

1. **Claim:** What exactly is being asserted about purpose, technology, token rules, operations, or governance?
2. **Source and date:** Who published the evidence, and when? Is it a project statement or an inspectable record?
3. **Scope:** Which network, version, component, or period does the evidence cover?
4. **Corroboration:** Can another relevant record support the claim, or does it remain solely an assertion?
5. **Limitations:** What does the evidence not establish, and what information is missing or contradictory?
6. **Open question:** What additional disclosure or observation would make the claim easier to evaluate?

Use the same standard for positive and negative claims. A polished document is not independent corroboration, and a missing public record does not by itself prove misconduct. It does mean that the corresponding claim has not been established by the materials available to the reader.

Include operating dependencies and risk explanations, not just descriptions of intended features. If a project relies on external services, administrators, or other components, document what is known about those dependencies and what remains unclear. Avoid inferring reliability, adoption, or future success from technical activity alone.

## Keep legal, tax, access, and recovery questions separate

Regulation, tax treatment, compliance obligations, and asset availability are outside a general technical assessment unless supported by current, authoritative evidence for the relevant jurisdiction and circumstances. A global explanation cannot establish how a rule applies to an individual reader. This article makes no legal or tax conclusions and does not claim that any particular coin is available through a service.

Apply the same discipline to fees, yields, security, and recovery. Do not state a fee, yield, or access condition without documented, current terms. For security or recovery claims, use the relevant protocol or product documentation, identify its scope, and state its limits. Do not imply that an account or asset can always be recovered.

The result of this process is an evidence record, not a forecast. It should distinguish what is documented, what is independently checkable, what is only asserted, and what remains unknown—without turning “potential” into a prediction of price or success.

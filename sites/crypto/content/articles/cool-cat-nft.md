## What “Cool Cat NFT” refers to

“Cool Cat NFT” is a collection-specific label. Without authoritative project materials, the label cannot confirm the collection’s current status, precise scope, or official description. Nor does the name identify a particular token or establish what media or permissions are associated with it.

An NFT, or non-fungible token, is a token record created or assigned under a smart contract’s rules. Minting creates a token record and initial ownership under those rules. It does not, by itself, create, authenticate, store, or transfer rights in the work or asset that the token may reference. ([source](<https://ethereum.org/nft/>))

Several distinct things may therefore be involved: the collection name, an individual token, associated media, and project materials describing terms or uses. A fact about one does not automatically establish facts about the others. For instance, a project description may explain what a collection is intended to represent, but it does not itself show the technical details of an individual token or the permissions attached to the associated artwork.

This distinction is especially useful when a description uses broad words such as “ownership,” “artwork,” or “utility.” Those terms can point to different objects or claims. A blockchain record can identify the token and its recorded owner under the relevant contract rules; a media reference can point to an image or other file; and a license or project term can address what a holder may do with that material. The evidence for one category should not be substituted for evidence about another.

## The token, blockchain record, and associated artwork

The token is an onchain record; the artwork is the media associated with it. They are connected by a project’s technical and descriptive materials, but they are not necessarily the same data or object. A token can exist as a record even when the associated image or file is referenced separately.

For example, the ERC-721 metadata extension can associate a token ID with a URI, or Uniform Resource Identifier, whose JSON metadata may contain a name, description, and image URI. The URI and referenced media may be stored onchain, referenced through content addressing, or depend on mutable offchain hosting. This describes options supported by the standard, not a verified storage arrangement for Cool Cats. ([source](<https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-721>))

Consequently, a collection label alone cannot show where a particular image is stored, whether a reference can change, or whether the media will remain available. Those questions require evidence about the specific collection and token. This overview does not identify a Cool Cats network, contract address, token ID, or media-storage method.

The same caution applies when interpreting a token’s metadata. A metadata field can describe an item or point to media, but its presence does not prove that the file is permanently hosted, that the description is accurate, or that the person who created the token had authority over the referenced work. The technical record and the claims made about the represented artwork should be evaluated separately.

## What owning a Cool Cat NFT does—and does not—establish

In this context, “owning” an NFT refers to the token record identifying an address as owner under the contract’s rules. It should not be read as an automatic transfer of copyright in associated artwork. Nor does the token record alone establish permission to reproduce, adapt, distribute, display, or commercially use that artwork.

The token and its associated media are distinct from any linked license, copyright, access benefit, or real-world asset claim. Specific rights or asset connections require supporting implementation details and applicable documentation.

A license or other permission must be assessed from the terms that actually apply, including their scope. The evidence available here does not establish which Cool Cats license applies, what version governs, or what permissions it grants. It therefore cannot support a collection-specific claim about commercial use or other holder rights. A general statement about NFT ownership cannot fill that gap.

Technical standards can also describe features that are not automatic entitlements. For example, ERC-2981 standardizes a way for a contract to provide a royalty recipient and amount for a stated sale price. Payment remains voluntary and requires implementation by a marketplace or participant. This illustrates a technical mechanism; it does not establish a royalty entitlement for any Cool Cats token. ([source](<https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-2981>))

Similarly, ERC-4907 adds a time-limited user role separate from the NFT owner, with an expiration time that can end usage permission without transferring ownership. Applications must recognize and enforce the role; it does not create a legal lease by itself. This is an example of a specific extension, not an inherent feature of NFTs or a verified Cool Cats benefit. ([source](<https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-4907>))

Holder benefits, access, or other uses should therefore be treated as project-specific claims requiring current confirmation. The token label alone does not establish that a benefit exists, what its conditions are, or whether it remains available. This overview does not determine those matters.

## How to verify collection-specific claims

Match the evidence to the claim. For a project description or stated rights, look for authoritative project documentation and the applicable terms. Note the date and scope of those materials: a general description may not answer what rights apply to a particular token or what version of terms governs it.

For technical claims, verify network and contract details against reliable blockchain records before relying on them. A collection name is not a substitute for checking the specific identifier associated with a token. If the claim concerns artwork storage, examine the applicable metadata and media references. The existence of a metadata standard does not show which storage arrangement a collection uses.

For a license, access feature, or other use, compare the assertion with current, authoritative terms or documentation. Check whether the terms describe the specific work or token at issue and whether they state any conditions or limits. If those materials do not establish the point, leave it unresolved rather than inferring it from token ownership. This verification process is about understanding claims; it does not require making a purchase or transfer.

Keep the questions separate when reviewing materials: What token is being discussed? What media does its metadata reference? What do the applicable terms say about use or rights? What evidence supports any claimed benefit? Answering one does not automatically answer the others.

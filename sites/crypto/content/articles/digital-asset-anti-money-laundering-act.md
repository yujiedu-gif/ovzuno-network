## How to verify the bill’s status in official records

Begin with the relevant Congress.gov bill page. Confirm that the formal title and bill identifier match the reference at issue. Review the legislative actions recorded on that page, and open the linked bill text when the question concerns what the proposal contains. Check which version the text represents; when describing a proposed provision, name or otherwise identify the version consulted so that readers can distinguish it from later text.

Next, determine what kind of status claim is being made. “Introduced,” “amended,” and “passed” describe legislative actions; none of those words alone answers whether a measure became law. If someone says it was enacted, check for the official public law and its text in U.S. government records. The enactment claim should be tied to that record, not inferred from a bill-page headline or from a report that a chamber took action.

If the question is not merely historical but asks what rules apply now, verify the relevant current law and any applicable regulations separately. A bill’s history does not, by itself, establish the full current legal framework for an organization or activity. Nor does an enacted text alone establish how a particular institution’s facts fit within the relevant scope; that requires a separate, qualified assessment.

A useful record of the check should include the date consulted, the bill identifier, the action or public-law record reviewed, and the text version considered. This makes the status statement auditable and helps prevent an older description from being mistaken for a current one. Recheck before relying on the information in an internal policy, treasury decision, or external communication, since legislative information can change.

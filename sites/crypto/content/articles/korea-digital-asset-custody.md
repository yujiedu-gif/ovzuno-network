For an institution assessing digital-asset custody connected to South Korea, begin by mapping the actual entities, activities, locations, and control responsibilities, then verify each jurisdiction-specific requirement with current authoritative materials and qualified local advisers. The available evidence here does not establish South Korean licensing or registration rules, service availability, contract terms, security performance, tax treatment, or recovery commitments. Treat those as open diligence items—not as assumptions.

## Define the custody arrangement before assessing it

“Custody” can describe different operational arrangements. In a third-party custody model, a custodian manages access to private keys on behalf of customers; crypto exchanges may be among the possible third-party custodians. That high-level description does not establish the terms, legal rights, or control structure of a particular service. [source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>)

Before reviewing a proposal, create an arrangement map. Identify the assets and networks in scope, the institution contracting for the service, the entity operating the custody function, and any other entities with a role in key access, approvals, transaction execution, or support. Record where the institution, service provider, key infrastructure, and relevant operations are located. These facts are necessary for jurisdiction-specific review; the evidence available here does not determine which rules apply to a particular cross-border structure.

For each operational step, distinguish direct custody from delegated or shared responsibilities. Ask who can initiate a transfer, who can approve it, who controls signing credentials, and who can change the people or systems authorized to act. If a provider describes itself as a custodian but another party operates key infrastructure or transaction workflows, record that separation rather than treating the arrangement as a single service.

## korea digital asset custody: verify the applicable operating context

Do not infer South Korean legal status from a service label, a provider’s general statements, or the fact that an institution can access a service. The evidence available for this article does not establish current South Korean requirements or the classification of any particular activity or entity.

Build a jurisdiction review around the facts of the proposed arrangement. Ask qualified South Korean counsel to identify the current primary legal and regulatory materials relevant to the specific parties, assets, custody functions, and transaction flows. Request the basis for any conclusion about registration, licensing, reporting, or other duties, and confirm that the cited materials are current and applicable to the particular entity and activity. Where a conclusion depends on cross-border operations, outsourcing, or a division of responsibilities, ask the adviser to address those facts explicitly rather than applying a general answer.

Keep three categories separate in the review record: verified requirements supported by authoritative materials; interpretations that require legal analysis; and unresolved questions. This distinction prevents a working assumption from being recorded as a legal conclusion. The checklist is not a substitute for current South Korean advice, and it does not provide a comprehensive account of South Korean digital-asset regulation.

## Trace control, access, and recovery responsibilities

A wallet is used to generate, manage, store, or use asymmetric keys and blockchain addresses; that description alone does not reveal who controls a particular key or who can authorize transactions. [source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/Projects/Key-Management/Key-Management-Guidelines>) Key management concerns the protection and lifecycle of keying material, including generation, storage, distribution, use, recovery, revocation, and destruction. That lifecycle is a useful framework for asking operational questions, not proof that a provider performs any particular control.

Request a written responsibility matrix covering key generation and storage, access grants and removals, transaction initiation and approval, signing, recovery, and key retirement. Ask the provider to identify which steps are performed by the institution, the custodian, or another party. Document approval roles and change-control procedures, including who can modify permissions and how that change is authorized and recorded.

For recovery and continuity, request the actual procedures and contractual allocation of responsibilities. Ask what happens when an authorized user is unavailable, credentials are lost, or an operational incident interrupts access; ask who decides whether recovery is permitted and what evidence or approvals are required. These are diligence questions, not claims that any arrangement offers a particular recovery outcome. Do not treat words such as “recoverable,” “redundant,” or “always accessible” as established performance without specific, verifiable documentation.

## Review operational evidence and contractual allocation

Request current documents that explain transaction authorization, reconciliation processes, incident escalation, service continuity, and the handling of responsibilities assigned to other entities. Match each document to the specific service, legal entity, and asset scope under review. A policy description may explain a stated process, but the evidence available here does not establish that any provider has implemented a particular control or that a control has been independently tested.

Review the proposed contract alongside the operational map. Identify who is accountable for each step, what the institution must do, and where another party or subcontractor is involved. Ask counsel to assess contractual rights, dispute processes, and any consequences that depend on the arrangement or applicable law. The evidence available here does not support conclusions about asset segregation, insolvency treatment, or the legal effect of contract terms in South Korea.

For security claims, request documentation with a clear scope and date, and determine what it actually covers. Avoid turning a description of a control into a conclusion that the overall custody arrangement is secure. The available evidence does not establish a security rating, provider performance, or a universal standard for assessing one.

## Resolve fees, tax, and compliance questions before approval

Confirm fees, supported assets, service availability, and operational commitments directly from current contractual or official materials. The evidence available here does not establish any provider’s fees, supported assets, or availability in South Korea. Do not treat a general website statement or a sales summary as a substitute for the proposed agreement and its defined scope.

Obtain tax advice for the institution’s facts and the particular transactions under consideration. No tax conclusion for South Korea is supported here. Likewise, identify applicable compliance obligations with qualified advisers rather than importing rules from another jurisdiction. As one limited example of why jurisdiction matters, U.S. persons and virtual-currency businesses subject to U.S. jurisdiction have responsibility for avoiding prohibited sanctions transactions and applying a tailored, risk-based program appropriate to their activities. That U.S.-specific statement does not determine South Korean obligations or resolve whether it applies to a particular party. [source](<https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/913571/download?inline=>)

If a party is a virtual asset service provider under FATF standards, those standards call for preventive measures that include customer due diligence, record keeping, suspicious-transaction reporting, and handling originator and beneficiary information for applicable transfers. FATF standards do not, by themselves, establish the precise legal duties, thresholds, licensing requirements, data handling, or permitted services in South Korea; those depend on how standards are implemented in the relevant jurisdiction. [source](<https://www.fatf-gafi.org/en/publications/Virtualassets/Virtual-assets-fatf-standards.html>) Ask local advisers to determine whether and how these standards or local implementing requirements apply to each party and activity.

## Record decision conditions and unresolved risks

Make the review reproducible. For each material conclusion, record the evidence source, date reviewed, entity and service covered, responsible owner, and any assumptions or limitations. Maintain a separate list of unresolved legal, tax, compliance, security, recovery, and operational questions. Assign each item to an appropriate internal owner or qualified adviser rather than silently treating it as resolved.

Before approval, state the conditions that must be met, the questions that require escalation, and the events that would trigger renewed review. Reassess when the arrangement, responsible entities, contractual terms, supported assets, or relevant legal materials change. These are governance steps for making the decision reviewable; they do not establish that a particular custody arrangement satisfies South Korean requirements or is appropriate for any institution.

Institutional treasuries can receive digital-asset income only through a defined activity or arrangement—for example, a contractual payment or a protocol-based reward—but the available evidence here does not establish the mechanics, terms, or risks of particular income products. Decision-makers should identify the source of any proposed payment, who controls the assets, and what obligations apply, then obtain jurisdiction-specific accounting, tax, and legal review. A change in an asset’s market value is distinct from a payment or reward received.

## What counts as digital asset income for a treasury?

A digital token can be a ledger record about an asset and its owner, with programmable rules governing use or transfer. That technical description does not by itself determine legal title, enforceability, or the quality of the represented asset. Financial tokenization can also represent financial assets and liabilities on programmable digital ledgers; that programmability does not replace the need for legal certainty, governance, operational resilience, or reliable settlement assets. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/tokenisation-context-money-and-other-assets-concepts-and-implications-central-banks>) [source](<https://www.imf.org/en/publications/imf-notes/issues/2026/04/01/tokenized-finance-574921>)

For treasury analysis, separate amounts actually received under an arrangement from changes in the market value of an asset the organization already holds. The latter is a valuation change, not a payment generated by an income activity. The exact accounting treatment of either item cannot be inferred from the technical form of a token. It depends on the organization’s circumstances and applicable standards, which are not specified by the evidence available for this article.

The term “income” can describe different economic arrangements, but it should not be treated as a single mechanism. A protocol reward, a payment owed under a contract, and revenue received for providing a service may have different payers, conditions, and risks. The available evidence does not substantiate the operation or terms of particular staking, lending, liquidity, or service-revenue offerings. Those details must be verified in current primary documentation and governing agreements before classifying a proposed stream.

## How digital asset income mechanisms differ

Start by asking what event causes a payment or reward and who or what is responsible for it. A protocol-based reward would need to be assessed against the relevant protocol’s rules and operating conditions. A contractual payment would instead require review of the counterparty’s obligations and the agreement’s conditions. Service revenue would require evidence of the service being provided and how its payment is calculated. These are useful categories for inquiry, not claims that a specific mechanism is available, permitted, or suitable for a given treasury.

For each proposed activity, document the payer or source, the triggering event, how amounts are determined, whether payment is variable or discretionary, and what can interrupt or end it. A headline description such as “yield” is not enough to establish whether a payment is a protocol reward, a contractual claim, service revenue, or another arrangement. Nor does it show that a payment will occur.

Keep income activity separate from passive holding in treasury records and decision materials. That separation helps teams ask whether a return depends on active participation, a third party’s performance, a protocol’s rules, or an operational service. The available evidence does not establish specific reward rates, fee arrangements, lockups, withdrawal terms, or slashing conditions, so none should be assumed.

## Assess the source of return and who controls the assets

Trace the arrangement from the asset to the expected payment. Identify the relevant protocol, contracting party, service recipient, or other source; then record the conditions that must be met before value is received. Ask whether the organization retains the ability to direct transfers, whether another party acts on its behalf, and which party is responsible for keys, approvals, and recovery. Do not equate a dashboard balance or an advertised reward with an established right to withdraw assets or receive a payment.

In a self-custody arrangement, the organization controls and is responsible for the private keys and recovery material needed to access its crypto assets. That means treasury procedures need to address who can authorize access and how recovery material is protected. The technical label “self-custody” alone does not establish that the organization’s internal controls are adequate. [source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>)

If an intermediary or other party participates, review the actual agreement and operating model rather than assuming the treasury retains direct control. Determine which assets or rights are transferred, what the counterparty is obligated to do, and what happens if it cannot perform. The evidence available here does not establish the custody arrangements, credit exposure, liquidity terms, protocol dependencies, or security controls of any specific provider or product.

Treat operational and technical risks as questions to verify, not as generic assurances. Depending on the proposed activity, request documentation addressing asset control, transfer permissions, exit conditions, fees, protocol or service dependencies, incident handling, reconciliation, and recovery. For any claim about a security property, require evidence that describes the relevant system and its limits; a general statement that an activity is “onchain” or “institutional” does not resolve those questions.

## Accounting, tax, and regulatory questions

The evidence available for this article does not specify accounting recognition, measurement, or disclosure rules for a particular digital asset or income mechanism. Finance teams should identify the applicable accounting framework and the facts of the arrangement, then consult qualified accounting advisers. They should also preserve records that explain what was received, when, from whom, under what terms, and how the amount was determined. This is a review workflow, not a conclusion about accounting treatment.

Tax treatment and timing are likewise jurisdiction- and circumstance-specific. The evidence here does not support a tax conclusion or a universal treatment for protocol rewards, contractual payments, service revenue, or valuation changes. Organizations should obtain current jurisdiction-specific guidance from qualified tax professionals and verify that it addresses the actual transaction and entity.

Regulatory status and compliance obligations also require activity-specific, jurisdiction-specific review. For United States sanctions purposes only, U.S. persons and virtual-currency businesses subject to U.S. jurisdiction are responsible for avoiding prohibited sanctions transactions and applying a tailored, risk-based compliance program appropriate to their activities. This limited statement does not determine obligations elsewhere or whether a particular treasury or counterparty is a regulated virtual asset service provider. [source](<https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/913571/download?inline=>)

Do not infer regulatory permission from a technology’s availability or from another organization’s use of it. Before evaluating an activity, establish which entities participate, what each does, where the relevant activity occurs, and which jurisdictional requirements may apply. The evidence provided does not establish universal authorization, restrictions, or compliance duties for any income mechanism.

## A due-diligence checklist for institutional teams

Before considering a digital-asset income activity, assemble a review file that answers the following questions:

- **Mechanism:** What activity generates the payment or reward? Who or what supplies it, and what event or condition triggers it?
- **Terms:** What documents define the payment, fees, discretion, duration, exit conditions, and any limits on transfer or withdrawal? Which claims remain unverified?
- **Asset control:** Who controls the private keys, approves transfers, holds recovery material, and bears responsibility for access? If control is shared or delegated, how does that arrangement work?
- **Dependencies and risk:** Which counterparties, protocols, service providers, or operational processes must function? What documentation describes interruption, loss, recovery, and incident response?
- **Records and reporting:** Can the organization reconcile assets, payments, fees, and transaction history to internal records? What evidence supports finance and audit review?
- **Professional review:** Have accounting and tax advisers assessed the actual arrangement under the organization’s applicable framework and jurisdictions? Have legal and compliance teams checked relevant permissions and obligations using current, authoritative sources?

The checklist is a way to structure investigation, not a recommendation to pursue an activity. If the source of payment, asset-control model, applicable terms, or jurisdictional treatment cannot be documented, the uncertainty should remain explicit rather than being filled by an assumed return, accounting treatment, or claim of safety.

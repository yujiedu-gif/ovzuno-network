A crypto spending card is a card product linked to digital assets that uses a traditional card-payment network. The label alone does not tell you where the crypto is held or how a purchase is funded. Depending on the product, crypto may be converted to fiat before spending or in connection with a card transaction; card authorization and merchant settlement are separate stages, and an individual purchase need not be a blockchain transaction.

## What a crypto spending card does

A cryptocurrency is a digital asset or unit that can be cryptographically transferred between users of a blockchain network. A wallet is used to generate, manage, store, or use the keys and addresses associated with blockchain activity. A card, by contrast, is the payment instrument used in a card transaction. These terms describe different parts of a possible arrangement, not one combined mechanism. [source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/Cryptocurrency>) [source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/wallet>)

Some products connect a card to digital assets without having the card directly spend crypto at the merchant. For example, a product may let a user top up with fiat or convert supported cryptocurrency to fiat before loading a card. That is one documented funding model, not a rule for every crypto-linked card. Supported assets and funding methods depend on the product and location.

A separate model described by Visa for its programs may convert digital assets to local fiat at the point of sale, while merchants receive standard payments through Visa’s existing settlement process. Visa’s description is specific to its programs; it should not be treated as a universal account of all cards or networks.

The crypto balance, wallet or custody service, conversion process, and card-payment system may therefore be related but distinct. The card label does not establish who holds crypto, who can access associated keys, or at what point a conversion takes place. A third-party custodian, if used, manages access to private keys on customers’ behalf; that fact alone does not describe the card’s full funding or processing arrangement.  [source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>)

## The crypto card payment flow

A possible purchase can be understood as several linked steps, with their order depending on the product:

1. **The cardholder initiates a purchase.** The card is presented to a merchant, beginning a card-payment request. The user’s crypto-related funding may have been arranged earlier, such as through a fiat top-up or conversion before loading the card, or it may be handled in connection with the purchase.
2. **The card transaction receives an authorization response.** Authorization concerns whether the requested card transaction is approved under the relevant program’s processing arrangements. It is not the same thing as later clearing or settlement, nor does it specify when a crypto balance is converted or updated. The available evidence does not establish universal authorization checks or outcomes for these products.
3. **The funding arrangement supplies the payment value.** In a preloaded model, fiat may already have been added to the card after a user converts supported crypto. In a point-of-sale model described by Visa, digital assets may be converted to local fiat at the point of sale. These examples illustrate different arrangements, not a single required sequence.
4. **Card payment participants process the transaction.** The payment network can carry the card transaction through its settlement process; in Visa’s description, the merchant receives a standard payment through Visa’s existing settlement process. The evidence does not set out a universal chronology or timing for authorization, clearing, and settlement across all crypto-linked card products.

This outline is conditional. The evidence supports both a pre-purchase fiat-funding model and a Visa-described point-of-sale conversion model, but does not establish that every product uses either one. A card purchase also should not automatically be described as a blockchain transfer: the card label alone does not establish where crypto is held or how the purchase is funded.

## Conversion, authorization, and settlement are different

**Conversion** is the change from digital assets to fiat in the cited card-program examples. It may occur before spending, such as when crypto is converted before loading a card, or at the point of sale in the Visa-described model. These examples do not establish a universal conversion time, exchange-rate method, or balance-update rule.

**Authorization** is the card-payment stage at which the transaction request receives an approval or decline response. It should not be confused with final settlement, and the allowed evidence does not specify the authorization conditions for crypto spending cards. Nor does authorization alone explain whether a related crypto conversion has occurred or how an account record will change.

**Clearing** and **settlement** are later card-payment processes among relevant payment participants. The available evidence specifically describes merchants receiving standard payments through Visa’s existing settlement process in the Visa-facilitated programs it covers. It does not establish that all networks or products follow identical steps, or that settlement is the same event as crypto conversion.

The distinction is useful when interpreting a transaction record. A card authorization, a completed card payment, a conversion entry, and a change to a crypto-related balance may be different records or events; the evidence does not define a universal way they correspond. Product terms are needed to determine timing, rates, fees, and how reversals, refunds, or failed transactions affect the card and crypto sides. Do not assume that a refund restores the same amount of crypto or occurs at the same time as a card-side adjustment: the permitted evidence does not establish such an outcome.

## Who may take part in a card purchase

Possible participants include the cardholder, the card issuer or program operator, a wallet or custody provider, a conversion provider, the card network, an acquirer serving the merchant, and the merchant. This describes potential roles rather than a fixed roster. One organization may perform multiple functions, and a separate conversion provider may not be involved.

The network’s described settlement role does not establish that it holds or converts the user’s crypto. In the Visa example, the network description concerns conversion to local fiat at the point of sale and the merchant receiving a standard payment through the existing settlement process. It does not establish the custody structure of a user’s balance or a universal division of responsibilities across providers.

Similarly, the fact that a wallet is used for blockchain keys and addresses does not show that the wallet directly participates in card authorization or merchant settlement. A third-party custodian may manage access to keys, but product documents are needed to identify whether that role exists and how it relates to card funding. [source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/wallet>) [source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>)

## What to verify in product terms

Check the specific product’s current documentation to understand its funding model: whether it accepts fiat balances, supports converting particular crypto assets to fiat before loading, or describes conversion at the point of sale. Supported assets and methods depend on the product and location, so do not assume a symbol or card label establishes eligibility.

Then look for the described conversion timing, fees, exchange-rate method, limits, and handling of pending, declined, reversed, or refunded purchases. The allowed evidence does not provide universal values or outcomes for these details. It also does not substantiate general availability statements; verify current product terms for the relevant location.

Finally, distinguish card-payment terms from crypto custody and account-access terms. Review who manages access to keys, what records show a conversion or balance change, and what the product says about security, disputes, refunds, and recovery. A custodian’s role in managing key access does not by itself establish customer rights or the protections applying to crypto holdings. [source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>)

This explainer does not determine legal, tax, or compliance requirements. Those questions require current, authoritative information for the relevant jurisdiction and product. The practical takeaway is to treat a crypto spending card as a card-payment arrangement connected to digital assets—not as proof of one standard custody model, conversion point, or blockchain transaction flow.

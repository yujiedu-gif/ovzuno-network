An NFT is a distinct digital token recorded on a blockchain. Think of it like a numbered entry in a public record that is associated with a digital collectible: the entry is not necessarily the picture, and holding the token does not automatically give you copyright or control of the picture. That comparison is only an analogy. The precise meaning depends on the token system and any terms connected to it.

## What is an NFT, in simple terms?

NFT stands for “non-fungible token.” “Non-fungible” means the token is treated as distinct rather than interchangeable with another token as if both were identical units. An NFT is a record about a token in a blockchain system, not a general label for every copy of a digital image.

A useful, limited analogy is a numbered entry associated with a collectible. The number helps distinguish one entry from another, but it is not the collectible itself. Likewise, a blockchain token can be associated with a digital item without being the file that displays or contains that item.

More generally, a digital token can combine a ledger record about an asset and its owner with programmable rules governing use or transfer. That technical description does not, by itself, determine legal title, enforceability, or the quality of the represented asset. The word “ownership” therefore needs context: a blockchain can record who holds a token according to its system, while other questions about the associated item or rights may depend on separate arrangements. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/tokenisation-context-money-and-other-assets-concepts-and-implications-central-banks>)

## What does an NFT record on a blockchain?

At a simple level, the record identifies a token and reflects who holds it under the relevant system’s rules. In Ethereum’s NFT model, a smart contract associates a unique token identifier with an Ethereum address. This is a statement about the token record; it does not mean the address necessarily identifies a particular person, or that the person has every right someone might associate with the token. [source](<https://ethereum.org/nft/>)

Creating a token, often called minting, creates a token record and initial ownership under the smart contract’s rules. Minting alone does not create, authenticate, store, or transfer rights in the work or asset referenced by that record. The token and the underlying item are separate concepts from the outset. [source](<https://ethereum.org/nft/>)

A token may be linked to information about a digital item, but the blockchain record should not be confused with that information or the file itself. In a given system, the token may identify an entry while associated information describes or points toward content. The exact arrangement depends on the implementation; the definition of an NFT does not say that every related file is stored directly in the token.

## What does owning an NFT mean—and not mean?

In plain language, holding an NFT means that the system records an address as holding that token under the system’s rules. It does not automatically mean the holder owns copyright, owns a related physical or digital item, or has permission to use the associated work in every way.

The transfer of a token does not necessarily transfer the associated asset or copyright rights in the underlying work. Rights depend on applicable law and valid contracts, licenses, or other governing terms. A token record by itself cannot settle those separate questions. [source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>)

A helpful way to keep the ideas straight is to distinguish three things:

- **The token:** the blockchain record and the rules that identify or transfer it.
- **The content:** a digital file or other item that may be associated with the token.
- **The rights:** permissions or legal interests that may apply to the content or item.

These can be connected, but they are not automatically the same. A token record can say something about the token without proving that the holder has copyright or every other right in associated material.

## A simple example: token, link, and digital item

Imagine a hypothetical digital collectible represented by an image. A blockchain record identifies a particular token, and the system records an address as its holder. Information associated with the token refers to the image. In this example, the token is the record, the reference is the connection to information about the image, and the image is the content. The example illustrates a possible distinction; it is not a claim that every NFT uses the same design.

The reference and the content also raise separate questions. A reference does not, by itself, tell you who controls a file, what terms govern its use, or whether it will remain accessible. NFT-associated rights and content can depend on applicable law, contractual terms, product transparency, and external systems beyond the token record. [source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>)

For that reason, the numbered-entry analogy is useful only up to a point. It helps explain how one token can be distinguished from another, but it does not mean the content is physically inside the token record or that holding the token transfers every right in the content. Those conclusions cannot be drawn from the analogy alone.

## What this simple explanation leaves out

NFT systems and the terms associated with them can differ. The basic definition explains the token record, not every technical design, content arrangement, or legal outcome. In particular, the fact that a record associates an address with a token does not establish copyright ownership or guarantee access to a separate file.

The short version is: an NFT is a distinct token recorded on a blockchain and may be associated with a digital item. The token record, the content, and rights in the content are separate things. A reader should not infer copyright, control of a file, or permanent availability from the token record alone.

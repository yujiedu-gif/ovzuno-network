FDV, or fully diluted valuation, is a calculation that multiplies a token’s price by a chosen figure for its fully diluted supply. It is not a forecast, a measure of realized value, or a standalone conclusion about whether a token is overvalued or undervalued. The result depends on which supply figure and price are used, and on when those inputs were recorded.

## What does FDV mean in crypto?

Fully diluted valuation estimates a token’s valuation under an assumption that the selected fully diluted supply is counted at the stated token price. In general terms, it asks: what would the product of price and supply be if the supply input in this calculation were used?

The word “valuation” can sound like an appraisal of what an asset is worth. Here, FDV is more limited: it is an arithmetic result based on stated inputs. It does not establish that all tokens represented by the supply figure could be sold at the quoted price, or that the quoted price would remain unchanged if supply conditions changed.

That distinction matters because financial tokenization—the representation of financial assets or liabilities on programmable digital ledgers—does not by itself establish the legal or economic value of what a token represents. A ledger record and an asset’s valuation are separate questions. [source](<https://www.imf.org/en/publications/imf-notes/issues/2026/04/01/tokenized-finance-574921>)

## How is crypto FDV calculated?

The basic relationship is:

**FDV = token price × selected fully diluted supply**

For example, if a hypothetical calculation uses a token price of $2 and a fully diluted supply of 10 million tokens, the arithmetic result is $20 million. This example illustrates the multiplication only; it does not describe a particular asset, verify any supply schedule, or suggest a future price.

The calculation is only as clear as its inputs. A published FDV figure should identify the token price, the supply measure used, and the date or time associated with the data. It should also say how “fully diluted supply” is defined in that calculation. If a data provider’s methodology is unclear, the figure may not be comparable with another provider’s FDV for the same token.

Circulating supply, total supply, and maximum supply are not interchangeable labels. Circulating supply is the supply counted as currently circulating under the chosen source’s methodology. Total supply and maximum supply refer to different supply concepts, so a reader should not assume that either one is automatically the correct FDV input. The evidence available here does not establish a universal definition of fully diluted supply or a standard calculation method across data providers. Check the source’s stated methodology rather than infer one.

## FDV versus circulating market capitalization

Circulating market capitalization uses the token price multiplied by circulating supply. FDV uses the token price multiplied by a broader supply assumption, as defined by the calculation’s source. The two figures can therefore differ even when they use the same token price.

If a source’s FDV input is larger than its circulating-supply input, the resulting FDV will be larger than the circulating market capitalization at the same price. That difference describes the effect of the selected supply inputs; by itself, it does not prove that the token is mispriced or that future tokens will enter circulation on a particular schedule.

The two metrics answer different arithmetic questions. Circulating market capitalization applies price to a circulating-supply figure. FDV applies price to a broader supply figure. Neither number alone establishes demand, the ability to trade at the quoted price, or the value of the token under different market conditions. Market liquidity is a separate dimension involving factors such as transaction costs, price impact, spread, and immediacy, and it can vary with transaction size and market conditions. [source](<https://www.bis.org/speeches/20160502-market-and-funding-liquidity-overview>)

## What FDV can—and cannot—tell you

FDV can help a reader see how a supply assumption changes a price-times-supply calculation. It cannot, on its own, tell the reader whether the assumed supply will enter circulation, when that might happen, or what price would apply then.

Supply-related events may make a reported figure’s assumptions stale or change the supply data used in a later calculation. To interpret an FDV figure, check the supply definition, source, calculation date, and any relevant published information about supply changes. Do not treat a difference between FDV and circulating market capitalization as proof of future dilution, or as proof of overvaluation or undervaluation.

A fully diluted calculation is hypothetical in the limited sense that it applies a price to the selected supply figure. It does not establish that all units can be sold at that price, that buyers would be available for them, or that the result is realized market value. It is also not a price forecast or a recommendation to buy or sell.

## Data, jurisdiction, and risk considerations

Before comparing FDV figures, verify that the sources use compatible price and supply inputs and that the figures are dated. If the sources define supply differently, report that difference rather than presenting the results as directly comparable. If you cannot determine the supply methodology, state that limitation or avoid relying on the number.

Keep the calculation separate from claims that require additional evidence. An FDV figure alone does not establish token rights, future supply events, liquidity, or legal treatment. Claims about regulation, taxes, fees, yields, security, recovery, availability, or compliance require relevant, jurisdiction-specific support; without it, they should be omitted.

The practical reading is straightforward: treat FDV as a price-times-supply calculation, identify its inputs, and compare it with circulating market capitalization only with the supply definitions and dates in view. Neither metric should be mistaken for a forecast or a complete measure of value.

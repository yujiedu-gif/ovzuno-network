To make a crypto payment or transfer, first confirm that your wallet or service supports the exact asset and network in the recipient’s instructions. Then verify the destination, amount, and displayed transaction details before authorizing. What you can do next—and what happens after submission—depends on the asset, network, wallet, service, and your location.

## What “doing crypto” means for a payment or transfer

This guide uses “doing crypto” narrowly: preparing a payment or transfer of a digital asset. It does not cover trading, investing, mining, building applications, or setting up a merchant checkout system.

A cryptocurrency system includes protocol rules, a transaction ledger, and participants that update, store, and read the ledger. A wallet interface is one part of that system, not the whole payment process. The relevant network applies its own rules to transactions. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2018/cryptocurrencies-looking-beyond-hype>)

A wallet is used to generate, manage, store, or use cryptographic keys and blockchain addresses. The word “wallet” alone does not tell you who controls the keys or how the service handles access. [source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/wallet>)

Before acting, identify which parts of the process are handled by your wallet or service and which depend on the recipient’s instructions and the network. This distinction helps prevent a common planning error: treating a familiar screen or asset label as proof that a particular transfer route is supported.

## Before you send: choose the asset, network, and wallet carefully

Begin with the recipient’s payment instructions. Check the exact asset and network requested, and confirm that your wallet or service supports that combination. Payment instructions are not interchangeable across all assets and networks; compatibility and acceptance depend on the asset, network, destination, recipient requirements, and service. If any part is unclear, ask the recipient or service to confirm before preparing the transfer.

Do not rely on an asset name or symbol by itself. Confirm that the network selected in your wallet matches the network stated by the recipient. A destination intended for one network should not be assumed to work for another. Check the recipient’s requirements for any additional information, such as a memo or tag, rather than borrowing details from a different payment.

Next, understand how you access the wallet. With self-custody, the user controls the private keys and recovery material needed to access the crypto assets. This means the user is responsible for that material. [source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>)

If an exchange or other service manages access, consult that service’s current instructions for account access and recovery. Those steps are service-specific; the available information here does not establish a recovery procedure or guarantee that access can be restored. Do not proceed until you understand which access method applies to your setup.

Key management is more than writing down a recovery phrase once. General key-management guidance covers the lifecycle of keying material, including generation, storage, distribution, use, recovery, revocation, and destruction. For a beginner, the practical task is to know which credentials your setup requires, how you are expected to protect them, and what the provider says to do if access is lost. [source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/Projects/Key-Management/Key-Management-Guidelines>)

Keep private keys and recovery phrases private. Do not enter them into a form or send them to someone who contacts you with unsolicited instructions. A transaction check should involve verifying the payment details, not disclosing the material that controls access to your wallet.

## How to do crypto: prepare and verify a transaction

Use the wallet or service’s current instructions to start. Obtain the recipient’s address through a channel you trust, then compare it with the destination shown in the transaction. Verify the complete address and the network, not merely a familiar label or a few characters. If the address or network differs from the recipient’s instructions, stop and clarify the discrepancy before authorizing.

Enter the requested amount and confirm which asset the amount refers to. Read any accompanying recipient instructions carefully. If the recipient requires a memo, tag, or other field, follow the instructions for that specific destination. These requirements vary, so do not assume a field is optional—or required—because another service handled a previous transfer differently.

Before signing or confirming, review the transaction screen as a whole. Check the asset, amount, destination, selected network, and any required accompanying information. Review the fee and total debit if the interface displays them. If a field or charge is unclear, consult the wallet or service’s current documentation or support instructions instead of guessing.

Some network-specific details should not be generalized. For example, an Ethereum-family token transfer requires the sender to use the same network as the recipient and to hold enough native ETH to cover the transaction fee, unless a supported sponsored-fee mechanism applies. Fee assets and sponsored-fee behavior differ across other networks and wallet systems, so this example is not a rule for every crypto transfer. [source](<https://ethereum.org/guides/how-to-use-a-wallet>)

For a new recipient or unfamiliar process, check whether your wallet or service supports a small test transfer and whether its instructions recommend one. A test is a separate transaction, not a guarantee that a later payment will succeed. Verify the address and network again, and consider whether an additional fee or service limit applies. Not every wallet or service necessarily offers this option.

Authorize only when the final details match the payment you intend to make. A valid signature is associated with control of the relevant signing key for that transaction, but it does not by itself identify the human, organization, device, or service controlling the key. Treat key authorization and recipient identity as separate questions: check the payment instructions through a trusted channel rather than assuming the signature answers both.

## After confirmation: check status and keep records

After submission, use the wallet or service’s transaction status and instructions to follow progress. If you consult a network explorer, confirm that it relates to the network used for the transfer, then compare the displayed transaction details with your own records. A status label or transaction identifier can help you track the transfer, but this guide does not establish a fixed confirmation time or outcome.

Keep the transaction identifier, receipt, and relevant payment records somewhere you can access later. These records help you refer to the specific transfer if you need to check its status or contact the service. They are not a substitute for protecting credentials that control a self-custodial wallet. Never include a private key or recovery phrase in a receipt, message, or support request.

Do not assume that a submitted transaction can be stopped, reversed, refunded, or recovered. If the displayed information seems wrong or the status is unexpected, consult the wallet or service’s official support instructions. Whether support can help depends on the service and circumstances; there is no general guarantee of recovery. Keep any request focused on the transaction reference and the problem, not on disclosing secret access material.

## Fees, security, and rules depend on context

Fees are specific to the network and transaction. For Bitcoin on-chain transactions, fees can depend on transaction size and demand for block space rather than simply the amount transferred. This does not describe every crypto network or any additional charge imposed by an intermediary. Check the fee shown for the specific transaction and the current explanation from the wallet or service before confirming.

Supported assets and networks, service fees, transaction statuses, security controls, and recovery procedures can vary. Use current instructions for the wallet or service involved rather than relying on an old tutorial, an unrelated service’s screen, or an unsolicited message. If an instruction arrives unexpectedly, verify it using a channel you already trust before acting.

Legal, tax, and regulatory obligations depend on jurisdiction and individual circumstances. General Bitcoin guidance does not establish a jurisdiction-specific conclusion. Check the applicable local authority or consult a qualified professional for your situation.

Before authorizing any payment or transfer, make one final comparison: recipient instructions against the asset, network, destination, amount, and transaction details displayed by your wallet or service. If they do not match—or if you cannot tell what a field means—pause and seek clarification. The safe next step is to resolve uncertainty before confirmation, not to assume that a transfer can later be reversed or recovered.

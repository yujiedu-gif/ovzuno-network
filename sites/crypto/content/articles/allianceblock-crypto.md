## How to assess AllianceBlock’s exchange-market infrastructure role

“Exchange-market infrastructure” is a functional classification, not a label that follows from a broad association with markets or blockchain. Assess each proposed function separately. Does documentation say the service operates a trading venue, matches orders, routes orders, delivers market data, supports settlement, or provides another defined service to a venue or its users?

The distinction between supplying infrastructure and operating an exchange matters. A tool might support a venue without being the venue. A service that connects systems would not, for that reason alone, be an order-matching service, custodian, or settlement system. Each classification needs evidence explaining the service’s role, intended users, and interaction with other systems.

A conceptual map of an exchange environment can include participants and access interfaces connected to trading systems, alongside custody, settlement, data, and external-service relationships. Those relationships vary by arrangement; the map is a way to organize questions, not a universal design or a description of AllianceBlock.

A useful evidence record identifies the product version or publication date, describes the function in technical or operational terms, and clarifies whether the material describes a live service, proposal, or announcement. It should also identify who performs each task. Without that information, a claimed connection to exchange infrastructure remains unconfirmed.

## Infrastructure functions that should not be conflated

Exchange operation, order matching, data delivery, settlement support, custody, liquidity provision, and token issuance are distinct functions. An organization or system could combine some of them, but evidence for one does not prove the others. Exchange-related functions may also be divided among different firms and services rather than performed by one operator.

Liquidity provision illustrates why functional labels need boundaries. An automated market maker (AMM) is a trading design that uses pools of assets to facilitate swaps; liquidity provision means contributing assets to such a pool. A liquidity provider can experience inventory-value loss relative to holding the assets when pool prices move, and trading fees may or may not offset that outcome. This describes a possible exposure associated with a particular form of liquidity provision—not exchange operation, matching, or custody. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/trading-defi-era-automated-market-maker>)

Connectivity also needs precise description. A blockchain bridge coordinates token, message, data, or contract-call activity between otherwise isolated blockchain environments. In a cross-ledger transfer, assets are not physically moved from one ledger to another; designs coordinate value through mechanisms that can include minting, burning, locking, releasing, or swapping. The exact accounting and redemption path depends on the bridge design. This category should not be treated as proof of exchange order routing, matching, settlement support, or custody. [source](<https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/scaling/sidechains/>)

These distinctions are classification tools, not claims about AllianceBlock. The allowed evidence here does not link the name to any of these functions. Broad words such as “finance,” “market,” “liquidity,” or “blockchain” are not enough to assign a specific operational role.

## What evidence can establish product scope and status

Start with dated first-party technical documentation, product descriptions, and legal disclosures. Check what the materials say the product does, who operates it, who is meant to use it, and whether the described service is announced, available, discontinued, or geographically limited. These status categories are not interchangeable. For consequential claims, seek independent corroboration rather than treating a project’s own description as conclusive.

For technical scope, prefer descriptions of actual inputs, outputs, responsibilities, and system dependencies over broad promotional language. For example, a documented API schema may include an endpoint for indexing status alongside resources for blocks, transactions, addresses, contracts, tokens, logs, traces, and statistics. That demonstrates what the documented schema includes; it does not establish that a different project offers exchange data or any other capability. The schema is an implementation-specific example, not a universal route or a claim about AllianceBlock. Security claims require separate evidence. Review what the security architecture describes, what an audit actually covered and excluded, and whether relevant incident records are available. An audit reference by itself does not establish a security guarantee. Likewise, jurisdiction, regulatory or compliance status, fees, yields, and recovery procedures require current, appropriately authoritative documentation. The evidence available here does not establish any of these facts for AllianceBlock, so none should be inferred.

Evidence should also be matched to the exact claim. A document showing that a system can prevent ordinary updates or deletions in a particular type of database, for instance, would support a limited statement about that system’s record controls; it would not establish exchange operation, product availability, or the security of an unrelated service.

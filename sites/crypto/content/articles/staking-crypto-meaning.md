Crypto staking means committing eligible cryptocurrency to a network’s proof-of-stake process, either directly or through a protocol-specific delegation arrangement. Depending on the network, stake can influence consensus participation or weight; it does not by itself guarantee rewards, validator control, or risk-free access to the assets.

## What does staking crypto mean?

In proof of stake, cryptocurrency is committed as stake, and that stake can influence a participant’s role or weight in consensus. The details—including who may participate and what commitment means—are set by each protocol. “Staking” is therefore an umbrella term, not one universal procedure. [source](<https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/consensus-mechanisms/pos/>)

Staking is not simply holding an asset in a wallet. The relevant distinction is whether the assets have been committed under a network’s staking rules. For example, Ethereum’s proof-of-stake design uses ETH deposits as slashable economic collateral for validator participation. That is an Ethereum-specific description, not a rule for every network. [source](<https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/consensus-mechanisms/pos/>)

## How staked assets relate to validators

A validator is a network participant assigned protocol-defined duties. In Ethereum, validators check and attest to blocks and propose a block when selected. Their participation is tied to ETH deposited as slashable collateral.  [source](<https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/consensus-mechanisms/pos/>)

Some networks also allow delegation: a participant assigns stake to a validator, with the network’s rules determining the effect. Solana, for example, documents delegation that increases a validator’s consensus weight while the delegator retains token control under its stake-account model. This example should not be assumed to describe delegation elsewhere.

Selection rules, validator duties, and the role of stake differ among protocols. Staking assets does not necessarily mean the participant personally runs a validator or performs its duties. The network’s documentation is the place to check how a particular arrangement works.

## What happens when someone stakes

At a high level, a participant follows the applicable protocol’s process to commit or delegate assets. The network then applies its own rules to participation and to outcomes such as rewards, penalties, or withdrawal. A service may also be involved, but a service’s process should not be confused with the underlying protocol rules.

The relationship between participant and validator is not identical in every design. A delegated participant, for instance, can have a different operational role from the validator receiving the delegation. Solana’s documented model illustrates this distinction: delegation affects validator consensus weight, while the delegator retains token control under the stake-account model. Other networks may assign rights and responsibilities differently.

## Rewards, lockups, and risks are not universal

Rewards are protocol-specific and are not guaranteed. Ethereum’s protocol describes rewards for correct participation, missed rewards or penalties for inactivity, and slashing for specified conflicting messages. These are Ethereum rules; they do not establish reward rates or penalty terms for other networks. [source](<https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/consensus-mechanisms/pos/rewards-and-penalties/>)

Access to staked assets can also be subject to network-specific rules. For example, Tezos documentation distinguishes staked funds, which can be at risk of protocol slashing for specified delegate misbehavior, from unstaked delegation balances. Its cited documentation also warns that withdrawal delays and slashing rules are version-specific. This example is not a general statement about all staking systems. [source](<https://octez.tezos.com/docs/alpha/staking.html>)

Whether a participant uses a direct protocol process or a third-party service can also affect custody and operational arrangements. For Ethereum, third-party staking products introduce custody, smart-contract, operator, fee, and legal risks outside base-protocol staking. The exact terms of any particular service require separate review; they cannot be inferred from the word “staking.” [source](<https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/consensus-mechanisms/pos/>)

## Staking versus mining and ordinary holding

Proof-of-stake staking associates committed cryptocurrency with consensus participation under the network’s rules. Proof-of-work mining, by contrast, uses a computational process to produce blocks. The mechanisms are different, but that distinction alone says nothing about comparative profitability or safety.

Ordinary holding is not necessarily staking: an asset must be committed or delegated according to applicable protocol rules for the activity to count as staking. Before relying on the term, identify the network and check its current documentation for participation requirements, validator roles, rewards, penalties, and asset-access rules. No single definition supplies those details for every protocol.

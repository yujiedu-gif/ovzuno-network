## What the phrase “CDC crypto exchange” establishes—and what it does not

A search phrase is not proof of a company’s identity, an exchange brand, or a relationship between the two. The letters “CDC” could be used in different contexts; the phrase alone does not establish which meaning applies. It also does not show who operates a service, what product is offered, or where that service is available.

Separate three questions: What is the brand called? Which legal entity operates the service? What product or service does that entity provide? An accurate identification requires evidence connecting those elements. A matching acronym or a result in a search listing is not enough to confirm the connection.

The available evidence here does not name an operator or document an exchange product called “CDC.” That limitation means the phrase cannot be resolved on the evidence at hand; it does not prove that no related service exists. Keep the distinction clear: “I cannot identify it from this evidence” is not the same as “it does not exist.”

## How to verify an exchange identity

Begin with the service’s official product and legal disclosures. Look for the exact brand name, a description of the service, and the legal entity stated to operate it. Compare those details across the provider’s official pages. The goal is to establish a documented connection between the brand, operator, and exchange service—not merely to find one of the names in isolation.

Next, check relevant official records for the named entity and the jurisdiction in which the service claims to operate. Match the legal name, rather than relying on a similar brand or abbreviation, and verify that the record is relevant to the activity being claimed. A regulatory entry, if one exists, is a separate fact from the service’s identity. It should not be presented as regulatory endorsement or as proof of a particular user protection.

Prefer current, dated primary documentation and verify claims at their original source rather than relying on snippets or third-party summaries. Keep track of the exact entity name, product, jurisdiction, and date of the documentation you checked. If an official page does not make the relationship clear, or if the names conflict, leave the identity unresolved rather than guessing what “CDC” stands for.

A practical check is to ask whether the same named operator appears consistently in the product description and relevant legal disclosures, and whether the service being described is actually an exchange. If the documentation names a company but does not connect it to an exchange product, the company’s identity alone does not answer what the phrase refers to. If a product name appears without a clearly identified operator, the operator remains unconfirmed.

## Separate the exchange name from its infrastructure claims

Identifying a brand does not establish how a particular service handles assets or what functions it offers. Trading, custody, and other exchange-related functions may be combined by some centralized exchange operators, but that general pattern is not evidence about an unidentified provider. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>))

Custody is a distinct claim. A third-party custodian manages access to private keys on behalf of customers, and crypto exchanges are among the entities that may act as third-party custodians. That description does not establish that a particular service holds customer assets, what its custody arrangement is, or what rights a customer has under its terms. ([source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>))

Likewise, an exchange name alone does not substantiate claims about supported assets, market access, or other product features. Check documentation for the exact service and operator before attributing any such feature. Do not transfer an API, asset-support, custody, or service claim from another organization merely because its name or abbreviation looks similar. If documentation does not tie the feature to the identified product, treat it as unverified.

This distinction keeps identification separate from evaluation. First establish which service is being discussed. Only then can service-specific documentation be assessed for a particular operational claim. General statements about exchanges cannot fill in missing provider-specific evidence.

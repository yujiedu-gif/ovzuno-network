## What “Helios crypto” evidence identifies a project

Look for documentation that states who maintains the project, what the relevant product or software is called, and which network or service it concerns. Then check whether the document itself connects those names. A matching logo or similar spelling is not enough to establish that a token, organization, software tool, and network belong to the same project.

For a practical identity check, compare the reference against primary materials in this order:

1. **Project documentation:** Does it identify the project and describe the specific product or service?
2. **Official domain references:** Does project documentation point to the domain where the relevant material appears, and do the materials consistently identify the same project?
3. **Explicit relationships:** Does the documentation directly link the named organization, software, token, and network, rather than leaving their relationship implied?
4. **Current technical detail:** Is there documentation describing the product’s present role, supported network, and operating responsibilities?

Keep a note of what each source establishes and what it does not. A page identifying a software tool does not, by itself, establish that the project operates a validator service or offers a staking product. Likewise, a token mention does not establish that token holders can delegate or participate in validation.

Third-party directories, exchange listings, search snippets, and community posts can help locate primary sources. Treat them as leads to check, not conclusive evidence of project identity or current functionality. If primary references conflict, or if the project’s own materials do not make the relationship clear, report the identity as uncertain rather than choosing the most plausible interpretation.

## How to verify a staking or validator role

The evidence should describe a role, not merely use staking-related language in a project name or marketing phrase. Look for direct technical documentation that says what the project does and how it participates in a named network.

Distinguish among three different kinds of claim:

- **Software or node tooling:** Documentation should explain the software’s purpose and what an operator can do with it. Tooling alone does not show that its maker runs validators or accepts delegated assets.
- **Validator operation or service:** Documentation should say that the project operates validators or provides a validator service, identify the relevant network, and describe the responsibilities it performs. A general claim to support a blockchain is not enough to establish this role.
- **Staking product:** Documentation should explain whether the offering concerns delegation, pooled participation, or another arrangement, and identify who controls the relevant keys or assets. Do not infer a staking product from a validator tool or a token reference.

Then check whether the documented network and role match the claim you are evaluating. A project might describe software for a network without operating infrastructure on it; an operator might run infrastructure without offering a product to outside users. These are distinct statements and require distinct evidence.

If the documentation names no network, does not define the project’s responsibilities, or uses broad terms without explaining what users or operators actually do, the staking or validator connection is not established. In that case, the accurate conclusion is that the available documentation is insufficient—not that the project has a particular function.

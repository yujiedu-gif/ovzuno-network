## What counts as an NFT marketplace?

An NFT marketplace is a service or application for discovering, listing, or exchanging NFTs. It is distinct from the NFT token record, the blockchain on which that record exists, and the wallet or other tool used to interact with it. A marketplace may connect these parts of a workflow, but its interface does not by itself determine the behavior of a token or the operation of a network.

For comparison, keep the marketplace’s role separate from the underlying record. An NFT’s identity can depend on its contract address and token ID on a particular chain; names, symbols, and media alone are not reliable global identifiers. A marketplace page can help a person locate or view an item, but the page’s presentation should not be treated as a substitute for checking the token’s identifying details and the platform’s stated support. A marketplace can also depend on other components. For example, a wallet may manage keys and authorize a transaction, while an application provides a user-facing route to interact with a blockchain. The precise division of responsibilities varies by implementation, so compare what the marketplace itself does with what it expects a wallet, network, or external service to do.

## NFT marketplace examples by operating model

Marketplace descriptions commonly suggest broad models such as general-purpose services, curated services, or services associated with a particular ecosystem. These labels can help organize research, but they are not verified descriptions of any specific platform in the evidence available here. Nor do the labels prove quality, safety, or suitability.

A general-purpose model would be described in terms of a broad range of collections or NFT activities. A curated model would be described through selection or presentation criteria. An ecosystem-associated model would be defined by a stated relationship to a blockchain or application environment. These are comparison categories, not claims that any named marketplace currently fits one of them. A service could also combine characteristics or change its scope over time.

To classify a real platform, look for primary documentation that defines its operating model and the specific functions available. Distinguish what the platform says it supports from what its name, branding, or a third-party description might imply. If the documentation does not confirm a characteristic, leave it unclassified rather than treating a category label as evidence.

## Compare supported assets and networks

Asset scope and network scope are separate questions. First, identify which NFT categories or token formats a platform documents as supported. Then check the networks it names. Support for viewing or discovering an item is not necessarily the same as support for listing or completing a transfer; verify the specific action and asset combination relevant to the comparison.

A marketplace’s network support also does not establish interoperability. Even if a service lists more than one network, that fact alone would not prove that an NFT moves between those networks or that records on different networks represent the same token. Treat each chain and token representation as a distinct item to verify unless current documentation explains the relationship.

A useful comparison record can include:

- **Asset scope:** Which NFT categories or token formats does the platform explicitly document?
- **Network scope:** Which blockchain networks does it name, and for which functions?
- **Workflow scope:** Does the documentation cover discovery, listing, transfer, or another specific activity?
- **Eligibility:** Does it state conditions for collections, assets, or users?
- **Evidence status:** Is the source primary, current, and specific to the feature being assessed?

This checklist does not establish that any marketplace supports every region, collection, or action. It records only the claims confirmed by documentation. When a support list is unclear, incomplete, or out of date, preserve that uncertainty instead of inferring broader coverage.

## Marketplace features to examine for a use case

After confirming assets and networks, compare the functions relevant to the intended research or operation. A discovery-focused comparison may examine search and collection presentation. A creator or application-focused comparison may examine listing, transfer, or integration functions. Do not assume that similarly named features behave the same way across services; look for platform-specific descriptions of each workflow.

Separate platform features from wallet and transaction responsibilities. A wallet may manage keys and provide signing, while an application may assemble a request or display transaction details. The source claims available for this guide do not verify how particular NFT marketplaces handle custody, transaction flow, fees, account recovery, security controls, or compliance. Each of those is a platform-specific question that requires current primary documentation; absent that evidence, no conclusion about a named service is warranted.

For a careful comparison, record three things for each feature: what the documentation expressly says, what component performs the action, and what remains unspecified. This prevents a general platform description from being mistaken for proof about every interaction. It also keeps an interface’s security or compliance statements from being treated as a verified assessment of a particular user, asset, or transaction.

A **transaction service**, as used here, means a service that facilitates a purchase or another transaction. An **order book** is a record of buy and sell orders used in a trading venue’s matching process. These terms describe different functions. A service that facilitates purchases is not necessarily a venue where customers submit orders to be matched against other orders.

The distinction matters because “buying cryptocurrency” describes an activity from a customer’s perspective, not necessarily the infrastructure behind it. A purchase may involve a quoted transaction, a counterparty, or an execution route; an order-book model involves orders and matching. ## Transaction service versus order-book exchange

In a transaction-service model, the customer seeks to complete a purchase through a service. In an order-book model, buy and sell orders are recorded and matched according to the venue’s process. The models may be distinguished by the execution mechanism, but the information provided here does not show which mechanism Coinmama uses today.

A concrete example illustrates the limited meaning of “transaction service”: in the United States, a convertible virtual currency kiosk allows customers to exchange fiat currency for virtual currency, and supported two-way kiosks also allow virtual currency to be exchanged for fiat currency. This example concerns kiosks, not Coinmama. It shows that a crypto-related transaction can be facilitated without establishing that the operator runs an order-book market; it does not define every purchasing service or prove anything about another provider’s present functions. [source](<https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2025-08/FinCEN-Notice-CVCKIOSK.pdf>)

A quote or status display would also not, by itself, establish an order book. For instance, a documented invoice implementation can record a requested amount and a time-limited exchange rate while distinguishing states such as awaiting payment, processing, settled, expired, invalid, partial, overpaid, and late. That example describes payment and invoice tracking. It is not evidence that Coinmama uses this model, and it does not establish order matching. [source](<https://docs.btcpayserver.org/Users/invoices/>)

To classify a provider, look for explicit descriptions of the steps between a customer’s request and completion. Does the documentation describe a quote and a purchase transaction? Does it describe customer-submitted buy and sell orders, an order book, and matching? These questions help identify the functional model. If the documentation does not explain the execution path, the model remains unverified; do not infer an order book from the word “exchange,” or infer the absence of one from a purchase-oriented description.

Even a documented transaction or matching mechanism would answer only that specific question. It would not, on its own, establish the provider’s custody arrangements, legal structure, asset support, or complete set of services. Keep those classifications separate rather than treating one documented function as proof of another.

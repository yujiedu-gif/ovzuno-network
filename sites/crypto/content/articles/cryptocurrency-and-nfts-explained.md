Cryptocurrency is a digital asset that can be transferred between users of a blockchain network; an NFT is a distinct token identified individually rather than treated as an interchangeable unit. The practical difference is fungibility: equivalent cryptocurrency units are generally treated as interchangeable within their system, while NFTs are individually distinguishable. An NFT may be part of a crypto system without being a cryptocurrency itself. These are broad conceptual distinctions; a specific network’s documentation determines how its assets and transactions actually work.

## What cryptocurrencies and NFTs represent

A cryptocurrency is a digital asset used within a cryptographic network or protocol. The permitted evidence supports a narrow technical definition for blockchain-network cryptocurrency as a digital asset, credit, or unit that can be cryptographically transferred between users of a blockchain network. This definition is about the technical setting, not every legal or commercial use of the word “digital asset.” It also does not mean every asset associated with a blockchain is itself a cryptocurrency.

A cryptocurrency’s characteristics depend on the particular protocol. For example, ether is Ethereum’s native cryptocurrency and is part of that protocol’s market for computation. That is an Ethereum-specific example, not a universal description of how all cryptocurrencies are issued, used, or transferred. [source](<https://ethereum.org/developers/docs/intro-to-ethereum/>)

An NFT, or non-fungible token, is an individually distinguishable token. “Non-fungible” describes how the unit is identified and treated: it is not simply interchangeable with another unit as an equivalent item under the relevant system. That label alone does not explain what an NFT refers to, what practical function it serves, or what a holder’s rights may be.

The terms *cryptocurrency* and *NFT* therefore describe different features. Cryptocurrency refers to a kind of digital asset in a blockchain-network context; “non-fungible” describes the individual distinction of a token. The concepts can overlap in a crypto system, but the terms are not synonyms.

## How fungibility separates cryptocurrencies from NFTs

Fungibility is interchangeability between equivalent units under the relevant system. If a system treats two units as equivalent for its purposes, one can stand in for the other without the system treating them as distinct items. Cryptocurrency units are commonly described as fungible in this conceptual comparison. NFTs, by contrast, are distinguished as individual tokens.

This distinction is about representation and interchangeability—not price, quality, usefulness, authenticity, or legal status. Calling a unit fungible does not establish that it has a particular value or is reliable. Calling a token non-fungible does not establish that it is valuable, authentic, or connected to a particular right. The terms answer a narrower question: does the system treat units as interchangeable, or does it identify them individually?

Fungibility should also be understood relative to the system in question. A category label is not a complete description of a specific implementation. To understand what a particular asset represents, a reader needs to check the documentation for that asset and the network or protocol where it appears. The supplied evidence does not support a broader claim that every network implements fungibility in the same way.

## How cryptocurrency and NFTs can relate

Cryptocurrencies and NFTs can both be discussed in connection with crypto systems. A cryptocurrency may be native to a particular network, as ether is to Ethereum. An NFT may be an individually identifiable token in a crypto system. This association does not make the two categories identical: an NFT’s presence on a network does not, by itself, make it a cryptocurrency.

It is important not to turn one network’s operation into a rule for all systems. The available evidence supports Ethereum as a specific example but does not establish that every NFT uses Ethereum, that every NFT system requires cryptocurrency, or that cryptocurrency always pays for an NFT’s creation or transfer. Whether a particular network or transaction uses a cryptocurrency—and in what way—must be checked in documentation for that network and asset.

A useful way to keep the concepts separate is to ask two questions. First, is the asset an interchangeable unit or an individually distinguishable token? Second, what role, if any, does a particular network’s cryptocurrency play in that system? The first question concerns the asset’s representation. The second concerns protocol-specific operation. Neither answer should be inferred from the other.

## What an NFT does—and does not—establish

An NFT’s token record and any associated media, service, or other subject are not automatically the same thing. The existence of an individually identified token does not, by itself, establish what rights or utility it carries. The token label is not enough to determine whether a holder has a claim to associated content or services, or what conditions govern their use.

For that reason, do not infer ownership, intellectual-property rights, authenticity, permanence, or control over something outside the token solely from the fact that an NFT exists. Those conclusions require evidence about the relevant implementation, terms, and circumstances. The evidence available for this comparison does not establish universal rules for such rights or properties.

This limitation applies to comparisons with cryptocurrency, too. Fungibility describes whether units are treated as interchangeable; it does not answer who owns related rights, whether an asset is secure, or whether a service is available. Those are separate questions and require evidence specific to the asset and the claim being made.

## Questions to ask when evaluating either asset type

Start by identifying the network or protocol named in the asset’s documentation. Then look for the asset’s stated function and how the documentation says it is represented, issued, or transferred. These details are specific to the system; an example from one network should not be treated as proof of how another works.

For an NFT, check what the token record identifies and what separate documentation says about any associated media, service, or terms. Do not assume that the token alone establishes rights or control beyond the record. For a cryptocurrency, check whether the documentation identifies it as native to a particular network or describes another protocol-specific role; avoid generalizing that role to other assets.

Finally, match claims to evidence. Statements about legal status, regulation, taxes, fees, yields, availability, security, recovery, or compliance need current evidence specific to the asset, service, and relevant jurisdiction. The evidence for this comparison does not support conclusions on those subjects.

In short, cryptocurrency and NFTs are not interchangeable labels. Fungibility distinguishes units treated as equivalent from tokens identified individually. They may both appear within crypto systems, but their relationship and operation depend on the specific network and asset documentation—not on the labels alone.

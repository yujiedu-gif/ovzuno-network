DAO examples should be compared by their stated purpose, who can participate, and how decisions are approved and carried out. The evidence authorized for this article does not identify or document named DAO cases, so it cannot support a reliable directory of specific organizations or claims about their current rules. It does support a practical framework for comparing documented examples without treating the DAO label as proof of any particular governance quality.

## How to compare DAO examples fairly

A useful profile answers three questions. First, what purpose does the organization state? Second, who may take part in the relevant decisions? Third, what process turns proposals into decisions or actions? Keeping those questions separate helps prevent a description of one vote or contract from being mistaken for a full account of an organization’s governance.

A DAO may use proposals, voting, delegation, token-based or share-based membership, reputation, or multisignature execution. These are possible approaches, not a uniform template. Actual rights and execution paths depend on the DAO’s contracts, policies, and legal context. [source](<https://ethereum.org/dao/>)

For each example, distinguish written rules from observed practice. A document may describe who can submit a proposal or how a vote is conducted, while other arrangements determine whether an approved decision is implemented. A comparison should identify which part of the process its evidence covers instead of implying that a single mechanism explains the whole organization.

Participation also needs precise description. Permissionless access means participation is open; permissioned access is controlled by identified operators or authorized users. In practice, access rights can be divided: reading information, submitting transactions, validating activity, administering systems, and auditing need not be assigned together. [source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ir/8202/final>) Thus, “open participation” should not be used as shorthand for open authority over every decision.

A fair reference list should date its descriptions and mark historical material as historical. The available evidence here does not supply dated, case-specific materials about DAO identities, purposes, participation rules, or changes. As a result, the categories below describe what a reader should verify in examples—not claims that particular organizations currently follow a given model.

## Protocol governance: daos examples in context

For a protocol-governance example, first verify the stated remit: which protocol decisions, if any, does the organization’s process cover? Then identify the participants who can propose, deliberate, approve, or carry out those decisions. These roles may differ, so a profile should not collapse participation, authorization, and implementation into one general claim of “control.”

The decision path matters as much as the proposal format. A record of discussion or a vote does not by itself document whether the outcome is binding, who executes it, or whether protocol changes require coordination among other stakeholders. Those details need support from the specific organization’s current governing materials and relevant protocol documentation. The evidence supplied here contains no named protocol DAO and no case-specific record of these steps, so it cannot substantiate a named example or a current description.

Protocol-related mechanisms should also not be confused with general consensus terminology. Proof of stake, for example, uses cryptocurrency committed as stake, with stake influencing a participant’s consensus weight; selection, delegation, custody, rewards, penalties, and withdrawal rules depend on the particular protocol. [source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/proof_of_stake_consensus_model>) That describes a consensus model, not a complete account of how a DAO governs proposals or who has organizational decision rights. A DAO comparison should keep those subjects distinct unless case-specific evidence connects them.

## Community and public-goods organizations

A community or public-goods organization may state a collective coordination or funding purpose, but a comparison must establish that purpose from its own documentation. It should then specify who is eligible to participate and which decisions the process actually covers. Without those details, the category name alone cannot tell readers whether governance concerns project priorities, a particular funding decision, or some other scope.

A funding decision is not, by itself, evidence of how the organization is governed as a whole. To describe a documented case, check whether the material explains proposal submission, decision authority, and the route from approval to action. If the evidence reports only an outcome, the profile should report only that outcome and avoid extending it into claims about decentralization, effectiveness, or ongoing authority.

No named community or public-goods DAO is documented in the authorized evidence for this article. Listing familiar names or assigning them a current process would go beyond that evidence. For a publishable case entry, dated primary materials should establish the organization’s identity and stated purpose, while corroboration should be sought for consequential claims about how participation and decisions work. If evidence does not resolve a point, mark it as unknown rather than filling the gap by inference.

## Investment, collecting, and other collective models

Organizations with investment, collecting, or other collective objectives would need the same case-level verification: stated objective, eligible participants, decision scope, and approval and execution process. The objective alone does not show what participants own, what authority they hold, or what a governance decision can change. Those are separate questions that require evidence specific to the arrangement.

The authorized evidence does not verify named examples in these categories, their objectives, or their present governance. It therefore cannot support descriptions of particular investment or collecting DAOs. Nor does a governance mechanism establish legal ownership or control. Claims on those subjects require case-specific evidence and, where applicable, legal materials with a defined scope.

This limitation also keeps a governance comparison separate from an investment evaluation. A description of who may vote or what decisions are covered is not evidence about returns, token value, or whether someone should join or invest. Those questions are outside this article’s evidence and purpose.

## What the examples show—and what they cannot prove

The comparison framework can reveal differences among documented cases: one may define participation broadly or narrowly, another may use delegation, and another may specify a separate path for execution. But those contrasts should only be reported when evidence supports them for each case. The authorized material establishes that DAO governance can use varied mechanisms; it does not establish which named organizations use them today. [source](<https://ethereum.org/dao/>)

A blockchain’s shared record is also not a substitute for evidence about organizational authority. A blockchain can use cryptographically linked transactions, distributed ledger copies, validation, and conflict-resolution rules to maintain a tamper-evident shared history, but participation, consensus, privacy, finality, and governance vary across systems. [source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ir/8202/final>) A recorded decision therefore does not, on its own, show who was entitled to make it, whether it was implemented, or how the organization is governed more broadly.

For current comparisons, consult dated materials for each organization and record when the description was checked. Verify identity, stated purpose, participation rules, decision scope, and execution path separately; note material changes and unresolved points. In this evidence set, no named DAO satisfies that case-level documentation requirement. The supported conclusion is therefore a method rather than a list: DAO examples can differ in purpose and decision process, but a responsible comparison must demonstrate those differences from specific, dated evidence instead of inferring them from the label.

For institutional treasury teams, the “digital asset revolution” means a potential change in how assets and liabilities are represented, transferred, and governed—not a prediction about financial performance. A digital token can be a ledger record about an asset and its owner, with programmable rules for use or transfer. That record alone does not establish legal title, enforceability, or the quality of the asset it represents. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/tokenisation-context-money-and-other-assets-concepts-and-implications-central-banks>) Treasury analysis therefore needs to look beyond the token to the workflow, rights, controls, and organizations involved.

## What the digital asset revolution means in a treasury context

The phrase does not have one settled meaning in the available evidence. In a treasury context, it can describe potential changes enabled by programmable digital ledgers, including the representation of financial assets and liabilities. [source](<https://www.imf.org/en/publications/imf-notes/issues/2026/04/01/tokenized-finance-574921>) It should not suggest that all digital assets share the same design, rights, infrastructure, or operating model.

A token and the asset or right it refers to are distinct. The token is a technical record; it does not, by itself, determine who has a claim against an issuer or intermediary, whether that claim can be enforced, or what happens if a party fails. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/tokenisation-context-money-and-other-assets-concepts-and-implications-central-banks>) Treasury teams need to identify what the organization would actually hold or owe, and which record and contractual arrangement govern that position.

This definition also separates infrastructure change from investment claims. A digital representation or programmable transfer rule does not establish a suitable treasury use, expected outcome, or operational improvement. The narrower question is whether a particular process could be carried out differently—and what rights, controls, and dependencies that change would bring.

## Where digital assets could affect treasury workflows

A team can examine whether a particular arrangement changes how an asset is recorded, transferred, or governed. Token arrangements can support platform-based intermediation across the life cycle of financial assets. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/tokenisation-context-money-and-other-assets-concepts-and-implications-central-banks>) That describes a possible arrangement; it does not establish that a specific process will be faster, less costly, or easier to reconcile.

Start by mapping the current process and identifying the step that might change. For example, document who initiates an instruction, who approves it, how the counterparty receives it, which record is authoritative, and how completion is confirmed. Then ask whether a digital representation changes those steps or adds another record, service, or handoff to monitor. These questions help define the operational case without assuming that a tokenized process replaces existing treasury systems.

The answer depends on the asset, network, counterparties, and organizational setup. A technical capability in one arrangement does not establish that another arrangement is ready for production. A pilot or technical feature should be distinguished from a treasury process with defined authority, controls, reconciliation, exception handling, and responsibilities. The evidence available here does not establish universal effects on payments, settlement, liquidity visibility, or recordkeeping.

## Controls and operating decisions institutions must examine

A treasury workflow needs clear responsibility for authorization, approval, execution, reconciliation, and exceptions. For a digital-asset process, the organization should identify who performs each task and what evidence confirms the result. A ledger entry does not resolve those operating questions on its own.

Custody is one important distinction. In third-party custody, a custodian manages access to private keys on behalf of customers; crypto exchanges are among the possible third-party custodians. Custody structure, customer rights, segregation, insurance, regulation, and insolvency treatment vary by provider and jurisdiction. [source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>) A treasury team should therefore examine the actual arrangement rather than infer protections from a service label.

Custody can also be combined with other functions. A centralized crypto exchange operator may combine trading, proprietary activity, lending, token issuance, and custody, although not every operator performs every function. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>) Separate review of provider terms and legal structure is necessary to understand which functions are actually involved and what dependencies they create.

A practical control review should establish who can authorize and carry out a transfer, how access is controlled, how activity is recorded and reconciled, and how failed or unconfirmed instructions are handled. If a provider participates, document its responsibilities and the organization’s dependencies on it. If the process crosses systems or counterparties, identify the handoffs and how discrepancies are escalated.

Technical records should not be treated as evidence of legal rights. Before relying on a tokenized position, treasury and legal teams need to establish what claim or right the holder has and how any backing assets relate to it. The available evidence does not settle those questions for a particular arrangement: legal effect, segregation, redemption, and insolvency treatment depend on the arrangement and applicable law. Pair the operating assessment with review of the governing documents and the parties’ roles.

## Regulation, accounting, tax, and compliance are jurisdiction-dependent

A technical description cannot establish an organization’s legal, accounting, or tax treatment. Those questions depend on the asset, arrangement, entity, and relevant jurisdictions. The available evidence does not support general rules for these matters. Teams should not infer a conclusion from the fact that a record or transaction uses a digital ledger; obtain current, jurisdiction-specific analysis from qualified legal, tax, and accounting specialists.

Requirements may also depend on the activity and location. As one limited example, California kiosk rules effective January 1, 2025 require covered pre-transaction disclosures to show the digital-asset amount, fees and charges in U.S. dollars, the price charged, a referenced licensed-exchange price, and a warning when no reversal or refund method is provided. [source](<https://dfpi.ca.gov/regulated-industries/digital-financial-assets/digital-financial-assets-law-information-for-kiosk-operators/>) This is specific to a jurisdiction and activity. It is not a general rule for institutional treasury or a conclusion about other locations.

For a proposed process, confirm which activities the organization and its providers perform, where they occur, and which current requirements apply. Verify authoritative requirements and effective dates rather than relying on an old summary or assuming rules are identical across jurisdictions. Where facts or obligations remain uncertain, refer them for qualified review; technical feasibility alone does not establish permission to operate.

## A practical evaluation framework for treasury teams

A neutral assessment can proceed in four steps:

1. **Define the process.** Name the treasury task and the intended operational outcome. Describe the existing workflow and the specific step a digital-asset arrangement might change.
2. **Map rights and responsibilities.** Identify what the organization would hold or owe, which record matters, who controls each operation, and which counterparties or providers are involved. Confirm the governing arrangement rather than assuming the token itself establishes the relevant rights.
3. **Document controls and exceptions.** Specify authorization, approval, reconciliation, recordkeeping, and escalation responsibilities. Identify external dependencies and how failed, delayed, or unresolved operations would be handled.
4. **Verify issues beyond the technical design.** Obtain current, arrangement- and jurisdiction-specific review of legal, tax, accounting, security, and compliance questions. Verify any service, fee, or availability claim with reliable current evidence rather than inferring it from a general description of digital assets.

This framework does not prescribe adoption. It helps distinguish a change in representation or transfer mechanics from a complete treasury operating model. The central evaluation is whether a defined process can be governed with clear rights, responsibilities, controls, and evidence—and whether unresolved questions have been referred to the appropriate specialists.

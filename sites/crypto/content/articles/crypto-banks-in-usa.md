“Crypto bank” does not establish that a provider is a bank or explain who provides each service. In the United States, verify the legal entity offering the product, check that entity against current official banking records, and read the product’s account and custody disclosures. The evidence available here does not identify specific banking records or establish any provider’s charter, regulator, or deposit-insurance status, so those details must be checked in current official sources.

## What “crypto bank” can mean in the United States

The phrase may describe different arrangements: a bank offering a service connected to crypto, a crypto company providing account or custody features, or a product involving several providers. The label alone does not settle which arrangement applies. Start by identifying the legal entities involved and the function each one performs, rather than treating “bank” in a brand or product description as evidence of bank status.

A crypto wallet is not necessarily a bank account. A wallet generally manages private keys or passcodes used to access and authorize crypto assets; it does not store the assets themselves. A wallet feature or displayed balance therefore does not, by itself, establish who holds the asset or what legal terms govern it. [[source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>)]

This explainer covers provider status and service verification. It is not a provider directory or recommendation, and it does not explain how a crypto card converts assets for purchases.

## Crypto banks in USA: verify the institution and its status

Begin with the exact legal name of the entity offering the service. A brand, app, or corporate group may present a product while another entity provides a particular financial or crypto function. The name visible in an interface may not identify the entity responsible for every part of the service.

Compare the legal name with current official federal or state banking records. Confirm that the record applies to the same entity, and read what the record actually establishes. A record for one company does not prove that an affiliate, product, or partner has the same status. Similarly, do not infer a charter or regulator from branding or an app’s appearance; check any stated status against the relevant official record.

The evidence available for this article does not identify the appropriate banking databases or establish any particular provider’s charter, regulator, or status. It also does not support a general statement that crypto assets are covered by deposit insurance. For an insurance-related claim, consult current official deposit-insurance guidance and the account disclosures for the named institution. Determine which institution holds the account and what balances the disclosures address. Do not extend an insurance statement about an account to crypto assets without explicit supporting documentation.

Treat a provider’s description of its status as a lead to verify, not as a substitute for the record. The relevant question is not only whether a bank appears somewhere in a product’s structure, but whether the official record and applicable account documents identify the entity responsible for the service being considered. If the legal name in the agreement differs from the brand name, keep those entities distinct while checking the records.

## Separate the bank, crypto provider, and payment service

A single product can involve separate entities for fiat accounts, crypto custody, and payment processing. Read the agreements and disclosures for the specific product. Identify the legal entity named as the account provider, the entity responsible for crypto custody or key access, and any entity described as handling payment functions. Then check which terms apply to each role.

Custody arrangements can differ. In a common centralized-exchange model, a customer transfers funds and custody to the exchange before trading through its internal order book. That describes a particular exchange model; it is not a rule for every product marketed as crypto banking. [[source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>)] The point for verification is to locate the product-specific custody description rather than assume that an account feature means the provider holds crypto in the same way it holds fiat funds.

Assess partner relationships narrowly. If an agreement identifies a bank in connection with one part of a product, check which entity provides the other functions and which agreements govern them. A bank partnership or the availability of a card does not, by itself, show that the bank provides crypto custody, trading, or payment processing. Keep the roles separate in your notes: account institution, crypto custodian or access manager, and payment provider may be different entities.

## Check service availability, fees, and applicable rules

Consult the provider’s current official terms for eligibility and geographic availability. The evidence here does not establish that any particular product is currently available nationwide or that every customer can access the same features. Treat availability as a product-specific question to verify, not an assumption based on the phrase “in USA.” Check the terms applicable to the named account and service rather than relying on a general company description.

Review the applicable fee schedule and transaction terms. Identify charges and conditions tied to access, transfers, conversion, or settlement when those functions are offered. Do not assume that a fee shown for one service covers another entity’s service or a different transaction type. If the product documents describe multiple providers, match each fee and condition to the relevant service and legal entity.

Regulatory, tax, and compliance questions also require current, authoritative support. ## Assess custody, security, and recovery disclosures

Read the account and custody disclosures to understand who controls access to crypto assets and what responsibilities remain with the customer. Look for the provider’s explanation of asset treatment, access controls, recovery procedures, complaint channels, and incident handling. The evidence available here does not establish how any named provider handles these matters, so a claim about a specific firm requires specific, current support.

Distinguish a documented process from a guaranteed result. A provider’s description of recovery steps does not prove that assets can always be recovered or that access will be restored in every circumstance. Broad words such as “secure” or “protected” are not enough to establish what safeguards apply. Check whether the disclosures describe the safeguards and define their scope for the particular product.

Use this verification checklist before relying on the term “crypto bank”:

- Match the legal name in the product documents to current official federal or state banking records; confirm which entity the record covers.
- Check any stated charter or regulator against the relevant official record instead of relying on branding.
- Verify insurance-related statements against official guidance and the named account institution’s disclosures; do not assume crypto assets are insured deposits.
- Identify separately who provides the fiat account, manages crypto custody or access, and handles payment functions.
- Read current product terms for eligibility, geographic availability, fees, custody, and recovery procedures.
- Seek current authoritative materials for regulatory, compliance, or tax questions, and do not treat general information as an individual determination.

If records or agreements do not make the provider roles clear, the available information is not enough to classify the service confidently. “Crypto bank” is a starting label to investigate, not evidence of bank status or a complete description of the services offered.

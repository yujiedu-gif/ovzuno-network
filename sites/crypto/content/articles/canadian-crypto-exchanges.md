A Canadian business assessing or using a crypto exchange should treat security and compliance as separate, evidence-dependent workstreams. It should identify which exchange functions its workflow relies on, verify current provider documentation and terms, and assign internal responsibility for access, approvals, and review. The evidence available for this explainer describes general exchange models; it does not establish Canadian regulatory requirements or the practices, registration status, security, or availability of any particular provider.

## What Canadian crypto exchanges do in business operations

A crypto exchange is not necessarily a single, uniform service. In a common centralized-exchange model, a customer transfers assets to the venue before trading through its internal system. Custody and legal ownership arrangements can vary by platform and jurisdiction. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>))

A centralized venue commonly records customer orders and executed trades in an internal order book and account system. An operator may combine trading with functions such as custody, lending, proprietary activity, or token issuance, but not every exchange performs every function. Combining roles can create operational dependencies or conflicts. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/aer-2022/future-monetary-system>))

For a business, the first operational task is to identify the service actually being used. Is the arrangement limited to trading through an internal venue, or does it also involve the provider holding assets or performing another function? The available evidence does not establish which services a named provider offers or what terms apply to a particular business. Confirm the service and entity involved in current provider materials rather than inferring them from the word “exchange.”

This distinction helps define the boundary between provider and customer responsibilities. A provider may operate parts of a service, but the business still needs to decide who may access its account, who may authorize transfers, and who reviews activity. Do not assume a product label explains that division. Map the actual workflow and compare it with the provider’s documentation and the business’s own procedures.

The business should also consider whether one provider function depends on another. Where an operator combines roles, the business can ask which entity performs each function, where instructions and records are handled, and which terms govern the relationship. These are verification questions, not claims that a particular provider combines functions or that any one organizational structure is inherently unsuitable.

## Security controls to verify before relying on an exchange

Treat a security statement as a claim requiring evidence, not as proof of an outcome. Request current documentation describing controls relevant to the service and account arrangement. Depending on the workflow, questions may concern account access, withdrawal safeguards, incident handling, and custody arrangements. The evidence available here does not confirm that any specific provider has particular controls or that controls prevent loss.

Separate provider descriptions from independent assurance. Provider documentation can explain what the provider says it does. If independent assurance is offered, check what entity, system, service, and period it covers; do not treat evidence about one scope as proof about another. The permitted evidence here does not establish the existence, scope, or result of assurance for any provider. Neither a provider statement nor an assurance document should be read as a guarantee of continuous service, recovery, or protection from compromise.

The business must address its own side of the control boundary. Assign who can access the account, who can initiate an instruction, who approves it, and who reviews completed activity. Where duties are split among staff, document the division and the path for resolving an exception. These are internal operating decisions; they do not establish that a provider uses matching controls or that its controls replace the business’s approvals.

Ask how the business’s process connects to the provider’s documented process. For example, determine what evidence the business will retain to show an instruction was approved and how staff will handle a discrepancy or an unexpected request. The available claims do not specify required records, provider response procedures, or a universally appropriate approval design. Set those details from the actual workflow and verify provider-specific facts directly.

## Canadian compliance responsibilities and verification

The allowed evidence for this article does not identify Canadian registration, licensing, or compliance obligations for an exchange or its customer. It cannot establish whether a provider is registered, which rules apply to a particular service, or how a business’s activities are classified. Those questions require current authoritative Canadian sources and assessment of the specific facts.

When a provider makes a regulatory or compliance statement, identify its precise scope before relying on it: which legal entity, which service, which jurisdiction, and the date of the supporting material. A general statement may not answer whether a particular workflow or business activity is covered. Verify the claim against current official Canadian material and consult qualified Canadian counsel for legal analysis. This explainer does not provide a legal conclusion.

Keep the business’s actual conduct in view. The relevant analysis may depend on the service and activity, but the evidence available here does not resolve those details. Do not infer that a provider’s status, even if verified for one entity or service, settles the compliance questions for a different entity, product, counterparty, or workflow. Record what remains uncertain rather than filling gaps with assumptions.

This distinction also matters operationally: provider due diligence and the business’s own compliance assessment are related but not interchangeable. Provider documentation can be evidence about that provider’s stated service or position; it does not, on the evidence available here, determine the business’s legal obligations. Recheck official sources and seek qualified advice when a decision depends on current Canadian requirements.

## Operational checks for business continuity and transfers

Map the workflow from internal authorization through the exchange interface and into the business’s records. Identify who approves a transfer, who submits it, and who checks whether the business’s records agree with the information available from the provider. The evidence here does not prescribe a specific reconciliation method or establish which records a particular exchange supplies, so document the steps that fit the verified workflow.

Record the service dependencies and escalation route before relying on the workflow. Ask the provider for current information about incident communications and operating procedures relevant to the service. Identify who in the business receives a notice, who assesses its impact, and who can pause or revise internal activity under the business’s own process. These questions support readiness; they do not imply that a provider will be available at a particular time or that an incident will lead to recovery.

Verify transfer limits, processing conditions, fees, and service availability in current provider terms before building those assumptions into an operating plan. The allowed evidence does not establish any such details for a particular exchange. Note the terms’ scope and date, and check whether they apply to the specific account, service, or transaction type under consideration.

Do not infer the cost or speed of a specific business transaction from broad statements about exchange models. Market liquidity can reflect the time and expense of buying or selling, including spread, transaction cost, price impact, and immediacy; it changes with transaction size and market conditions. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/speeches/20160502-market-and-funding-liquidity-overview>)) That general description is not a provider-specific fee schedule, processing-time estimate, or promise of execution conditions.

A practical continuity review should distinguish confirmed procedures from unresolved dependencies. If a provider’s documentation does not answer a question about service conditions or incident escalation, mark it for confirmation rather than treating silence as evidence of a particular outcome. The available claims provide no basis for asserting a recovery guarantee, transfer completion time, or uninterrupted access.

## What to document before adopting an exchange workflow

Create a dated record of the intended business use, the exchange function involved, the internal control owners, the provider evidence reviewed, and unresolved questions. For each item, distinguish verified facts from assumptions and note whether confirmation must come from the provider, a current Canadian authority, or qualified counsel. This record is an internal review aid, not certification that a provider is compliant or that the workflow is secure.

Include the limits of the evidence reviewed. For example, note whether a document describes a general model rather than a named provider, whether a provider statement concerns only one service, and whether independent assurance was available and what its scope covered. Do not turn a general description of centralized exchange operations into a conclusion about a particular company’s custody structure, legal status, or controls.

Reassess when the service, business workflow, provider documentation, jurisdictional information, or relevant operating conditions change. A review date makes the evidence boundary visible, but does not keep a claim current indefinitely. For decisions that depend on Canadian legal requirements, use current authoritative sources and qualified legal advice rather than treating this operational explainer as a compliance determination.

This framework is not a vendor ranking, exchange comparison, or tax advice. Its purpose is to help Canadian business operators separate provider functions from customer responsibilities, identify evidence that needs verification, and document what remains unknown before relying on an exchange workflow.

## What “DAI crypto” refers to

The phrase “DAI crypto” refers to DAI as a crypto asset. But the evidence available here does not substantiate further DAI-specific facts, such as how it is issued, what supports its value, which networks represent it, or whether a particular token contract is current. Those details should not be inferred from the name alone.

It is useful to keep the asset distinct from the services surrounding it. A wallet is software or a service involved in managing keys; an exchange may provide trading or custody; and an application interface may help a user initiate activity. These components do not establish what DAI itself is or guarantee that a particular service supports the correct representation. Verify each component against current documentation.

## How DAI fits into crypto infrastructure

A crypto-asset transfer can involve a signing key, an address, and a blockchain network. A digital signature based on asymmetric keys can authorize a transfer under a network’s protocol. The signature demonstrates control of the signing key for that protocol; it does not, by itself, establish the signer’s legal identity or whether the transaction is appropriate.

That general mechanism is not a DAI-specific transfer guide. Before describing a DAI workflow, an operator needs current technical documentation identifying the intended asset representation, network, and transfer requirements. The allowed evidence does not confirm those details or establish that support is consistent across wallets, exchanges, or other services.

Protocol behavior and service-provider behavior should also be evaluated separately. A service can impose its own access rules and operational processes; an exchange, for example, may combine trading, proprietary activity, lending, token issuance, and custody functions. Not every exchange performs all of these activities, and combining roles can create operational dependencies or conflicts. The existence of a service relationship does not establish the exact custody or transaction arrangement for a given DAI workflow.

## Security and custody questions for operators

Start by identifying who controls the private keys used to authorize activity. In a third-party custody arrangement, a custodian manages access to private keys on behalf of customers; crypto exchanges are among the possible third-party custodians. This description does not establish a particular provider’s terms, customer rights, segregation practices, security controls, or insolvency treatment. Those must be checked in current provider documentation and the relevant contractual materials.

For each proposed workflow, document who can initiate and approve transactions, how activity is monitored, and who handles incidents. The evidence available here does not verify any DAI-specific access-control design, audit, monitoring capability, incident procedure, or recovery process. Do not treat a general description of custody or transaction signing as proof that a service provides a particular safeguard.

Recovery also requires specific evidence. Ask the relevant service or wallet documentation what recovery methods exist, who can use them, and what their limitations are. The allowed evidence does not establish a recovery process for DAI or any named wallet. Avoid assuming that lost key material, mistaken transfers, or service interruptions can be reversed or remedied.

## What to verify before using DAI in a workflow

Before an organization approves a DAI-related workflow, assemble evidence for the exact asset representation and network involved, then confirm that the intended wallet, exchange, or other service supports that specific configuration. Check transfer instructions and provider terms directly; the evidence available here does not identify a current DAI contract, network, or service integration.

Next, record the custody model and responsibilities: who controls signing keys, who may authorize transactions, how activity is reviewed, and where incident responsibilities sit. Confirm any recovery procedures and their limits from current documentation rather than assuming a recovery option exists.

Finally, document the jurisdiction-specific compliance review and verify service availability and fees with the relevant provider. The allowed evidence does not establish current fees, yields, availability, or DAI-specific security properties. If any of these points remain unverified, record them as open questions rather than treating general crypto mechanisms as answers about DAI.

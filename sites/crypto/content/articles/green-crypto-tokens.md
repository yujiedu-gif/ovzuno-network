“Green” is not a standardized technical category for crypto tokens. The label may refer to a token’s stated purpose, an activity it helps fund or track, or an asserted environmental outcome. To assess the claim, identify the activity and impact it covers, examine how the evidence was produced, and note what remains uncertain. A token’s purpose does not, by itself, establish that an environmental benefit occurred or that the network it uses has a particular environmental footprint.

## What “green” can mean for crypto tokens

A token may be presented as part of a financing or tracking workflow for an environmental activity. Token arrangements can support platform-based intermediation across the life cycle of financial assets, but that role does not itself establish an environmental result. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/tokenisation-context-money-and-other-assets-concepts-and-implications-central-banks>))

The token record and the activity it refers to are separate things. A token can support standardized ownership and transfer records for individually distinguishable token IDs that applications associate with digital or physical subjects. The meaning, authenticity, utility, and legal effect of the associated subject, however, depend on additional systems and terms. ([source](<https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-721>))

For example, a token associated with a physical asset does not, just by existing, prove that the asset is authentic or that a claimed environmental outcome followed. An interface for linking a token to a physical asset or device still depends on the device, key protection, issuer, firmware, and the process that binds the physical item to the onchain record. ([source](<https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-4519>))

Treat “green” as a claim to investigate, not as proof supplied by the label. First determine whether the statement concerns the token’s intended role, a project’s activity, or a measured environmental result. These assertions differ, and evidence for one does not automatically support the others.

## Separate token purpose from network footprint

Start by identifying the system and activity relevant to the claim. A statement about a token-funded project is not automatically a statement about the network that records token activity; a statement about a network is not automatically a measurement of every token or project on it. Keep the claimed project outcome distinct from the token’s recording or transfer role.

A useful review asks the claimant to define the boundary: which activity is included, which period is covered, and what the reported figure or outcome represents. If the claim makes a comparison, ask what was measured or estimated and what assumptions or allocation method connect that figure to the token or project. Without those details, readers cannot evaluate the comparison from the claim alone.

Do not infer a network’s energy use or emissions from a token’s name, purpose, or association with an environmental project. The evidence available for this article does not establish network-level energy or emissions figures, a method for allocating network impacts to individual tokens, or a basis for comparing networks. Those points require relevant, specific evidence before they can support a conclusion.

This boundary matters because a token may identify or refer to an activity without measuring the resources used to record or transfer it. Conversely, a network-level estimate would not, on its own, establish the environmental outcome of a particular project. Keep the unit of analysis explicit: token, project, activity, or network. If the claim moves between these levels, ask for evidence supporting each step rather than treating them as interchangeable.

## What evidence supports a green token claim?

Use a claim-by-claim review instead of treating “green” as one broad promise:

- **Define the objective.** What environmental activity or outcome is claimed? Is the statement about financing, tracking, or an outcome said to have occurred?
- **Set the boundary.** Which project, activity, and time period are covered? What is expressly outside the claim?
- **Request the method.** How was the outcome measured or estimated, and what assumptions connect the result to the token or activity?
- **Identify the evidence source.** Is the information self-reported, supported by project records, or checked by another party? Can the stated method and underlying records be reviewed?
- **Explain the token’s role.** How does issuance, holding, transfer, or use relate to the activity? A token record associated with a project is not, on its own, proof that the token caused an outcome. ([source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>))

The distinction between an onchain record and an authoritative record can also matter. In issuer-sponsored tokenized-security models described by SEC staff, the crypto network may form part of the master ownership record, or an onchain transfer may trigger an update to an authoritative offchain record. This example shows why reviewers should ask which records substantiate a claim and how they relate. It is not evidence of environmental impact and does not describe every token arrangement. ([source](<https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/corp-fin-statement-tokenized-securities-012826-statement-tokenized-securities>))

For practical review, keep the materials that support each step together: the claim as published, its stated boundary and period, the methodology, and the project or activity records it relies on. If an independent check is claimed, identify what was checked and what the reviewer could access. A statement that information was reviewed is less useful when the scope of that review or the evidence examined is unclear.

## Limits, trade-offs, and misleading shortcuts

A token may be associated with an activity without demonstrating that the token caused the activity or its result. Association, financing, tracking, and verified outcome are distinct claims. Evidence for one should not be presented as proof of another.

Be cautious with broad labels that do not specify a project, boundary, period, or method. Ask whether relevant effects outside the stated boundary have been omitted. The evidence available here does not establish a universal lifecycle method, offset standard, or equivalence test for green crypto-token claims. Do not infer such a conclusion from a label or project description.

Missing data is a limitation, not evidence of zero impact. If methods change over time, ask which method was used for each reporting period and whether earlier figures were recalculated or explained. If the claimant cannot describe how a result was measured or estimated, treat it as unsubstantiated for the purpose of the review rather than filling the gap with an assumption.

Also distinguish the existence of a record from the truth or completeness of the underlying environmental information. A token may connect to records or a physical asset through additional systems, but those links involve dependencies beyond the token itself. The available evidence here does not establish that any particular record is accurate, that a reported outcome is independently verified, or that an environmental benefit has been achieved. ([source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>))

## Questions to ask before relying on a green token claim

Before repeating or relying on a claim, seek clear answers to these questions:

1. What precisely is claimed: intended purpose, funding, tracking, or an environmental outcome?
2. Which project or activity, boundary, and time period does the claim cover?
3. What method and source records support it, and can another party review them?
4. How does the token’s issuance or use relate to the claimed activity, without assuming causation?
5. What is excluded, uncertain, or subject to change, and how are corrections disclosed?

If the answers leave the boundary, method, or evidence unclear, state that limitation plainly. A careful assessment can describe what the token is intended to do and what records support the claim while avoiding a stronger environmental conclusion than the evidence allows.

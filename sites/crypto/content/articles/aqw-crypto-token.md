A label such as “AQW” is not enough to identify a crypto token. To determine what AQW refers to, look for attributable project documentation that names the asset, its network, contract address, and stated function, then compare those details with records on that network. The evidence available for this explainer does not establish a specific AQW project or contract, so its identity and role remain unverified.

## What the AQW crypto token label can—and cannot—tell you

A ticker or name is a search clue, not a complete identifier. It does not, by itself, establish which project issued an asset, where it exists, or what it does. Do not infer a particular token’s existence, affiliation, or purpose from the letters “AQW” alone.

On Ethereum, an ERC-721 token is uniquely identified by the combination of its contract address and token ID on a particular Ethereum chain. That is a useful example of why a displayed name may not be enough to distinguish a token. It does not establish that AQW is an ERC-721 token, that AQW exists on Ethereum, or that the same identification method applies to every token standard. ([source](<https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-721>))

A token’s name and ticker are also different from evidence about its function. A project might describe a token as connected to an application or protocol, but that description alone does not show what action the software permits or what conditions apply. Keep the project’s wording separate from details that can be checked against a network record.

For AQW specifically, the materials available here do not confirm a full token name, a project, a network, a contract address, or a function. That limitation should remain visible in any description: without those details, a reader cannot reliably connect the label to a specific asset. It is more accurate to call the identity unverified than to select a plausible search result and treat it as definitive.

## Evidence to check before identifying AQW

Begin with documentation that can be attributed to the project. Look for an explicit statement of the asset’s full name, ticker, network, contract address, and intended function. Note what the source actually says, and record any missing fields rather than filling them in from a different page or assumption.

Then compare the published contract address with records on the named network. The address should be treated as a precise reference to check, not as proof by itself that the project’s claims about ownership, purpose, or affiliation are true. If project materials and network records do not match, leave the discrepancy unresolved unless a source explains it. Do not silently choose one version.

A practical identification worksheet can keep the evidence organized:

- **Name:** What full name and ticker does the project documentation give? Are they presented together, or is the ticker appearing without a source that connects it to a full name?
- **Network:** Which network does the documentation identify? Is that information specific, or does it merely use a broad term such as “blockchain”?
- **Contract:** Is an address actually published? If so, does it correspond to a record on the named network?
- **Attribution:** Can the documentation be connected to the project, rather than an unaffiliated page, copied description, or search snippet?
- **Function:** Does the source explain what the token is said to do, or only use broad labels such as “utility” or “governance” without describing a mechanism?

These questions are a way to report what has and has not been verified; they do not guarantee that a project is genuine or that its claims are correct. If a field is absent, conflicting, or impossible to attribute, mark it as unknown. If details are present but cannot be independently matched, describe them as claims made by the source rather than as established facts.

## How to assess the token’s stated role

Separate a project’s description of a token from a verifiable explanation of how it is used. A label such as “utility,” “governance,” or “access” is not a complete account of the token’s role. Look for documentation explaining what software or protocol uses it, what action a holder or application can take, and what conditions the documentation places on that action.

A digital token can be a ledger record about an asset and its owner, with programmable rules governing use or transfer. A technical record does not by itself determine legal title, enforceability, or the quality of the represented asset. This general distinction is not a finding about AQW; it is a reason not to treat a token description as proof of rights beyond what the evidence supports. ([source](<https://www.bis.org/publications/tokenisation-context-money-and-other-assets-concepts-and-implications-central-banks>))

When writing or evaluating a description of AQW, preserve the source of each claim. For example, “the project documentation describes the token as…” is different from stating that the token definitively provides a particular right or function. If the documentation does not explain how AQW interacts with the relevant software, say that its stated role is unclear or unconfirmed.

This distinction also helps avoid combining separate assertions into a broader conclusion. Evidence that a token record exists would not, by itself, establish the project’s identity, the token’s usefulness, or a holder’s rights. Evidence for one of those points should not be treated as proof of the others.

## Risks of relying on a name or listing

Similar names, tickers, or copied branding can cause readers to confuse different assets. Verify the network and contract independently against attributable project materials and the relevant network record. A search result or a page that displays a token name is not, by itself, sufficient evidence of project affiliation.

The distinction between a token and the asset or rights associated with it is important in particular when a project describes a token as representing something beyond the token record. NFT standards, for example, support standardized ownership and transfer records for individually distinguishable token IDs, while the meaning, authenticity, utility, and legal effect require additional systems and terms. This is a general point about that standard, not evidence that AQW is an NFT. ([source](<https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-721>))

Similarly, NFT technology may support intellectual-property management, transfer, or licensing workflows when valid legal terms and authorized rights holders are present. That possibility does not establish that a particular token conveys intellectual-property rights, and it is not evidence about AQW. ([source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>))

Do not infer that AQW has been audited, can be recovered, or is protected by a particular safeguard unless evidence specific to that asset supports the statement. The information available for this explainer does not establish any such details. The same caution applies to claims that a token’s name or appearance proves its source: attribution should be tied to evidence, not assumed from branding.

# Digital Currency vs Cryptocurrency: What’s the Difference?

## The short answer

“Digital currency” and “cryptocurrency” are related terms, but they do not describe the same thing in every context. The supplied sources support a precise technical definition of cryptocurrency: the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) defines it as a digital asset, credit, or unit that can be cryptographically transferred between users of a blockchain network. NIST also describes transfers as authorized by digital signatures based on asymmetric-key pairs.

The source pack does not provide a general definition of “digital currency” or evidence establishing that cryptocurrency is commonly treated as its subset. So this comparison should not present that category relationship as a sourced fact. Instead, it can distinguish what the cited technical definition does establish from broader uses of the term “digital currency,” which must be defined in the context where it appears.

That qualification matters for builders, operators, institutions, and technical decision-makers. A label alone does not tell you who issues a unit, what obligation it represents, where records are kept, or how transactions are handled. Those questions require system-specific evidence.

## What counts as digital currency?

The available sources do not establish a single general definition for “digital currency.” The phrase may be used in a particular document or system description, but its scope should be checked there rather than assumed. In particular, describing something as digital does not, on the evidence supplied here, establish that it uses a blockchain, has a particular issuer, or follows a particular transaction-validation process.

A central bank digital currency (CBDC) is a more specific category with a source-backed description. The Federal Reserve generally defines a CBDC as a digital liability of a central bank that is widely available to the public. This definition identifies the nature of the liability and the intended availability described by the Federal Reserve; it does not establish that every central bank has issued a CBDC or that one is available everywhere. The Federal Reserve states that it has not decided to issue a U.S. CBDC.

A CBDC therefore offers a useful example of why scope and attribution matter. The Federal Reserve’s description says what it means by CBDC in that context. It should not be expanded into an unsupported universal definition of digital currency, nor should it be taken as a statement about current issuance in other jurisdictions.

## What makes something a cryptocurrency?

NIST’s glossary, drawing on NIST IR 8202 and related NIST publications, defines cryptocurrency as a digital asset, credit, or unit that can be cryptographically transferred between users of a blockchain network. This is a technical definition with a specific blockchain-network condition. The source explicitly limits its scope: it describes blockchain-network cryptocurrency rather than every legal category called a digital asset.

NIST identifies digital signatures based on asymmetric-key pairs as the mechanism used to authorize a cryptocurrency transfer between users. The definition does not, by itself, establish who issued or backs the unit, what governance arrangements apply, or which transaction-validation process a particular network uses. Nor does the presence of cryptographic transfer alone answer every legal or institutional classification question.

The source also sets a limit on what authorization means: a valid signature proves control of the signing key under the protocol, not the legal identity of the person controlling it or the wisdom of the transaction. These are separate questions, and the signature mechanism should not be treated as an answer to either one.

## Examples and category boundaries

**A central bank digital currency.** Under the Federal Reserve’s general description, a CBDC is a digital liability of a central bank that is widely available to the public. Whether a specific CBDC also fits NIST’s definition of cryptocurrency would depend on whether it meets that definition’s conditions, including cryptographic transfer between users of a blockchain network. The supplied information does not support a blanket answer for all CBDCs or designs.

**A blockchain-network cryptocurrency.** An asset, credit, or unit fits the cited NIST definition when it can be cryptographically transferred between users of a blockchain network. The definition describes a technical category; it does not, by itself, establish the unit’s issuer, backing, legal treatment, or governance.

**An electronically recorded bank balance.** The source pack does not establish whether bank deposits or electronically recorded balances should be called digital currency. It also does not support claims about how different sources classify deposits. For clarity, identify the subject as a bank balance or deposit when that is what is meant, and consult the relevant source’s terminology before assigning a broader category label. An electronic record alone does not establish that the NIST cryptocurrency definition applies.

**A stablecoin.** The International Monetary Fund’s 2025 conceptual framework describes a stablecoin as aiming to maintain a stable value relative to a specified asset, currency, or basket through a stated stabilization mechanism. “Stable” refers to an objective: market value and redemption value can diverge from the reference. This description does not determine whether a particular stablecoin meets NIST’s definition of cryptocurrency. That classification depends on the relevant definition and the system’s documented design.

These examples should not be treated as mutually exclusive categories by default. A system may be described using one label in one framework and assessed against a different technical definition in another. State the definition being applied and avoid inferring more than its conditions establish.

## How to classify a digital money system

A practical classification starts with documented properties rather than a name. For technical and institutional review, use the following questions:

1. **What exactly is being described?** Identify whether the subject is a unit, a liability, a bank balance, a payment record, or a network-based asset. Do not substitute a broad label for a precise description.
2. **Who issues or backs the unit?** Look for explicit documentation. Neither “digital” nor “cryptocurrency,” by itself, establishes an issuer, backing, or an obligation owed to a holder.
3. **What record system is specified?** Check whether the relevant documentation identifies a blockchain network. An electronic interface or record is not, by itself, evidence that the record system is a blockchain.
4. **How is a transfer authorized?** For a system said to meet the cited NIST definition, check whether it supports cryptographic transfer between users of a blockchain network. NIST’s description of asymmetric-key digital signatures concerns authorization; it does not establish the user’s legal identity.
5. **What does the source say about validation and governance?** Do not infer a validation method, access policy, or governance arrangement from the word *cryptocurrency*. Seek documentation for each property separately; the supplied NIST definition does not specify them.
6. **Which definition is being applied?** Record whether a statement relies on NIST’s technical definition, the Federal Reserve’s CBDC description, or another explicitly identified framework. A conclusion should not silently move from one source’s scope to another’s.
7. **Which questions remain unresolved?** Legal status, convertibility, and monetary backing are not settled by a technology label. Treat them as separate questions requiring relevant evidence and context.

This method helps prevent category errors. A CBDC description identifies a central-bank liability under the Federal Reserve’s definition; NIST’s cryptocurrency definition identifies a blockchain-network and cryptographic-transfer condition. Neither description alone answers every question about a system. Where evidence is missing, the accurate result is to qualify the classification or leave it open.

## Frequently confused distinctions

- **A digital label does not establish a technical design.** The supplied sources do not support inferring blockchain use, a particular record system, or control arrangements from the phrase “digital currency.”
- **The NIST definition is narrower than “any digital asset.”** It specifies cryptographic transfer between users of a blockchain network and does not cover every legal category called a digital asset.
- **Cryptographic authorization does not establish legal identity.** A valid signature demonstrates control of the signing key under the protocol, not the controller’s legal identity or the wisdom of the transaction.
- **Authorization does not specify every transaction process.** NIST’s signature description does not prescribe a universal validation process or governance arrangement.
- **A CBDC description is not a universal issuance claim.** The Federal Reserve states that it has not decided to issue a U.S. CBDC; its general definition should not be read as evidence of issuance everywhere.
- **A stable-value objective is not a guaranteed value.** The IMF framework says market value and redemption value can diverge from the reference.

The most defensible distinction is therefore source-specific: NIST supplies a technical definition of cryptocurrency tied to cryptographic transfer between users of a blockchain network, while the supplied sources do not establish a general definition of digital currency or a universal category relationship between the two terms. To classify a system, state the definition being used and verify its documented issuer or liability, record system, transfer authorization, and other properties separately.

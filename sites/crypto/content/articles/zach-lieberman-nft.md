## How to verify an NFT attribution

Begin with an authenticated channel associated with the artist or an authorized representative. Look for a specific reference to the work or collection, not merely a name, image, or general statement about digital art. The reference should provide enough identifying detail to compare it with the listing or token record under review.

Next, compare available token identifiers and artwork details. Depending on the record, relevant identifiers may include the network, contract address, and token ID. For Ethereum, an NFT’s recorded ownership associates a unique token identifier with an Ethereum address; the contract address and token ID together identify the token on that chain. This helps distinguish a token from another token, but it does not establish the creator’s identity or prove that the artist authorized the token or image. [source](<https://ethereum.org/nft/>)

Then compare the token or collection record with the artist’s authenticated reference. Check whether the collection or contract and the described work correspond to what the artist or representative actually identified. A copied image, similar title, or matching name is not enough on its own to establish that connection. If details do not match, or the source cannot be authenticated, the attribution remains unresolved rather than confirmed.

Finally, keep the conclusion proportional to what was checked. A verified token identifier can establish which token record is being discussed. A reliable artist reference may support an attribution to a work. Neither should be silently substituted for the other, and the available evidence here does not provide the item-specific materials needed to make either finding about a particular Zach Lieberman NFT.

## Token, artwork, and rights are different things

The token is a blockchain record. The digital artwork or file associated with it is a separate object, and the rights governing that artwork are another matter. A token record can indicate an address associated with token ownership, but transfer of an NFT does not necessarily transfer rights in the underlying work. In the United States intellectual-property analysis cited here, rights depend on applicable law and valid contracts, licenses, or other governing terms. [source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>) [source](<https://ethereum.org/nft/>)

That distinction affects what a holder can accurately claim. A token transfer may change the address recorded as the token’s owner. It does not, by itself, establish permission to reproduce the artwork, distribute copies, use it commercially, or license it to someone else. Those permissions must be supported by terms or a license that applies to the specific item. This article does not determine the legal effect of any particular token or terms, and the cited rights discussion is scoped to the United States. [source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>)

NFT technology may support workflows for managing, transferring, or licensing intellectual-property rights when valid terms and authorized rights holders are present. But the token itself does not create those terms or prove that the person who minted it was entitled to grant them. For a specific work, rights should be described only when the applicable documentation is available and clearly connected to that work. [source](<https://www.copyright.gov/policy/nft-study/Joint-USPTO-USCO-Report-on-NFTs-and-Intellectual-Property.pdf>)

A royalty field, if encountered, also needs careful interpretation. ERC-2981 standardizes retrieval of a royalty recipient and amount for a stated sale price. The standard’s information is a royalty signal; payment remains voluntary and depends on marketplace or participant implementation. It does not, by itself, establish copyright ownership or a universal payment obligation. [source](<https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-2981>)

The phrase “Solactive crypto index” points to a crypto index associated with Solactive, but the phrase alone does not identify a particular index or establish its rules, inputs, or current status. Confirm the exact index name and consult documentation that applies to it. An index is a rule-based measure; it is not, by that description alone, a token, exchange, blockchain, or on-chain analytics platform.

## What the term Solactive crypto index identifies

Treat the phrase as a starting point for identifying a named index, not as proof that a particular index or related product currently exists or has specific features. Details for one named index should not be assumed to apply to another. The first step is to locate the exact official name and the documentation associated with it.

An index and an analytics platform answer different questions. An index represents a measure formed under defined rules. An analytics platform, by contrast, may provide ways to inspect or organize blockchain records. The name of an index does not establish that it is a blockchain, that it is a token, or that it supplies tools for exploring blockchain activity.

This distinction also matters when a separate product refers to an index. The index label alone does not describe that product’s terms, operation, or availability. Those facts require documentation about the specific product or service, rather than an inference from the index name.

## How to read an index methodology

A methodology is the documentation to consult for the rules that apply to a named index. Start with the exact index name and the version of its methodology. Look for what the document says about the index’s objective, eligible assets, selection criteria, weighting, calculation, and review or rebalancing procedures. These are items to verify; they should not be treated as universal features of every crypto index.

Read each rule as written and keep its limits intact. For example, an eligibility rule would describe what may qualify under the stated criteria; it would not by itself identify all assets included at a particular date. A weighting rule would describe how weights are determined, but a dated record would still be needed to establish actual weights at that time. The methodology explains the rule; a current or historical snapshot answers what resulted on a specified date.

That distinction applies to performance as well. A general description of construction rules does not establish performance for a period. Any claim about constituents, weights, or performance needs an appropriate dated record for the specific index. If the relevant information is not in the applicable official material, treat it as unknown rather than filling the gap with assumptions.

A methodology may also leave questions open or use terms that require definitions elsewhere. Check whether the official documentation defines its inputs and calculation steps clearly enough to understand how a measure is produced. If a rule, definition, or version is ambiguous, record that limitation. Do not borrow an explanation from another index simply because its name or subject appears similar.

## Index inputs are not the same as on-chain analytics

Index inputs are the data and other information a methodology says is used in constructing or calculating an index. On-chain analytics, in contrast, involves analyzing blockchain data: Ethereum documentation describes data analysis as potentially including access to, processing, transformation, organization, and querying of blockchain data. That general activity does not establish what inputs a particular index uses.

A useful boundary is to separate the data activity from the index rule. Blockchain records may be organized into queryable tables; one documented analytics schema separates blocks, transactions, receipts, logs, decoded events, token transfers, traces, and selected account-state records. That is an example of an analytics dataset structure, not evidence that a Solactive index uses any of those records or measures. ([source](<https://docs.cloud.google.com/blockchain-analytics/docs/schema>))

Do not infer an index input merely because the data could be found or analyzed on a blockchain. To say that on-chain information contributes to a named index, the index’s own applicable documentation must identify that information and explain its role. Without that evidence, whether the index uses on-chain inputs remains unverified.

This also keeps data preparation distinct from index calculation and publication. Collecting, structuring, or querying records describes work on data. Applying a documented index methodology describes a separate process. The two can be related in a particular case, but the relationship must be established by documentation rather than assumed from the words “crypto index.”

## What to verify before relying on a specific index

For a specific index, check the official materials for the exact identity and applicable methodology version. Then verify what they say about data definitions, eligible assets, selection, weighting, calculation, and review. If publication terms or calculation timing matter to the question, confirm those details in documentation that applies to that index and period.

Check whether a claim is about a rule or a dated outcome. A methodology is evidence of stated rules; it is not automatically evidence of current constituents, weights, or performance. For time-sensitive facts, look for official records tied to the relevant date. If no suitable record is available, state that the fact is not established by the material at hand.

Other claims need evidence specific to the product or service involved. An index description alone does not establish availability, fees, licensing, security, recovery, or compliance. Regulatory or tax statements require authoritative evidence applicable to the jurisdiction being discussed. Do not generalize from one market, product structure, or jurisdiction to another.

Where a separate financial product refers to a tokenized security, structure can matter: in the United States federal securities-law context, a third-party tokenized security may represent a custodial entitlement or synthetic instrument rather than direct ownership of the referenced security, and rights and bankruptcy exposure depend on the structure and governing documents. This is a limited example, not a statement about Solactive or any particular index-linked product. It reinforces why an index name cannot establish a separate product’s legal or operational details. ([source](<https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/corp-fin-statement-tokenized-securities-012826-statement-tokenized-securities>))

## Limits of an index explanation

A general explanation of the term does not establish that a particular index-linked product exists, is available to a reader, or suits any purpose. It does not establish current constituents, weights, performance, or future outcomes. Those are separate questions, and time-sensitive details call for dated, primary documentation relevant to the named index.

The practical approach is to identify the exact index, read the methodology that applies to it, and verify its documented inputs separately from general on-chain analytics. When a detail is missing or unclear, label it unresolved. Do not treat an index label, an analytics dataset, or an example from another product as proof of facts that the applicable documentation does not state.

## How blockchain supports food traceability

A shared ledger can give multiple participants a common record of submitted information. In a blockchain system, published records are designed not to be simply changed under normal operation. That property can help preserve a history of what was recorded, but it does not by itself establish who supplied the information, whether it was checked, or whether it was accurate.

For a food traceability platform, the distinction is important: blockchain concerns the handling of recorded data, while the quality of a trace depends on the information entered and made available. The evidence here does not establish IBM Food Trust’s specific network, participant permissions, consensus mechanism, data storage arrangements, or verification process. It would be unsupported to describe those implementation details as facts about the platform.

Nor does the available evidence establish that every record was independently validated, or that a particular product’s complete history was present. A shared record can preserve submitted information without proving that the physical product matched the record or that every relevant event was captured.

## What a traceability record can—and cannot—show

When relevant records are available, a traceability system may help users follow reported movements or events associated with a product. The record can show what information the system contains; interpreting it requires understanding who entered that information, what checks were applied, and whether the record covers the events a reader wants to assess. The available evidence does not document IBM Food Trust’s answers to those platform-specific questions.

A useful distinction is between **record integrity** and **real-world truth**. Record integrity concerns whether stored entries were altered. Real-world truth concerns whether an entry accurately describes an origin, movement, handling event, or other fact. A tamper-resistant ledger can support the first without guaranteeing the second. The ledger alone also cannot establish completeness: missing or unsubmitted information is not made visible merely because the records that do exist are difficult to change.

Accordingly, a traceability record should not be treated on its own as proof of food safety, product origin, regulatory compliance, or an unbroken chain of custody. Those conclusions require suitable evidence beyond the existence of a blockchain record. This is a limitation of what the evidence supports, not a claim that any particular IBM Food Trust record was inaccurate or incomplete.

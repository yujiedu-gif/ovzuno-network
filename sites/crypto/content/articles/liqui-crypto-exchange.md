## What records can establish about its exchange operations

The evidence allowed for this article does not document Liqui-specific trading pairs, order handling, deposits, withdrawals, or operating dates. Those details should not be inferred from general descriptions of centralized exchanges.

A historical claim should rest on material that is dated, attributable to the platform or its operator, and specific to the activity described. A platform page or archived interface can show what the page represented at that time. By itself, however, it does not independently establish that trades were executed or that deposits or withdrawals were successfully processed. A market listing should likewise be tied to a date and a clearly identified platform before it supports a statement about historical markets.

Keep each conclusion no broader than the underlying record. A document describing a deposit feature would support a claim that the feature was described, not necessarily that a particular deposit succeeded, users had access throughout a given period, or all platform services were functioning. A record of a trading pair would not establish how orders were handled or whether the pair remained available later.

General exchange descriptions are useful only as context, not as Liqui-specific proof. For example, one general model has customers transferring assets into an exchange’s custody before trading through its internal system. [source](<https://www.bis.org/publ/work1227.pdf>) That model does not show that Liqui used the same arrangement. Without records attributable to Liqui, do not assign it a custody or trading design based on how another exchange may operate.

The same restraint applies to dates. A dated page may establish that a description existed by that date, but it does not necessarily establish when a service began, how long it operated, or when it stopped. Those claims need records that address those particular events and identify the relevant operator and service.

## Liqui crypto exchange status: verify before relying on claims

The evidence here does not establish whether Liqui is currently operating, accepting users, or providing any service. A current-status claim needs current, authoritative support. A historical page, an old listing, or a surviving website cannot answer the present-status question by itself.

When reviewing a status statement, check its date, provenance, scope, and identity. Ask when it was issued or updated and whether it addresses the present rather than an earlier period. Identify who published it and whether the source is attributable to the entity in question or is merely a repost. Then check what it actually confirms: a named service, a particular function, or only the Liqui name. Finally, determine whether the source clearly links to the company, domain, or platform under review.

Treat search snippets and third-party posts as leads, not confirmation. A page remaining accessible does not by itself establish that an exchange is operating or that services can be used. Nor should a statement about one function be expanded into a claim that every service is active. If a source has no clear date, operator, or scope, its usefulness for establishing current status is limited.

Look for current confirmation from authoritative company or domain records and, where relevant, applicable official records. The appropriate records depend on the claim being checked; a company identity record, for instance, does not automatically establish that a trading service is currently available. If no current authoritative confirmation supports the specific claim, describe the status as unverified. That conclusion does not prove either that a service is active or that it is inactive.

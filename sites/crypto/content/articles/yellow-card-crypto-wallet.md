## What a crypto wallet does—and what it does not do

A wallet is better understood as a way to manage access than as a container that necessarily holds crypto assets. In general, the wallet manages keys and addresses used in relation to assets recorded on a blockchain. Exactly how this works depends on the wallet implementation and the underlying network. The general definition should not be taken as a full technical description of every network or wallet design. ([source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/wallet>))

A key distinction is custody. In a self-custody arrangement, the user controls the private keys and recovery material needed to access the user’s crypto assets, and is responsible for that material. That describes a general self-custody model only; it does not establish that Yellow Card uses self-custody or that a particular wallet feature follows that model. ([source](<https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/crypto-asset-custody-basics-retail-investors-investor-bulletin-0>))

A wallet interface does not, by itself, establish who controls the keys. It also does not prove that assets are protected in a particular way, that a transaction can be reversed, or that access can always be restored. These questions depend on the product’s actual arrangement and documented terms. A user should not infer a recovery promise from the word “wallet,” or assume that a process covers lost credentials, mistaken transfers, or other specific problems unless the terms say so.

This separation matters when interpreting product language. A provider may describe an interface or account as a wallet, but readers still need to identify the custody model and the responsibilities attached to it. The general fact that wallets manage keys does not answer who holds those keys in a particular service or what recourse exists if access is lost.

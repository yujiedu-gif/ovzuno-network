Publicly available evidence in the claim set for this explainer does **not** establish specific crypto-related capabilities in BlackRock’s Aladdin platform. It also does not confirm that Aladdin integrates with a BlackRock digital-asset product, custodian, trading venue, or other provider. Institutions should treat those points as unverified here—not as proof that no such capabilities or relationships exist—and seek current, direct documentation before relying on them.

## What the Aladdin BlackRock crypto question covers

The question is whether reliable documentation establishes a particular crypto workflow in Aladdin and, if so, which named platform or provider performs it. “BlackRock,” “Aladdin,” and “crypto” appearing together do not establish that Aladdin itself offers custody, execution, settlement, asset issuance, or access to a particular product. Those are separate capabilities that require evidence tied to the platform, product, or provider in question.

A useful evidence standard separates three categories:

- **Confirmed:** current primary documentation names the relevant product or service, describes the function, and identifies its scope.
- **Qualified:** documentation describes a capability but limits it by asset, workflow, client, jurisdiction, or another condition.
- **Unverified:** the available evidence does not establish the feature, integration, or operating terms. This does not establish that the capability is absent.

For this explainer, the supplied allowed evidence does not include current official Aladdin documentation, BlackRock product documentation, or primary evidence confirming an Aladdin integration with a crypto-related provider. As a result, no specific Aladdin crypto function can be stated as confirmed on this evidence alone. That is an evidence limitation, not a finding about the contents of every current or historical product offering.

The distinction is especially important in institutional review. A capability should be attributed to the exact product or service named in its documentation, rather than to a corporate family or brand by association. If a document describes a feature but does not say whether Aladdin, another BlackRock product, or an outside provider performs it, the responsible party remains unresolved.

## What current documentation says about crypto-related capabilities

The allowed evidence does not document a current Aladdin capability, publication date, supported asset, workflow, or access condition. It would therefore be inaccurate to present portfolio tracking, risk analysis, trading, custody, settlement, or another function as an established Aladdin crypto feature on this evidence.

General crypto documentation cannot fill that gap. For example, ERC-721 standardizes ownership lookup, approvals, operator authorization, transfer behavior, and transfer events for distinct tokens. That describes functions of a token-contract interface; it does not show that Aladdin supports ERC-721 assets or processes those functions. A standard’s technical scope and a platform’s supported functionality are different questions.

The same caution applies to wallet terminology. A crypto wallet generally stores private keys or passcodes used to access and authorize crypto assets rather than storing the assets themselves. That distinction can help clarify what a wallet does, but it does not establish that Aladdin supplies a wallet, holds keys, or provides custody. Those claims require direct platform and service documentation.

Likewise, documentation about a particular privacy mechanism does not prove support in an investment-management platform. Zcash viewing keys, for example, provide protocol-defined selective visibility into shielded transaction data without spending authority. That fact describes a feature of the Zcash protocol; it does not establish that Aladdin reads such data, supports Zcash, or offers any related workflow.

When examining a product statement, institutions can record the exact product and module name, the function described, the publication or update date, and all stated limits. The review should also note what the document does not say. If it does not identify supported assets or distinguish platform activity from a provider’s activity, those points should remain questions for verification rather than being inferred.

## Separate platform features from products and third-party services

Investment-management software, an investment product, and an operational service are distinct categories. A platform’s association with an asset manager does not by itself establish that the platform issues a product, holds assets, routes or executes transactions, or settles them. Nor does the existence of a product establish that the product is natively available through a particular software platform.

The same separation applies to counterparties. A reference to a custodian, trading venue, issuer, or other service provider is not enough to establish a technical integration or define its scope. Direct documentation would need to identify the parties and describe the functions or information exchanged, along with relevant boundaries and conditions. The evidence available here does not establish such a relationship for Aladdin, so this article does not attribute custody, execution, settlement, or product access to the platform.

There are also different types of claims about assets themselves. A third-party tokenized security, for example, may represent a custodial entitlement or synthetic instrument rather than direct ownership of a referenced security. That distinction concerns the possible structure of a tokenized-security arrangement; it does not establish that Aladdin supports such instruments, that a particular BlackRock product has that structure, or that an integration exists. Product, ownership, and platform questions require evidence specific to the arrangement under review.

These boundaries help avoid a common category error: treating “related to crypto” as equivalent to “functionality inside Aladdin.” A product may be associated with an organization, while a platform may or may not support workflows involving that product. A service provider may perform custody or another operational role without that role being a platform capability. Each link in the chain needs its own documentation.

## Questions institutions should verify before relying on a capability

Before treating a crypto-related function as available through Aladdin, ask the relevant provider for current written documentation addressing the following:

1. **Product and function:** Which named Aladdin product, module, or service provides the capability? Is it analysis, recordkeeping, order routing, execution, custody, settlement, or something else? Avoid treating these functions as interchangeable.
2. **Scope and access:** Which assets, workflows, client types, jurisdictions, and permissions are covered? What conditions or restrictions apply? The supplied evidence does not answer these questions for Aladdin.
3. **Providers and responsibilities:** Which organizations perform each operational role? What does the platform do, and what remains the responsibility of a custodian, venue, issuer, or institution? Confirm any claimed relationship directly rather than inferring it from shared branding or a product reference.
4. **Security and operations:** What documentation describes data handling, access controls, recovery procedures, and operational dependencies? Do not infer these properties from a platform name or an announcement that does not specify the relevant controls and responsibilities.
5. **Commercial and compliance terms:** Confirm availability, fees, service levels, eligibility, and onboarding or compliance requirements with the relevant provider. Assess regulatory and tax questions with qualified advisers; the evidence here does not establish jurisdiction-specific conclusions.
6. **Evidence currency:** What is the document’s publication or update date, and does it refer to the exact product version and use case under consideration? If the source is undated or does not identify its scope, request clarification before treating it as confirmation.

These are verification questions, not claims that any particular capability is offered. Answers should be tied to the specific product, provider, and workflow being evaluated. If written materials identify only some parts of a process, institutions should keep the unaddressed roles and terms open rather than extending a narrow statement into a general conclusion.

## Evidence limits and a safe conclusion

The evidence available for this explainer supports a narrow conclusion: it does not establish specific crypto-related functionality in BlackRock’s Aladdin platform or confirm a relationship between Aladdin and a crypto product or third-party service. It also does not establish that such functionality or relationships are unavailable. “Not established by the evidence here” and “does not exist” are different conclusions.

A responsible assessment therefore depends on current official documentation that identifies the capability, its owner or operator, its scope, and its terms. Institutions can then evaluate any provider responsibilities and operational dependencies separately. Where documentation is silent, request direct clarification; do not fill gaps by combining general crypto facts, corporate association, or product references.

This explainer does not assess investment suitability, fees, availability, performance, regulatory status, tax treatment, or security guarantees for Aladdin or any BlackRock product. Those matters require current, context-specific evidence and review by the relevant qualified advisers. No conclusion about universal access or institutional suitability follows from the evidence presented here.

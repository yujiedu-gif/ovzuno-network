“NFT series” is a context-dependent label for a group of related non-fungible tokens. The relationship might be defined by a project, theme, release, or collection, but the phrase alone does not specify how the tokens are organized onchain, what metadata or files they share, or what rights an owner receives.

## What does “NFT series” mean?

In project materials or marketplace descriptions, an NFT series usually means a set of NFTs presented as related. The connection might be a shared creative theme, a project identity, a release, or a collection defined by the project. The phrase is descriptive: by itself, it does not name a single technical standard or guarantee that different platforms use the same definition.

That makes context important. A project may define which tokens belong to a series in its own documentation, while a marketplace may display a grouping using its own interface or indexing. To understand the label, look for the project’s definition and the records it identifies, rather than treating the word “series” as a complete technical description.

## How a series relates to individual NFTs

An NFT is an individual token record. A series can group multiple such records in a project’s description or through a specific implementation. The group label does not, on its own, tell you whether the tokens use one contract, multiple contracts, or a shared collection identifier. Those details require project documentation and relevant token records.

Token standards also should not be confused with the series label. For example, ERC-1155 allows one contract to manage multiple token types, including fungible, non-fungible, and semi-fungible configurations, and supports single and batch transfers. A token ID under that standard can have a quantity greater than one, so whether it functions as non-fungible depends on the implementation and supply. The standard describes a way to organize and transfer tokens; it does not establish what a project means by “series.”

When a project says a token is part of a series, check how it identifies the included tokens. Compare the project’s stated membership with the token identifiers and records it points to. A displayed group name can help readers navigate, but it should not be taken as proof of shared onchain structure unless the documentation and records support that interpretation.

## Shared artwork, metadata, and rights are separate questions

Tokens in a series may be presented around the same theme without sharing identical artwork, metadata, or media files. A project’s documentation should explain what, if anything, is common and what varies between individual tokens. Check the specific token’s information and the project’s explanation instead of inferring uniformity from the series name.

Metadata and media links also need their own review. A project may describe how token information relates to files or other content, but the series label does not establish that those details are identical across tokens. Read the project’s documentation for its stated metadata and media arrangements, and inspect the relevant records where possible. Do not treat the presence of a link or a shared description as proof of permanence, accuracy, or availability.

Rights are distinct from token grouping and ownership. The SEC staff describes third-party tokenized securities as potentially providing an indirect custodial entitlement, a contractual claim, or synthetic exposure, with rights that differ from direct ownership of the referenced security. This is a reminder that a token’s recorded holder and the rights connected to an underlying subject are separate questions; it is not a determination about any particular NFT series.

For an NFT, read the project’s terms to see whether they describe a license, access permission, utility, or other claimed right, and who grants it. The series label alone does not establish copyright, a license, access rights, or legal permissions. Do not infer those rights solely from the token record or the fact that several tokens belong to a named group.

## What to check when a project calls NFTs a series

Use the project’s own documentation and inspectable records to answer these questions:

- **Definition and membership:** How does the project define “series,” and which specific tokens does it include?
- **Token identification:** What identifiers or records does the project use? Do the identified tokens match its stated grouping?
- **Structure:** Does the project explain whether the grouping is descriptive or connected to a shared contract, collection identifier, or other onchain organization?
- **Metadata and media:** What does the project say is shared or different between tokens, and where do the relevant records point?
- **Terms and claimed rights:** What do the applicable terms say about ownership, licenses, access, or utility, and who is responsible for granting those rights?

Keep project statements distinct from what the records show. If documentation does not explain a detail, the term “series” is not enough to fill in the gap.

## What the phrase does not tell you

“Series” alone does not establish authenticity, scarcity, permanence, interoperability, or value. It does not guarantee that grouped tokens share a contract, metadata, media, or rights. Each of those properties needs separate evidence tied to the specific project and token.

The useful reading is narrow: an NFT series is a project- or context-defined grouping of related tokens. To understand what that grouping means in practice, verify its membership, technical organization, metadata, and stated rights separately.

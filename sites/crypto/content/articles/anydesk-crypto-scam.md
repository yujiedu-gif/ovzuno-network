If someone claiming to help with cryptocurrency asks you to install AnyDesk, share your screen, or grant remote access, treat the request as a warning sign—not proof by itself that the person or transaction is fraudulent. Do not reveal passwords, recovery phrases, or verification codes, or approve wallet activity because the caller instructs you to. If you suspect an active unwanted session, end it, disconnect the affected device from the network, then use a separate trusted device to secure relevant accounts and contact providers through independently verified channels.

## Why AnyDesk access can be a warning sign in a crypto interaction

Remote access means another person may be able to interact with a device or see information displayed during a session. In a cryptocurrency interaction, that can expose information related to wallet or exchange activity. A request to install remote-access software while discussing a wallet, account security, a transfer, or supposed recovery therefore deserves caution.

The request alone does not establish that a scam has occurred. The facts available to you may not show who initiated the session, what the other person could see or do, or whether a transaction was authorized. Assess the surrounding behavior rather than treating the software name as a verdict. If you cannot determine what access was granted, respond cautiously and verify the situation through channels you locate independently.

A payment path can involve more than one party. For example, U.S. guidance describes kiosk operators that may fulfill a transaction by connecting to a separate convertible virtual currency exchanger or by drawing on virtual currency held by the operator while maintaining sufficient virtual currency and cash balances. This is an example of an operating model, not a universal description of kiosks or evidence that a particular transaction is fraudulent. It also illustrates why a caller’s description of where funds are going should not substitute for checking the destination and the instructions independently. ([source](<https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2025-08/FinCEN-Notice-CVCKIOSK.pdf>))

## Common warning signs to assess before granting access

Pause if the person pressures you to act immediately, insists that you keep the conversation secret, or tells you to bypass ordinary account-security checks. These behaviors are reasons to stop and verify independently, not conclusive proof of identity or intent. A caller’s urgency does not establish that the requested access is necessary.

Be especially cautious if the person asks you to reveal a password, wallet recovery phrase, or verification code, or asks you to approve an unfamiliar wallet action. Do not provide credentials or authorize activity simply because someone says it is needed to protect an account, fix a technical issue, or retrieve funds. If you have already shared information or approved something, treat it as a possible exposure and move to containment rather than continuing to follow the caller’s directions.

Consider how the contact began and what the person is promising. Unexpected contact framed as technical support, account protection, investment assistance, or recovery help should be checked through a channel you find independently—for example, a provider’s official app or contact details obtained separately from the conversation. A link, phone number, or screen shared by the person requesting access is not independent verification.

Keep a distinction between a warning sign and a verified fact. A request for remote access may justify refusing it, ending a session, and checking relevant accounts. It does not, by itself, establish that a named person, business, platform, or transaction is fraudulent. Record what you know directly—such as what was requested, what you agreed to, and whether a transaction appears in an account—separately from what the other person claimed.

## If an AnyDesk session is active or already happened

Prioritize containment over confrontation. If you believe someone is actively controlling your device, end the session and disconnect that device from the network. If you are unsure how to do that safely, consult official guidance from the device’s operating-system or security provider using a separate trusted device. Do not rely on instructions from the person whose access you are trying to end.

Next, use a separate trusted device to review and secure relevant accounts. Contact the wallet, exchange, or other affected provider through independently verified channels and explain that remote access may have occurred. Ask what account-specific protective steps are available. Avoid assuming that a provider can reverse a transaction or restore assets; the facts here do not establish that either outcome is possible in an individual case.

If the session has ended but you do not know what was visible or changed, do not treat the end of the session as proof that every account or device is secure. Use official device-security guidance to decide how to check the affected device. Where an account might be affected, contact its provider from a separate trusted device. Keep the two tasks distinct: device checks address possible access to the device, while provider contact addresses account-specific questions.

Preserve potentially useful records before deleting messages or resetting devices, when doing so does not prolong active exposure. Save the conversation, contact details, account alerts, transaction records, and relevant dates. Keep copies somewhere the affected device’s session cannot alter, if possible. Do not edit records in a way that obscures what happened. If the device may still be exposed, containment comes first; evidence collection should not extend an active session or require you to continue interacting with the caller.

Avoid sending more money or granting additional access while you investigate. In particular, do not pay an unsolicited person who promises to recover cryptocurrency or demands another transfer to unlock, trace, or return funds. The available evidence does not support a promise that funds can be recovered, that reporting will produce reimbursement, or that a particular service can reverse a transaction. A report may document what you experienced, but it does not guarantee recovery or reimbursement.

## Reporting, regulation, and tax questions depend on jurisdiction

The appropriate reporting route depends on where you are and what happened. Identify your country or region, then check current instructions from official government, law-enforcement, financial-regulator, and affected-platform sources as applicable. Verify that any reporting channel is genuinely official before sharing personal or transaction information. The evidence available for this article does not establish a current reporting route for a particular jurisdiction, so no agency or procedure is named here.

Some regulatory descriptions apply only to defined activities in a defined place. For example, FinCEN describes certain U.S. convertible virtual currency kiosk operators that facilitate transfers between an exchanger and a customer wallet, or operate as exchangers, as generally money services businesses under the U.S. Bank Secrecy Act, subject to applicable registration, recordkeeping, reporting, and transaction-monitoring obligations. This is a U.S.-specific description of those kiosk operators; it is not a conclusion about every remote-access service, crypto business, or person involved in a suspected scam. ([source](<https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2025-08/FinCEN-Notice-CVCKIOSK.pdf>))

Keep legal, regulatory, and tax questions separate from immediate security steps. Do not assume that reporting duties, tax treatment, fees, or legal outcomes are universal. Check the relevant official sources for your location before relying on jurisdiction-specific instructions. Reporting can create a record of an incident, but it does not establish that a transaction will be reversed or that funds will be returned.

The practical sequence is to stop granting access, contain any active exposure, secure relevant accounts from a separate trusted device, preserve records without delaying containment, and verify reporting options through official sources for your location. A request involving AnyDesk is a reason to pause and examine the circumstances; it is not, on its own, proof of fraud or a promise that any particular response will recover funds.

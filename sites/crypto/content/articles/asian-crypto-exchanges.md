For business readers assessing crypto exchange infrastructure in Asian markets, treat “Asian” as a regional label—not evidence that exchanges share one market, legal status, customer base, or service model. Compare each operator, the specific jurisdiction and service, and the evidence supporting each claim. The available evidence supports only a limited description of custody arrangements and one execution factor; it does not establish current licensing, customer eligibility, fees, product availability, or security practices for any Asian exchange.

## What “Asian crypto exchanges” means as a regional category

The phrase can refer to exchanges based in an Asian jurisdiction, exchanges serving customers there, or exchanges authorized to conduct particular activities there. These are different attributes. An operator’s location does not, by itself, establish which customers it serves, which products it makes available in a market, or whether it is authorized to perform a particular activity there.

A useful comparison begins by naming the legal entity and the exact market and service being assessed. If a company has multiple entities or provides more than one service, a claim about one entity or service should not be transferred to another without evidence. The available claims do not identify any specific Asian exchange, its location, or its legal authorization. Those details therefore remain questions to verify, not facts that can be inferred from a regional label.

The same discipline applies to regional descriptions of customer markets. A provider’s stated reach does not establish that every product is available to every customer in each named market. A regional comparison should identify the relevant product and customer group, and should mark availability as unverified when current, specific evidence is missing.

## How exchange infrastructure connects users, markets, and assets

For comparison purposes, separate trading functions from the arrangements for holding assets and recording customer activity. A common centralized-exchange model has customers transfer funds and custody to the exchange before trading on its internal order book. This is a general model, not a universal description: custody and legal ownership arrangements vary by platform and jurisdiction, and some venues use different custody or settlement arrangements.

An internal order book is a venue’s record of customer orders and executed trades. It is useful to distinguish that internal record from the custody arrangement: the fact that trades are recorded within an exchange system does not, by itself, specify who holds assets, how a customer’s rights are structured, or how settlement occurs. These details need evidence tied to the particular operator, product, and applicable terms.

That distinction matters when comparing exchanges that serve customers across multiple jurisdictions. A trading interface may connect a customer to a market-facing platform, while the arrangements for custody and settlement depend on the operator and service. The available evidence does not establish the local-currency deposit and withdrawal methods, payment rails, supported assets, or settlement interfaces of any particular Asian exchange. Each is a separate market- and provider-specific question.

A practical infrastructure map can therefore record four things separately: the customer-facing access route; the trading system and how it records orders or executions; the custody arrangement; and the settlement path. This map is a comparison framework, not a claim that every exchange has the same architecture. It prevents a documented feature in one part of a service from being mistaken for evidence about another.

## Compare market structure without ranking providers

A neutral comparison can track supported products, trading pairs, customer eligibility, and operating arrangements when current documentation identifies the entity, service, market, and relevant customer group. A product listed for one market does not establish availability elsewhere. Likewise, a general statement about an operator does not necessarily describe every product it offers.

Liquidity and reported trading volume require particular care. “Liquidity” is not a single measure that can be compared without defining the data and method. For a specific public-blockchain automated market-maker swap, public transaction visibility, execution ordering, pool depth, and price impact can affect execution before block inclusion. That evidence describes a particular on-chain execution setting; it does not establish how centralized exchanges in Asian markets execute orders or whether their reported activity is comparable.

A cross-venue comparison should therefore state what its figures measure, which venues and products are included, and the relevant measurement period. The available evidence provides no comparable, methodology-disclosed data for Asian exchange trading activity or liquidity. Without such evidence, it cannot support a ranking or a conclusion about relative market depth. Report the comparison as unavailable or non-comparable rather than treating unlike figures as equivalent.

Do not infer service quality, security, or customer suitability from an operator’s location, apparent size, or regional reputation. Those characteristics need their own evidence. A comparison can remain useful while acknowledging unknowns: document what is supported, name its scope and date, and leave unsupported points unresolved.

## Jurisdiction-specific rules and operating claims

Licensing, registration, permitted activities, customer eligibility, and compliance obligations should be verified separately for each relevant jurisdiction, entity, and service. The allowed evidence does not include current primary regulatory materials for Asian jurisdictions. It therefore cannot establish whether a particular exchange or activity is licensed, registered, or permitted in any such jurisdiction. A regional comparison must not turn the absence of evidence into a conclusion about legal status.

One limited U.S. example illustrates why jurisdiction and activity matter: under the described U.S. federal Bank Secrecy Act treatment, certain convertible virtual currency kiosk operators that facilitate transmission between an exchanger and a customer wallet, or operate as an exchanger, are generally considered money services businesses. This statement concerns specified U.S. kiosk activities. It is not a rule for Asian exchanges and does not determine the status of an operator or service in another jurisdiction.

For an Asian-market assessment, use current primary materials from the relevant jurisdiction to support any regulatory claim, and tie the claim to the named entity and activity. If the materials are unavailable or do not address the exact service, describe the point as unverified. Company location, branding, or claimed regional presence cannot fill that evidentiary gap.

## Evidence checklist for evaluating an exchange infrastructure claim

For each claim, record the following before using it in a comparison:

- **Date and scope:** When was the evidence published or checked? Which product, entity, market, and customer group does it cover?
- **Entity and authorization:** Which legal entity operates the service? What current primary material supports any claim about authorization or permitted activities in the relevant jurisdiction?
- **Availability:** Does operator documentation identify the relevant market, product, asset, and customer eligibility? Check each market separately rather than inferring regional availability.
- **Trading, custody, and settlement:** What do the applicable terms and technical materials say about order handling, asset custody, and settlement? A common centralized-exchange model is not proof of an individual provider’s arrangement.
- **Fees, yields, security, and recovery:** Require current, specific terms for fee or yield claims, and operational documentation for security controls, incident handling, and account or asset recovery. The evidence available here does not establish these practices for any Asian operator.
- **Volume and liquidity comparisons:** Identify the source, measurement period, included products, and methodology. If evidence is missing or figures are not comparable, disclose that limitation instead of ranking providers.

The comparison should preserve these distinctions from start to finish. Shared technical functions can provide a vocabulary for describing infrastructure, but they do not establish an operator’s market access, legal status, custody terms, or security arrangements. Those claims require evidence specific to the jurisdiction, entity, service, and date. Where suitable evidence is absent, the responsible conclusion is that the claim remains unverified.

Crypto staking means committing cryptocurrency as stake under a proof-of-stake network’s rules, where stake can influence participation or weight in consensus. It is related to validator activity, but staking does not necessarily mean that the person staking operates a validator. The exact arrangement depends on the network and its protocol rules.

## What crypto staking means

In proof-of-stake systems, cryptocurrency is committed as stake, and that stake can influence a participant’s role or weight in consensus. The protocol determines how the commitment works and how it relates to participation; the term does not describe one identical mechanism used by every network.

For example, Ethereum uses ETH deposits as slashable economic collateral for validator participation. That is a network-specific design, not a universal definition of staking. A general definition should therefore focus on committing assets under a protocol’s proof-of-stake rules, rather than assume a particular deposit format or set of conditions.

The word “staking” also does not, by itself, establish who controls the assets or who performs network duties. Those details depend on the protocol and the particular arrangement. Keep the core meaning separate from the way a service, wallet, or other participant may be involved.

## How staking relates to validators

A validator is a participant assigned consensus duties by a proof-of-stake protocol. On Ethereum, validators check and attest to blocks and propose a block when selected. Stake is the economic collateral used for validator participation in that network, but the stake commitment and the act of running validator infrastructure are distinct concepts.

Delegation, where supported, is another distinct arrangement: a participant can assign stake to a validator rather than personally perform the validator role. Solana provides a protocol-specific example in which a delegator can assign stake to a validator, increasing that validator’s consensus weight while the delegator retains token control under the stake-account model. This example should not be treated as a rule for other networks.

The practical distinction is straightforward: staking describes committing or assigning assets under protocol rules; validator operation describes taking on the protocol’s validator role and duties. Some network designs connect these activities directly, while others allow stake to be delegated. Whether delegation exists, what it changes, and what control remains are protocol-specific questions.

## Staking is not the same as mining

Mining is associated with proof of work, where participants expend computational effort on a problem that is costly to solve but straightforward for other nodes to verify. Staking is associated with proof of stake, where committed cryptocurrency influences consensus participation or weight. These are different ways networks organize participation; the distinction does not require treating every network’s implementation as identical.

This high-level contrast is enough to define the terms. It does not explain every consensus rule or establish that one approach eliminates operational or security risks. For example, Ethereum’s stake is slashable collateral, and its protocol specifies rewards for correct participation, missed rewards or penalties for inactivity, and slashing for certain conflicting messages. Those Ethereum-specific conditions should not be generalized to all proof-of-stake networks.

## What the definition does not promise

The word “staking” alone does not promise a fixed yield, payout, or asset value. Nor does it establish the conditions for locking or withdrawing assets, the fees involved, or how custody and recovery work. Those questions require evidence about the particular protocol and arrangement; they cannot be inferred from the definition.

Likewise, a general definition is not a security assessment or a statement about service availability or compliance. Network rules, validator duties, and delegation mechanisms are protocol-specific. Additional arrangements may have their own conditions, but no universal terms follow from the word “staking.”

For a concise reading, ask two separate questions: what does the protocol mean by committing or assigning stake, and who actually performs the validator duties? Keeping those questions distinct clarifies how staking can support validator participation without equating every staker with a validator operator.

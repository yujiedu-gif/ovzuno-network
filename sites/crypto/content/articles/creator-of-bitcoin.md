## Who is credited as the creator of Bitcoin?

Bitcoin’s white paper is authored under the name Satoshi Nakamoto. The document describes Bitcoin as a peer-to-peer electronic cash system. On that basis, Satoshi Nakamoto is the name credited with Bitcoin’s creation. The attribution is meaningful, but it should be stated precisely: it connects the name with the document and its subject, rather than independently identifying the person or people who used the name. [source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>) [source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>)

A pseudonym is a name used in place of a person’s ordinary identity. Here, the name in the authorship attribution answers one question—what name is associated with the white paper—but not another: who, in real-world terms, was behind it? The available evidence in this article supports the attribution, not a conclusion about a particular person’s identity.

This distinction also guards against turning a short answer into an unsupported biography. Saying “Satoshi Nakamoto created Bitcoin” is common shorthand for the attribution. A more exact formulation is that Bitcoin’s creation is attributed to the name Satoshi Nakamoto. The latter leaves open whether the name represented one person or a group, because the evidence presented here does not resolve that question.

## What evidence connects Satoshi Nakamoto to Bitcoin’s creation?

The evidence considered here is the Bitcoin white paper and its authorship attribution. The document is attributed to the name Satoshi Nakamoto, and its title describes Bitcoin as a peer-to-peer electronic cash system. Those facts establish a public association between that name and Bitcoin’s introduction. They do not, by themselves, verify who authored the document as a matter of civil identity. [source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>) [source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>)

It helps to separate two kinds of evidence. **Attribution evidence** connects a work or project to a name. **Identity evidence** connects that name to a specific, verifiable individual or group. The white paper attribution is evidence of the first kind. The evidence available for this article does not provide the second kind, so it cannot support naming a specific person as the confirmed creator.

That boundary matters when readers encounter statements that go further than the document’s attribution. A reported association, a proposed identification, or a person’s own claim would need corroboration before it could establish a verified identity. This article does not assess particular identity claims or supply corroborating records for them. It therefore treats the public attribution as the supported conclusion and does not extend it into a claim about who used the name.

The scope is deliberately narrow. The evidence used here supports describing Bitcoin as a peer-to-peer electronic cash system and identifying the name under which its white paper is authored. It does not provide a broader record of project activity or establish an identity beyond that attribution. [source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>) [source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>)

## Why is the creator’s identity still uncertain?

A pseudonym can be associated with a project without publicly establishing the identity of its user. In this case, the available evidence does not establish a verified civil identity for the person or group behind the name Satoshi Nakamoto. That is a limit on what can be concluded from the evidence presented, not proof that no identity is known to anyone or that no further evidence exists elsewhere. [source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>)

The distinction is especially important when a claim is repeated confidently. Repetition does not turn an attribution into identity verification. Nor does a name alone determine whether it belongs to one person or multiple people. Without evidence that connects the pseudonym to a verifiable identity, a proposed identification remains a claim rather than an established answer.

The same standard applies to self-identification. A person saying they are Satoshi Nakamoto would be a claim about identity; it would not, on its own, supply the independent corroboration needed to treat the identity as verified. The evidence considered here neither evaluates any such claim nor endorses a proposed identification. Its conclusion remains limited to the name in the white paper’s attribution and the documented absence of a verified civil identity in the evidence used. [source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>) [source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>)

This careful wording avoids two opposite overstatements. It would be too strong to say the attribution proves the identity of a particular person. It would also be too strong, based only on this evidence, to make a sweeping claim about every record or every possible source of information. The defensible statement is narrower: the evidence here names Satoshi Nakamoto as the credited author, while not verifying the real-world identity behind that name.

## What can readers conclude—and what remains unknown?

Readers can conclude that Bitcoin’s creation is attributed to Satoshi Nakamoto, the name used in the white paper’s authorship attribution. The white paper describes Bitcoin as a peer-to-peer electronic cash system. These points establish the public attribution, not a confirmed personal identity. [source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>) [source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>)

What remains unverified in the evidence presented is the real-world identity of the person or group behind the name. The attribution does not establish that the name belongs to a particular individual, and the evidence here does not resolve whether it refers to one person or several. Claims that identify a specific creator should therefore be described as claims unless supported by evidence that verifies the identity, rather than merely repeating the pseudonym. [source](<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>)

In short, **Satoshi Nakamoto is the name credited with Bitcoin’s creation; the identity behind that name is not verified by the evidence discussed here**. That is the boundary between what the attribution supports and what remains unknown in this account.

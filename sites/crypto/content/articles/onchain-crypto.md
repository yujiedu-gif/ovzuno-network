In DAO governance, **onchain crypto** means activity recorded or processed through a blockchain. It identifies where an action is handled or recorded—not a particular governance mechanism, and not proof that a decision was legitimate, secure, or understood by everyone involved.

## What onchain crypto means in DAO governance

“Onchain” is a broad descriptive term. It refers to activity that takes place through a blockchain and may leave a record in its shared ledger. It does not, by itself, name a voting method or specify how a project makes decisions.

A blockchain can enable a community to record transactions in a shared ledger whose published records cannot simply be changed under normal operation. This makes the ledger evidence of what was recorded, within the limits of that system. It does not establish that the record is a complete account of the project’s governance or the reasons behind an action. [source](<https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ir/8202/final>)

Keep three questions separate when reading a claim about an onchain decision: Was an action recorded on a blockchain? What does the record actually say? What does that establish about the decision? A record may help answer the first two, subject to the system’s design. It does not automatically settle the third.

A tracker, for example, can observe activity associated with configured onchain addresses. But that observation is not, by itself, a complete accounting or governance record. Seeing an action is different from establishing its full context or significance. [source](<https://ethereum.org/dao/>)

The phrase “onchain crypto” should not be read as a claim that every blockchain, application, or governance process works the same way. The term points to a relationship with blockchain records or processing; the specific meaning of a particular action depends on the system involved.

## How onchain decisions differ from offchain governance

Offchain activity is discussion or coordination that occurs outside the blockchain record. Participants may discuss an idea, develop a proposal, or coordinate a response elsewhere. A later action may then be submitted to a blockchain. The resulting record can show what the relevant system recorded, but it does not necessarily include the discussion that preceded it.

This distinction matters because deliberation and implementation are not the same event. A proposal may be discussed outside the chain; a subsequent transaction or contract interaction may record an action onchain. Those events have different evidence: the discussion’s context is outside the blockchain record, while the later action may be reflected in that record.

A practical reading sequence is:

1. **Identify the record.** Determine which blockchain record or contract interaction is being discussed.
2. **Separate it from the deliberation.** Locate the relevant proposal or discussion context outside the record when it is needed to understand the claim.
3. **Limit the conclusion.** State what the record shows without treating it as a complete history of how a decision was reached.

This sequence prevents three separate things—offchain discussion, an onchain recorded action, and an interpretation of that action—from being collapsed into a single claim. It also helps identify what evidence would be needed to support a broader statement about how a decision was made.

## What an onchain governance record can show

An onchain record can be evidence that specified activity was recorded in the relevant ledger. Exactly what it shows depends on what that system records. Interpret it in relation to the particular blockchain, contract, or application involved, rather than treating it as a universal account of DAO governance.

A visible record is not enough, on its own, to establish a participant’s identity, intent, authority, or the legal effect of an action. These are different questions from whether a blockchain contains a record. A transaction may be observable without revealing all the context needed to interpret it.

For example, a record associated with an address does not by itself tell a reader all the reasons an action occurred or what participants understood it to mean. The record and the surrounding explanation should be treated as distinct evidence. If a claim depends on a particular DAO’s procedures or interpretation of an action, verify it against reliable documentation and the records being described. Do not assume that a general description of onchain activity establishes the details of a specific implementation.

A careful account therefore uses bounded wording: “the record shows” or “the transaction records” should refer to what can be observed in the relevant system. A broader statement about decision-making needs supporting information beyond the fact that a record exists. This distinction keeps a technical observation from being presented as a complete governance conclusion.

## Limits and risks of interpreting onchain activity

A blockchain record or automated action is not, by itself, proof that a governance process is secure, fair, or compliant. Visibility concerns what is recorded; it does not answer every question about the design, authority, or operation of the system.

Keep claims tied to evidence about the particular system and circumstances. Statements about security, recovery, fees, yields, availability, or operational outcomes require evidence supporting those details; the general meaning of “onchain” does not establish them. Similarly, legal, tax, or regulatory treatment should not be generalized across jurisdictions. This explanation does not determine those matters.

The useful distinction is narrow: **onchain** identifies activity recorded or processed through a blockchain, while **offchain** identifies relevant discussion or coordination outside that record. An onchain record is evidence of what the relevant system recorded—not, on its own, a complete explanation of the decision, its context, or its significance.
